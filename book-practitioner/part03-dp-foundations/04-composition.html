22  Advanced and optimal composition

22.1 What it is

A dashboard refreshes a hundred counts, and a training run takes thousands of noisy steps, yet you report one differential privacy (DP) guarantee for the whole run. Each step has its own privacy level \(\varepsilon\) and slack \(\delta\) (The definition of differential privacy). Basic composition simply adds them, so a run of \(k\) steps costs \(k\varepsilon\) and \(k\delta\) (Post-processing, group privacy and composition). That is correct but pessimistic. The privacy losses of the steps add up, but each depends on the noise drawn and is often negative. So they partly cancel, and their sum almost never reaches its maximum \(k\varepsilon\).

Advanced composition (Dwork, Rothblum & Vadhan 2010) uses this. You pick an extra slack \(\delta'\) for the whole run. The steps are then together \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}, k\delta + \delta')\)-DP, with \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}} = \varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2). \tag{22.1}\] The first term is the spread of the total loss, which grows only like \(\sqrt k\). The second is its average, the drift.

Optimal composition (Kairouz, Oh & Viswanath 2015) is the exact best bound for steps that are known only by their \((\varepsilon, \delta)\). For example, take a hundred counts at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) each, with no slack, on a dataset of \(n = 10{,}000\) people. Basic composition gives a total of 10, still with no slack. The other two rules spend a slack \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\), the book’s default of \(1/n^{1.1}\) rounded down to a power of ten. At that slack, advanced composition gives 5.30 and optimal composition 4.31.

22.2 When you meet it

You meet these rules in papers, in privacy reviews and in any library that quotes a total \(\varepsilon\) for many steps. They change which number you plan with and which one you report (Table 22.1).

“Optimal” means optimal for steps described by one pair each. A real mechanism has a whole privacy curve, one \(\delta\) for every \(\varepsilon\), and a single pair forgets the rest of it. Take a thousand Gaussian steps with noise standard deviation 20 on a query of sensitivity 1. Composed as curves, they are exactly \((7.51, 10^{-5})\)-DP. Summarizing each step by its best single pair and composing optimally gives 62.7, about eight times worse. So whenever you know the mechanism, compose its curve with an accountant (Privacy accounting).

22.3 How to use it

Composition does two jobs. The first is planning, where you split a total budget \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}\) over \(k\) steps. Run advanced composition backwards. When \(\varepsilon\sqrt k\) is small the drift is negligible, so each step can have about \[\varepsilon \approx \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}}{\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}}. \tag{22.2}\] Once \(\varepsilon\sqrt k\) nears 1, the drift takes over and this shortcut gives each step too much.

The second job is reporting. When you know the mechanism, report the accountant’s number at the run’s total \(\delta\), and otherwise report optimal composition. This snippet plans the hundred counts within a total of \((1, 10^{-5})\), giving the whole slack to \(\delta'\). It then reports the thousand Gaussian steps with the privacy loss distribution (PLD) and Rényi differential privacy (RDP) accountants of Google’s dp_accounting.

import math

import dp_accounting

print(f"{1.0 / math.sqrt(2 * 100 * math.log(1e5)):.4f}")  # 0.0208
step = dp_accounting.GaussianDpEvent(noise_multiplier=20.0)
run = dp_accounting.SelfComposedDpEvent(step, 1000)
pld = dp_accounting.pld.PLDAccountant()
pld.compose(run)
print(f"{pld.get_epsilon(target_delta=1e-5):.2f}")  # 7.51
rdp = dp_accounting.rdp.RdpAccountant()
rdp.compose(run)
print(f"{rdp.get_epsilon(target_delta=1e-5):.2f}")  # 8.08

Each count gets 0.0208, against basic composition’s 0.01. The PLD accountant matches the exact 7.51 quoted above, and the RDP accountant gives a valid but looser 8.08 (Choosing an accountant).

22.4 What goes wrong

For a few steps, advanced composition can lose to basic composition, so always take the smaller of the two. Every rule also adds up the steps’ slacks, so count each \(\delta\). When the steps have different privacy levels, optimal composition is computationally hard (Murtagh & Vadhan 2016), which is one more reason to use an accountant.

All these rules still hold when each query depends on earlier answers, provided each step’s budget is fixed in advance. Choosing the budgets from the answers needs a privacy filter (Adaptive composition). Stopping early is different. A stopping rule that reads only the noisy answers is post-processing, so the run costs no more than its planned length. But a run that stops when the true count of some group crosses a threshold reveals that count through its length. Every answer can then be \(\varepsilon\)-DP while the whole run is not DP for any finite \(\varepsilon\).

For a reviewer, a total below optimal composition is a red flag unless an accountant computed it from the mechanism’s curve.
