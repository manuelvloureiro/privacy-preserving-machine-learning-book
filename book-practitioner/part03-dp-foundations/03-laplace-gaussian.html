15  The Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms

Author

Manuel V. Loureiro

Published

Version 2026-10-30

15.1 Why this matters

Suppose that a hospital wants to publish, with a differential privacy (DP) guarantee, how many of its patients have diabetes. Almost every such release, and every noisy gradient of private training, uses one of the two mechanisms of this chapter. Both calculate the exact answer of a query \(f\) on the dataset \(D\), add fresh random noise, written \(Z\), and publish \[M(D) = f(D) + Z. \tag{15.1}\] Three decisions fix such a mechanism. The noise must hide the sensitivity, which is how far one record can move the answer. The noise distribution sets the kind of guarantee, because Laplace noise gives pure DP and Gaussian noise needs a small slack \(\delta\). And a calibration formula sets the amount of noise for a target \(\varepsilon\), through the privacy loss of the definition of differential privacy.

15.2 Sensitivity

The noise must cover the largest change that one record can make to the answer. A query often gives a vector of \(d\) numbers, such as the bins of a histogram, so the change needs a size. Laplace noise uses the \(\ell_1\) norm, the sum of the absolute changes. Gaussian noise uses the \(\ell_2\) norm, the square root of the sum of the squared changes. For a pair of neighbours \(D \sim D'\), the \(\ell_p\) sensitivity of the query is the largest change between their answers, \[\Delta_pf = \max_{D \sim D'}\big\|f(D) - f(D')\big\|_p, \qquad p \in \{1, 2\}. \tag{15.2}\] The two are equal when one record moves a single coordinate. When it moves all \(d\) of them, \(\Delta_1f\) can be \(\sqrt d\) times \(\Delta_2f\).

The sensitivity also depends on the neighbouring relation. Under add/remove, one dataset has one record more than the other. Under replace, one record changes into another, so the size \(n\) is public. With \(B\) for the largest value of a record after clipping, Table 15.1 gives the standard queries.

A replaced record leaves one histogram bin and goes into another, so it changes two bins. The threshold counts, the numbers of records at or above each of \(d\) values, are a dense query, in which one record can move every coordinate.

Calculate the sensitivity from the domain, before you look at the data. If you take it from the data, for example as the largest value, the noise leaks that value (Beyond global sensitivity). Unbounded data have infinite sensitivity, so first clip every value to a fixed range. With ages clipped to at most 100, the mean age of 1,000 patients has sensitivity \(B/n = 0.1\) under replace.

15.3 The Laplace mechanism

The Laplace mechanism adds independent noise from the Laplace distribution, a two-sided exponential with its peak at 0, to each coordinate. We write \(\mathrm{Lap}(b)\) for this noise with scale \(b\), which sets its typical size. For an \(\varepsilon\)-DP guarantee, the scale is the sensitivity divided by \(\varepsilon\), \[b = \frac{\Delta_1f}{\varepsilon}. \tag{15.3}\]

The error of the mechanism is the noise itself. Each coordinate of the noise is \(b\) from zero on average, and goes past a distance \(t\) with probability \(e^{-t/b}\). Give all \(d\) coordinates together a small failure probability \(\beta\), so that each coordinate gets a share \(\beta/d\). Then the width that holds for all of them is \[\Pr\Big[\max_i|Z_i| > b\ln\frac d\beta\Big] \le \beta. \tag{15.4}\] Publish this width with the release. In reverse, the width gives an error budget, because for a single number you can fix a target width \(t\) and solve for the privacy level, \[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta_1f\,\ln(1/\beta)}{t}. \tag{15.5}\] So a count that must be within 10 of the truth with probability 0.95 needs \(\varepsilon = \ln(20)/10 = 0.2996\). For \(d\) numbers, solve each with the failure probability \(\beta/d\), and add their values of \(\varepsilon\) by composition.

At the hospital, 1,234 of the 10,000 patients have diabetes. A count has sensitivity 1 under add/remove, so at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) the noise is \(\mathrm{Lap}(10)\). Its 95% error bar is \(10\ln 20 = 29.96\), or 2.43% of the count.

The scale does not depend on the size of the data, so the relative error of a count of size \(n\) is about \(1/(n\varepsilon)\). But the sensitivity must include every number that one record can move. If one record can change all of 10 released counts, noise for one count makes the release \(10\varepsilon\)-DP, not \(\varepsilon\)-DP.

15.4 The Gaussian mechanism

The Gaussian mechanism adds independent normal noise with mean 0 and standard deviation \(\sigma\) to each coordinate. Its privacy loss has no upper limit in the far tail, so it always needs a \(\delta\). But its noise follows the \(\ell_2\) sensitivity, which can be much smaller than the \(\ell_1\) one. Only the ratio of the sensitivity to the noise is important, so write \(\mu = \Delta_2f/\sigma\) for the number of noise standard deviations between the answers on two neighbours. Write \(\Phi\) for the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution. Then the mechanism is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP exactly when \(\delta\) is at least \[\delta_\mu(\varepsilon) = \Phi\Big(\frac\mu2 - \frac\varepsilon\mu\Big) - e^\varepsilon\,\Phi\Big(-\frac\mu2 - \frac\varepsilon\mu\Big). \tag{15.6}\]

You calibrate with this formula. As \(\mu\) increases, the exact \(\delta\) increases from 0 to 1, so exactly one value \(\mu^*\) gives your target. The smallest noise that works is then \[\sigma^* = \frac{\Delta_2f}{\mu^*}, \qquad \text{where } \delta_{\mu^*}(\varepsilon) = \delta, \tag{15.7}\] and libraries solve for \(\mu^*\) (Section 15.7). For 10,000 patients, the book’s default slack is \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). At \(\varepsilon = 1\), this analytic calibration then gives \(\sigma^* = 3.7306\,\Delta_2f\).

Many papers and libraries still use the classic calibration of Dwork and Roth (2014), which has a proof only for \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\) between 0 and 1, \[\sigma = \frac{\Delta_2f\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}. \tag{15.8}\] At \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\delta = 10^{-5}\), it gives \(4.8448\,\Delta_2f\), 30% more noise than the analytic \(3.7306\,\Delta_2f\). Above \(\varepsilon = 8.42\), it gives too little noise for the guarantee it claims. So use the analytic calibration.

A Gaussian mechanism has a whole curve of guarantees, one \(\delta\) for each \(\varepsilon\), and the accountants of Part 4 compose whole curves. So report \(\sigma/\Delta_2f\) with every Gaussian release, together with the pair that you chose.

At the hospital, Gaussian noise for the diabetes count at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) gives a 95% error bar of 60.27, which is 1.96 standard deviations. Laplace noise gave 29.96 with no \(\delta\), so for a single count Laplace noise is better in error and in guarantee.

15.5 Laplace or Gaussian?

The choice depends on how one record spreads its change across the coordinates. To see why, compare the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of each released number at the same \(\varepsilon\). Laplace noise of scale \(b = \Delta_1f/\varepsilon\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt2\,b\). With \(\sigma_1\) for the analytic Gaussian noise level at unit \(\ell_2\) sensitivity, the errors per coordinate are \[\text{Laplace: } \frac{\sqrt2\,\Delta_1f}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \text{Gaussian: } \Delta_2f\,\sigma_1. \tag{15.9}\]

In a sparse query, such as a histogram under add/remove, one record moves one coordinate, so the two sensitivities are equal. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) and the default \(\delta\), \(\sigma_1 = 3.7306\). So Laplace noise is better at every \(d\), because \(\sqrt2 = 1.4142\) is smaller. In a dense query, such as the threshold counts, one record moves every coordinate. Then the Laplace error grows like \(d\) and the Gaussian error only like \(\sqrt d\). So Laplace noise is more accurate only while \[d < \frac{(\varepsilon\sigma_1)^2}2, \tag{15.10}\] so with \(\sigma_1 = 3.7306\), Gaussian noise is better for 7 or more threshold counts.

Gaussian noise is also better for many releases, because several Gaussian releases compose exactly into one Gaussian mechanism. Private training has dense queries and many releases, because one record’s gradient moves every coordinate of a model at every step. So it clips the \(\ell_2\) norm of each gradient and calibrates Gaussian noise to that clip.

15.6 Calibrating a release

Every noisy release has five steps.

  1. Fix the neighbouring relation and the unit of privacy, usually one person under add/remove.
  2. Clip every value to a range fixed in advance. Then take the sensitivity from Table 15.1, or calculate it from the worst record.
  3. Use Laplace noise for one number or a sparse vector, and Gaussian noise for a dense vector.
  4. Set the scale with Equation 15.3 or Equation 15.6. Charge it to your budget (Post-processing, group privacy and composition).
  5. Draw the noise with a library that experts have checked. Publish the error bar and the noise parameters with the result.

A mean under add/remove has no simple sensitivity, because its denominator also moves. So release a noisy sum and a noisy count, each with half of the budget. Then divide the sum by the count, which is post-processing and costs nothing. For the error bar of the mean, take the bar of each at 97.5%, so that both hold together 95% of the time. Then the mean is above the sum’s lower end divided by the count’s upper end. It is below the sum’s upper end divided by the count’s lower end, if both lower ends are positive.

OpenDP’s .dp.mean makes the same split, and summarize(alpha=0.025) gives the two bars at 97.5%. When you do not know the range for the clip, Private mean estimation finds it privately. Use plain numpy.random.laplace only for prototypes, because textbook floating-point samplers can leak through the low-order bits of their output (Discrete and floating-point-safe mechanisms). For a whole release with clipping, grouping and one budget, follow Release statistics with a library.

15.7 Code

The snippet checks the hospital’s numbers and the analytic \(\sigma^*\) at \(\varepsilon = 1\). In OpenDP, a mechanism’s map takes the sensitivity and gives the \(\varepsilon\) that it costs, and the accuracy helpers change a scale into a 95% error bar or back. For a count, with sensitivity 1, one over that scale is the \(\varepsilon\) of the error budget. IBM’s diffprivlib calibrates the analytic Gaussian.

import math

import opendp.prelude as dp
from diffprivlib.mechanisms import GaussianAnalytic

dp.enable_features("contrib")
laplace = dp.m.make_laplace(dp.atom_domain(T=float, nan=False), dp.absolute_distance(T=float), scale=10.0)
print(laplace.map(1.0))  # 0.1
print(f"{dp.laplacian_scale_to_accuracy(10.0, alpha=0.05):.2f}")  # 29.96
print(f"{1 / dp.accuracy_to_laplacian_scale(10.0, alpha=0.05):.4f}")  # 0.2996
gauss = GaussianAnalytic(epsilon=1.0, delta=1e-5, sensitivity=1.0)
print(f"{math.sqrt(gauss.variance(0.0)):.4f}")  # 3.7306

To add the noise, call the mechanism on the value, as in laplace(1234.0).

15.8 Cheat sheet

Rules of thumb

  • Calibrate Laplace noise to \(\Delta_1f\) and Gaussian noise to \(\Delta_2f\), never the other way round.

  • When \(n\) is not public, never use \(B/n\) as the sensitivity of a mean.
