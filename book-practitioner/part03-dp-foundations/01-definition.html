19  The definition of differential privacy

19.1 Why this matters

Every attack in Threat models and attacks runs an algorithm on one of two datasets that differ in one person, and guesses from the output which dataset it saw. Differential privacy (DP) makes the two outputs look alike against every test, including tests that nobody has invented yet. It is a property of the algorithm, not of the data, so you can check it before anyone touches the data.

19.2 Neighbouring datasets

The first question about a claim is what “differ in one record” means, and the neighbouring relation answers it. Under add/remove, two datasets are neighbours when one has a record that the other lacks, so the guarantee hides whether a person is in the data at all. Under replace, they have the same size and differ in the value of one record, so the guarantee hides only what the record says. A replacement is a removal and then an addition, and each step costs the privacy level \(\varepsilon\) of Section 19.3, so \[\varepsilon\text{-DP under add/remove} \implies 2\varepsilon\text{-DP under replace}. \tag{19.1}\] The reverse fails, because replace never compares datasets of different sizes.

You also choose the unit of privacy, what one record is. At the example level, it is one training record or one event, and at the user level, all the data of one person. A user who wrote 200 messages is then a group of 200 records under example-level DP, with protection only at \(200\varepsilon\) (Post-processing, group privacy and composition). So check that the claim protects the person. The guarantee must also hide every record that the data format allows, even a person aged 900, who could move an average a long way. So private algorithms clip each input to a fixed range before they add noise.

19.3 The definition

A mechanism \(M\) is a randomized algorithm that takes a dataset and returns an output. Write \(D\) for a dataset and \(D'\) for one of its neighbours. The definition asks that no set of outputs becomes more than a factor \(e^\varepsilon\) more likely when one record changes, up to a small additive slack \(\delta\). Here \(\varepsilon\) is not negative. A mechanism is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-differentially private if every pair of neighbours and every set \(S\) of outputs satisfy \[\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^\varepsilon\,\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta. \tag{19.2}\] The probabilities come only from the randomness of the mechanism, and the data stay fixed.

When \(\delta = 0\), the guarantee is pure DP, and otherwise it is approximate DP. In both cases, a private mechanism must be random. A deterministic one that answers differently on two neighbours gives that answer probability 1 on one and 0 on the other, which no factor \(e^\varepsilon\) covers. So rounding, aggregation or the removal of small cells gives no guarantee by itself.

19.4 The privacy loss and δ

For an output \(y\), write \(p_{M(D)}(y)\) for its probability, or its density, when the mechanism runs on \(D\). The privacy loss of \(y\) is the logarithm of how much more likely the output is on \(D\) than on \(D'\), \[L(y) = \ln\frac{p_{M(D)}(y)}{p_{M(D')}(y)}. \tag{19.3}\] Independent outputs multiply their likelihood ratios, so their losses add, and that is why the accountants of Part 4 work with the loss. Pure \(\varepsilon\)-DP holds exactly when every output has a loss between \(-\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon\). Laplace noise meets that condition, but the loss of Gaussian noise has no limit in the far tail (The Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms).

Under approximate DP, \(\delta\) pays for the outputs whose loss is more than \(\varepsilon\). It counts only the part of each one’s probability on \(D\) above \(e^\varepsilon\) times its probability on \(D'\). So \(\delta\) is not the chance that the loss is more than \(\varepsilon\), and the total is the hockey-stick divergence (Divergences). Each \(\varepsilon\) has its own smallest \(\delta\), and the function \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) is the privacy curve of the mechanism. The noise parameters fix the whole curve, so report them with the pair, and others can recompute it (Numerical accounting).

19.5 Randomized response

In randomized response, a survey design, each person tells the truth with some probability \(t\) and lies otherwise, so a single answer proves nothing. A person who tosses a coin, answers truthfully on heads and answers by a second toss on tails tells the truth with probability \(\frac34\). So the true answer is three times as likely as the false one, which gives pure DP at \(\varepsilon = \ln 3\). In general, randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\) tells the truth with probability \[t = \frac{e^\varepsilon}{1 + e^\varepsilon}. \tag{19.4}\]

With independent coins, the guarantee is \(\varepsilon\) per person for any number of people. But it holds only under replace, because under add/remove the number of reports shows who answered. Write \(\bar y\) for the share of “yes” reports among the \(n\) people. A correction with \(t\) for the lies gives the estimate of the true fraction \(\pi\) and its standard deviation, \[\hat\pi = \frac{\bar y - (1 - t)}{2t - 1}, \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\hat\pi) = \frac{e^{\varepsilon/2}}{(e^\varepsilon - 1)\sqrt n}. \tag{19.5}\] For 1,000 people, the two-coin scheme has a standard deviation of 0.0274. When the true fraction is 0.3, that is about twice the sampling error of 0.0145 that the survey already has. This doubling is the price of the local model, in which nobody sees a true answer (Local, shuffle and distributed trust models).

OpenDP builds the mechanism from its truth probability, 0.75 for the two coins, and map turns one changed answer into its \(\varepsilon\), \(\ln 3\). The contrib feature turns on mechanisms that are not yet formally vetted.

import opendp.prelude as dp

dp.enable_features("contrib")
rr = dp.m.make_randomized_response_bool(prob=0.75)
print(f"{rr.map(1):.4f}")  # 1.0986

19.6 What ε means for an attacker

A membership attacker looks at the output and decides whether to accuse the target of being in the data (Hypothesis testing). Its false positive rate (FPR) is how often it accuses a target who is out. Its true positive rate (TPR) is how often it accuses one who is in. The outputs on which it accuses form a set, which the definition bounds, so under add/remove every attack satisfies \[\mathrm{TPR} \le e^\varepsilon\,\mathrm{FPR} + \delta. \tag{19.6}\]

Use this form, because Part 2 judged attacks by their TPR at a low FPR. Take \(\varepsilon = 1\) and the \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) that Section 19.7 gives for 10,000 people. At an FPR of \(10^{-3}\), no attack gets a TPR above \(e \cdot 10^{-3} + 10^{-5} = 0.002728\). As the FPR goes to 0, so does the first term, and the guarantee then depends almost entirely on \(\delta\).

Read backwards, the cap turns a measured attack into a lower bound on \(\varepsilon\), \[\varepsilon \ge \ln\frac{\mathrm{TPR} - \delta}{\mathrm{FPR}}. \tag{19.7}\] Suppose that a model is claimed to be \((2, 10^{-5})\)-DP, and an attack gets a TPR of 0.05 at an FPR of 0.001. If those rates were exact, \(\varepsilon\) would be at least about \(\ln 50 = 3.91\), so the claim would be false. A real audit puts confidence bounds on the rates and plants a canary, a record that is as easy to detect as possible (Auditing).

Two summaries are easier to explain to a lawyer. The first, the membership advantage, is the TPR minus the FPR, how much better than a coin flip an attacker does. Under \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, it is at most \[\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR} \le \frac{e^\varepsilon - 1 + 2\delta}{e^\varepsilon + 1}. \tag{19.8}\]

The second, the posterior bound, is about beliefs. Under pure DP, one output can multiply the odds of an adversary between two neighbours by at most \(e^\varepsilon\). So a prior belief \(p\) that the data are \(D\) can rise at most to \[\Pr[D \mid y] \le \frac{e^\varepsilon p}{e^\varepsilon p + 1 - p}. \tag{19.9}\] Table 19.1 turns a claimed \(\varepsilon\) into both numbers.

So an \(\varepsilon\) of at most 1 is strong for any mechanism, because a 1% prior rises to less than 3%. Between 1 and 10, a claim needs the privacy curve of the mechanism as its justification. Above 10, where the bounds promise almost nothing, it needs an audit as well (Choosing ε). For statistics, start from the error that you can accept and solve for \(\varepsilon\) (The Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms). The table is for pure DP, so for an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) claim, quote the advantage bound and the cap on the TPR, which both include \(\delta\).

19.7 How small δ must be

A small \(\delta\) almost does not change the advantage bound. But the advantage is an average, so it can hide total harm to a few people. Take a mechanism that publishes each record in full, independently with probability \(\delta\). It is \((0, \delta)\)-DP under add/remove, because a record changes the output only when its own coin publishes it. But over \(n\) records, it publishes at least one with probability \[1 - (1 - \delta)^n \approx 1 - e^{-n\delta}. \tag{19.10}\] With 10,000 records and \(\delta = 1/n\), that is \(1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321\).

So treat \(\delta\) as a budget per person for catastrophe, such as these total failures. The default of this book follows Ponomareva et al. (2023) and keeps \(n\delta\), the expected number of people exposed this way, well below 1. Let \(n\) count people, not records, and choose \[\delta \le \frac{1}{n^{1.1}}, \tag{19.11}\] rounded down to a power of ten. For 10,000 people, the rule gives \(10^{-5}\). The publishing mechanism then has \(n\delta = 0.1\), and it exposes someone with probability only 0.0952.

19.8 What differential privacy does not protect

DP compares a world with your record and a world without it, so the guarantee does not cover what someone can learn in both worlds. A study that never used Alice’s record may show that smokers get cancer at a high rate. That changes what anyone believes about Alice, a smoker, yet the study is 0-DP with respect to her. So a harm is a DP violation only if the inference would be materially different without her record. Correlated records are a second gap, because a household of five that always answers alike is a group of five, unless the household is the unit of privacy. The third gap is the implementation (Discrete and floating-point-safe mechanisms).

19.9 Writing the guarantee down

A reviewer can check only what a claim writes down. So a guarantee names the unit of privacy with its neighbouring relation, and \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\) for the whole release, not per query. It also names the mechanism, the library version, the trust model and the data treated as public, which the guarantee does not protect. This example uses the central model, in which a trusted curator holds the raw data and adds the noise:

This release satisfies \((1, 10^{-5})\)-DP for adding or removing one customer and all their invoice lines, at most 100 per customer, under the central model, with Gaussian noise from OpenDP 0.16.0. The market list was fixed in advance.

Release statistics with a library and Release and governance print such statements from the code that made the release.

19.10 Code

This snippet checks two numbers quoted above.

from math import exp, log

print(f"{(exp(3) - 1) / (exp(3) + 1):.4f}")  # 0.9051
print(f"{log((0.05 - 1e-5) / 1e-3):.2f}")  # 3.91

19.11 Cheat sheet

Rules of thumb

  • When people contribute many rows, the unit of privacy is the person.
  • An \(\varepsilon\) is never a probability of re-identification. Under pure DP it bounds how far any output can shift an attacker’s odds.
