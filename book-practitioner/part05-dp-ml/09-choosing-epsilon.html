46  Choosing ε

46.1 Why this matters

Every model trained with differential privacy (DP) ships with a privacy budget \(\varepsilon\). The chapter on private stochastic gradient descent (SGD), DP-SGD, showed what each budget costs in accuracy. This chapter adds what it means for the people in the data, so that \(\varepsilon\) is not chosen by habit (“everyone uses 8”).

An attacker who wants to know whether a record was used for training runs a membership test. The test catches real members, its true positives, and wrongly flags non-members, its false positives. The budget \(\varepsilon\) and its small slack \(\delta\) cap the first rate in terms of the second (The definition of differential privacy). But each training run also has its own curve of the best rates an attacker can reach, usually far below the cap. So compute that curve, read it at the false positive rate of your threat, and set it beside the accuracy each budget buys.

46.2 Justifying a choice

A defensible choice says what the budget protects before it picks the number.

  1. Unit and period. Say whether one unit is a record, a user or a device, and whether neighbouring datasets differ by adding or removing one unit or by replacing one. Say which runs the budget covers, since every tuning run spends budget too (Hyperparameter tuning).
  2. Threat. Say whether the concern is membership, reconstruction or attribute inference, and what the attacker knows and sees. This sets the false positive rate at which you read the curve, a low one for an attacker who must rarely accuse a non-member.
  3. Whole guarantee. Choose \(\delta\) by the book’s rule (Section 46.5), then compute the trade-off curve (Section 46.3) and read it there.
  4. Evidence. The curve is a theorem about an algorithm, not about your code, so run an audit that could refute it (Auditing private training).
  5. Utility. Put accuracy against \(\varepsilon\) next to the risk, and pick the smallest budget past the bend, where accuracy stops improving. Draw it on public or proxy data, because private runs that choose \(\varepsilon\) are a search whose cost the reported \(\varepsilon\) leaves out.
  6. Comparison. Only now compare with deployments (Section 46.6) and with the tiers in the rules of thumb.

Adult, a public census survey, shows step 5 on a real run. Train a model with DP-SGD trains a small network on 30,000 of its people at five budgets, each with \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). Table 46.1 sets their test accuracies beside a risk measure from Section 46.3. It is the largest true positive rate (TPR) that any membership test can reach at a false positive rate (FPR) of \(10^{-3}\).

Accuracy rises by five points from \(\varepsilon = 0.25\) to 1, then stays within a few thousandths of the non-private model, which is noise on one seed. The risk grows faster with each doubling of the budget, from 0.0023 at \(\varepsilon = 1\) to 0.0445 at \(\varepsilon = 8\). So on Adult the bend sits at \(\varepsilon = 1\), and a budget of 1 or 2 is the defensible choice. The script scored these runs on private rows, so the table only illustrates step 5. A real choice reads it from a public stand-in, and on a harder task, a larger model or a smaller dataset, expect the bend further right.

46.3 The pair and the whole curve

A membership test says “member” or “non-member” about one target record. Its TPR is the chance that it says “member” when the target was in the training set, and its FPR the chance when it was not. Every test against an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP mechanism satisfies \[\mathrm{TPR} \le \min\big\{1,\ e^\varepsilon\,\mathrm{FPR} + \delta,\ 1 - e^{-\varepsilon}(1 - \delta - \mathrm{FPR})\big\}. \tag{46.1}\] The middle term is the one that binds at low FPR, and the last applies the same cap to the members a test misses. Attacks are judged at a low FPR, because an attack that rarely flags a non-member identifies people with confidence (Membership inference). Yet at \(\varepsilon = 8\) and an FPR of \(10^{-3}\), the pair caps the TPR at 0.9997, which constrains almost nothing.

A real training run satisfies DP for a whole range of pairs, one \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) for each \(\varepsilon\), because its accountant can trade \(\varepsilon\) for \(\delta\). Each pair gives a line, the middle term of Equation 46.1, and every test must stay under all of these lines at once. The best test reaches the lowest line exactly, which is called the trade-off curve: \[\mathrm{TPR}^*(\mathrm{FPR}) = \min_{\varepsilon}\big(e^\varepsilon\,\mathrm{FPR} + \delta(\varepsilon)\big). \tag{46.2}\] For DP-SGD, a privacy loss distribution (PLD) accountant computes this curve. It can round the privacy loss up or down, and the two curves it gets bracket the true one (Numerical accounting).

Table 46.2 compares the pair with the curve for this part’s running example from DP-SGD, a synthetic run sampled at rate 0.01 for 10,000 steps.

So a budget of 8 means little as a bare pair, while its curve caps the TPR at 0.0589 at an FPR of \(10^{-3}\).

To compute the curve with Google’s dp_accounting, build the run’s PLD, ask it for \(\delta\) on a grid of \(\varepsilon\), and take the lowest line. The snippet’s first print falls inside the bracket of Table 46.2, and its second gives the last column of Table 46.1.

import numpy as np
from dp_accounting.pld import privacy_loss_distribution as pld

EPS_GRID = np.linspace(0.0, 20.0, 2001)


def tpr_at(sigma: float, q: float, steps: int, fpr: float) -> float:
    run = pld.from_gaussian_mechanism(sigma, sampling_prob=q).self_compose(steps)
    deltas = np.array(run.get_delta_for_epsilon(EPS_GRID))
    return float(np.min(np.exp(EPS_GRID) * fpr + deltas))


print(f"{tpr_at(1.36, 0.01, 10_000, 1e-3):.4f}")  # 0.0126
adult = [tpr_at(sigma, 1 / 30, 300, 1e-3) for sigma in (8.125, 2.383, 1.431, 0.973, 0.722)]
print(" ".join(f"{tpr:.4f}" for tpr in adult))  # 0.0013 0.0023 0.0046 0.0123 0.0445

The library’s default PLD rounds the loss up, so its curve is a valid upper bound. A Rényi differential privacy (RDP) accountant gives a valid but looser curve. The Gaussian differential privacy (GDP) approximation is quick but not a bound, so use it to explain and the PLD to certify.

The curve is a worst case, for an attacker who sees every intermediate model, so a typical record attacked through the final model alone is much safer. The curve bounds reconstruction too, since a guess among \(m\) equally likely records is a membership test at an FPR of \(1/m\) (Reconstruction attacks).

46.4 What an attacker can believe

Most people ask how sure an attacker can become that a person was in the data. Write \(p\) for the attacker’s prior, its probability of “member” before it sees the model. A privacy loss of at most \(\varepsilon\) means that no output is more than \(e^\varepsilon\) times as likely with the person’s record as without it. So the attacker’s belief afterwards is at most \[\Pr[\text{member} \mid \text{output}] \le \frac{p\,e^\varepsilon}{p\,e^\varepsilon + 1 - p}. \tag{46.3}\] So DP bounds the change of belief, not the belief itself.

For example, an attacker who starts at 1% can end at 35.55% when \(\varepsilon = 4\). That is for the worst output, and in DP-SGD the privacy loss of the released model is usually far below \(\varepsilon\).

Base rates matter as much as the loss. An attacker who accuses every person its test flags as a member is right with probability \[\Pr[\text{member} \mid \text{flag}] = \frac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR} + (1 - p)\,\mathrm{FPR}}. \tag{46.4}\] Take the running example’s \(\varepsilon = 8\) run and an attacker with a 1% prior who flags at an FPR of \(10^{-2}\). The curve caps its TPR there at 0.2092 (Table 46.2), so at most 0.1744 of its flags are right, and five out of six are false. An insider with a prior of 0.5 would be right far more often, so a statement about beliefs must name the attacker.

46.5 How small δ must be

For \(n\) privacy units, the book’s rule sets \(\delta \le 1/n^{1.1}\), rounded down to a power of ten (How small δ must be). The units are people when one person has many records (User-level differential privacy). The rule gives \(10^{-5}\) for both of this page’s runs. When \(n\) is just above a power of ten, as with Adult’s 30,000 people, that \(\delta\) is not far below \(1/n\). NIST SP 800-226, the US guidelines for evaluating DP claims, calls a \(\delta\) above \(10^{-5}\) suspicious unless justified.

For DP-SGD a smaller \(\delta\) is cheap. The RDP accountant that set the running example’s \(\varepsilon = 4\) run puts it at only 5.209 when \(\delta\) falls to \(10^{-8}\). So report \(\varepsilon\) at a second, smaller \(\delta\) too, and never quote a \(\delta\) as large as one over the number of units.

46.6 Comparing with deployed values

“Industry uses \(\varepsilon\) between 1 and 10” hides what every \(\varepsilon\) needs, its privacy unit and the period over which it is spent. The Census Bureau’s 2020 redistricting data used zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP), which states the guarantee as one number, written \(\rho\), that converts to \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) pairs. Its unit was one person’s record under the replace-one relation, and persons got \(\rho = 2.56\), reported as \(\varepsilon = 17.14\) at \(\delta = 10^{-10}\).

So before you compare, convert both budgets to the same unit, neighbouring relation and \(\delta\). A zCDP parameter \(\rho\) converts to a pair by \[\varepsilon = \rho + 2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)}. \tag{46.5}\] For the Census figures this formula gives 17.92, and the Bureau’s 17.14 comes from a tighter conversion. Since a replacement is a removal followed by an addition, an add/remove guarantee \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) implies only the replace-one guarantee \((2\varepsilon, (1 + e^\varepsilon)\delta)\) (The definition of differential privacy). And a budget stated per day adds up over time by composition, to 365 a year at \(\varepsilon = 1\) per day.

So it would be wrong to call the running example’s \(\varepsilon = 8\) run “less than half the Census budget”. At the Census’s \(\delta = 10^{-10}\) it is at 11.627, and under the Census’s replace-one relation that can double.

46.7 Cheat sheet

Rules of thumb

  • A budget of at most 1 is strong for any mechanism. A budget between 1 and 10 needs DP-SGD’s curve as justification, and a budget above 10 needs the curve and an audit (Ponomareva et al. 2023).
  • Never quote \(\varepsilon\) without its unit, \(\delta\) and period.
