# Threat models and attacks

This part teaches you to attack your own models and releases before someone else does. You need the attacks even if you plan to use differential privacy, because they are how you check that an implementation keeps its promise. Every attack here is a game, in which a model or a release depends on a hidden secret and an adversary sees what the threat model allows. Its guess counts only by how much it beats a baseline that never looks at the model.

Start with [Threat models and privacy games](01-threat-models.md), and then read [Membership inference](02-membership-inference.md). After those two, pick the chapter whose secret matches your question. For a trained model, membership inference asks whether one record was used, and [Attribute inference](03-attribute-inference.md) seeks a person's sensitive field. [Property inference](04-property-inference.md) seeks a fact about the whole training population, and [Memorization and extraction](05-memorization-extraction.md) seeks text the model memorized. In federated learning, [Gradient inversion](06-gradient-inversion.md) rebuilds a client's input from its updates.

For released data, read the last three chapters in order, because together they tell one story about releasing less. Records without names still fall to linkage ([Linkage and re-identification](07-linkage-reidentification.md)). Records hidden in crowds still give away sensitive values ([Syntactic anonymity](08-syntactic-anonymity.md)). And accurate aggregates alone can give away the whole dataset ([Reconstruction attacks](09-reconstruction.md)). This is the Fundamental Law of Information Recovery: answering too many statistics too accurately reveals the data. So "we removed the names" and "we only release aggregates" are not anonymization, and the next part starts from [differential privacy](../part03-dp-foundations/00-introduction.md) instead.

Two lessons drive every audit in this part. First, report the true positive rate at a low false positive rate, such as one in a thousand, not accuracy or the area under the curve. Second, the worst-off records leak first, such as outliers, duplicates and people with a unique combination of attributes, so an average over all records misleads. A failed attack proves little, because an attack only gives a lower bound on the leak.

At the end of this part you will be able to write a threat model, run a membership audit you can defend, test a table for unique records, and decide whether a dataset called anonymized really is. The full edition's scripts in `code/part02-attacks/` reproduce every number at toy scale.
