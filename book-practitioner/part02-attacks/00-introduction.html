Threat models and attacks

This part teaches you to attack your own models and releases before someone else does. You need the attacks even if you plan to use differential privacy, because they check that an implementation keeps its promise. Every attack here is a game, in which an adversary guesses a secret hidden in a model or a release. A guess counts only by how much it beats a baseline that never looks at the model.

Read Threat models and privacy games first and Membership inference second, then the chapter whose secret matches your question. For a trained model, Memorization and extraction looks for text the model memorized. In federated learning, Gradient inversion rebuilds a client’s input from its updates. Two short reference pages cover rarer attacks: attribute inference looks for a person’s sensitive field, and property inference for a fact about the whole training population.

For released data, read the last three chapters in order, because they tell one story. Records without names still fall to linkage. Records hidden in crowds still give away sensitive values. And accurate aggregates alone can give away the whole dataset (reconstruction attacks). So neither removing names nor releasing only aggregates is anonymization, and the next part starts from differential privacy instead.

Two lessons apply to every audit here. Report the true positive rate at a low false positive rate, such as one in a thousand, not accuracy or the area under the curve. And the worst-off records leak first, such as outliers, duplicates and people with a unique combination of attributes, so an average over all records misleads. A failed attack proves little, because it gives only a lower bound on the leak.

The part ends with a workflow, Pre-release privacy risk assessment. It fixes the blocking thresholds first, then reads the results of this part’s audits against them and writes the decision down. At the end of the part, you can write a threat model and run a membership audit you can defend. You can also test a table for unique records, and decide if a model or a dataset is safe to release.
