# Prerequisites

This part collects the mathematics the rest of the book stands on. It has no privacy in it: it is standard probability, statistics, optimization, information theory and cryptography, chosen because a later part needs it and taken only as far as that need goes. You need the basics of probability (expectation, variance, independence), enough machine learning to recognise a loss function and stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and enough Python to read a short script. If you know a topic, skim its cheat sheet; each later chapter restates in a sentence what it uses and links back here.

## The common thread

Later parts study algorithms whose output is random, and they keep asking the same few questions about them. How far is a random output from its expected value? How different are the outputs on two inputs that differ in one example, and how well could anyone tell them apart? How much does a trained model depend on a single training example? How much does an observation reveal about something hidden, and what can an observer with limited computing power learn?

Two ideas answer most of them. The first is the **likelihood ratio** $p(x)/q(x)$: how many times more likely an observation is under one distribution than under another. It measures what one observation says about which distribution produced it, so telling two distributions apart always comes down to it: divergences summarize it, the best test thresholds it, and when it is bounded no single observation is strong evidence. The second idea is the **effect of one input**: how far one example can move an average, a minimizer or a loss. A quantity that no single input can move much has little room to vary, so small influence turns into narrow error bars, and in learning into a model that generalizes.

## Why the chapters come in this order

The first four chapters build one argument, each on the last. [Probability distributions](01-distributions.md) introduces the few families that model counts and noise, and ends by comparing a noise density with a copy of itself moved sideways: the normal's likelihood ratio grows without bound in the tails, the Laplace's is capped. That raises the question of how often the extreme ratios occur, and [Concentration inequalities](02-concentration.md) answers it by turning what you know about a random quantity into an error bar that fails with a chosen small probability. [Divergences](03-divergences.md) then compresses the difference between two distributions into one number, and shows that each candidate number answers a different question. [Hypothesis testing](04-hypothesis-testing.md) says what those numbers mean for anyone trying to tell the two distributions apart: the best test, the curve of its two errors, and why a detector should be judged where false alarms are rare.

[Information theory](05-information-theory.md) continues the same thread from a different angle, asking how much an observation reveals about a hidden value, on average and for a single best guess. [Optimization](06-optimization.md) and [Algorithmic stability and generalization](07-stability-generalization.md) take up the second idea: how fast training converges, how far its result moves when one example changes, and why a model that depends little on each example generalizes. [Cryptography basics](08-crypto-basics.md) stands somewhat apart. It defines what an efficient observer must not learn, as two worlds an attacker must tell apart, and can wait until a later chapter needs it.

## How to read a chapter

Each chapter opens with its practical question and the idea that answers it, then reasons each result from a problem, with a worked example and the consequences for practice, and closes with a cheat sheet. The derivations and the scripts that check every number are in the full edition, linked at the end.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this part you will be able to put an error bar on a noisy answer, a measured rate or the maximum of many noise draws, and choose the inequality that gives the narrowest valid one. You will be able to compute the Kullback–Leibler (KL), Rényi, total variation and hockey-stick divergences between two normal or two Laplace distributions, and explain why processing data cannot increase any of them. You will know the best test between two distributions, read its trade-off curve, report a detector by its true positive rate at a low false positive rate, and size a test set to measure that rate. You will also be able to say how fast gradient descent converges and how much a trained model moves when one example changes, compute what an observation leaks about a hidden value, and state a security property as a game between an attacker and two worlds.
