1  Probability distributions

1.1 Why this matters

Almost every number in a private machine-learning system is either a count or a draw of noise. The number of mistakes a model makes on a test set is a count. So is the number of examples that land in a randomly sampled minibatch. The noise a system adds to a statistic on purpose is a draw from a distribution that someone chose, usually a normal or a Laplace distribution. Before you can trust any of these numbers, you need to know how it is spread. You need to know where it usually lands, how far it can stray, and how rare the large values are.

A handful of distribution families covers almost every case, and they are not a collection of unrelated formulas. Each one is built from simpler ones. A binomial count is a sum of coin flips. A Poisson count is a binomial with many trials, each unlikely to succeed. An exponential is the time you wait for the first of those rare events. A Laplace distribution is two exponentials placed back to back. A normal distribution is what a sum of many small independent pieces looks like. Because each family is built from simpler pieces, its average and its spread follow from the pieces, and this chapter explains each fact that way.

The chapter ends with a question that later chapters keep asking. Suppose noise is added to one of two values that differ by a known amount, and you see the result. How strongly can that one observation favour one value over the other? With normal noise, some observations settle the question almost for certain. With Laplace noise, no observation can ever favour either value by more than a fixed factor. The difference comes straight from the shapes of the two distributions.

1.2 Mean and variance

The mean of a random quantity \(X\), written \(\mathbb E[X]\), is the long-run average of many independent draws of it. The mean says where a distribution sits, but not how wide it is. The variance measures the width. It takes each draw’s distance from the mean and squares it, so that distances on either side count the same. Then it averages these squares. A second form is often easier to compute. The variance is the mean of the square minus the square of the mean: \[\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb E\big[(X - \mathbb E X)^2\big] = \mathbb E[X^2] - (\mathbb E X)^2. \tag{1.1}\] The standard deviation is the square root of the variance. It is in the same units as \(X\), so it is the number to compare with the mean. For a fair die, the mean is 3.5 and the variance is about 2.92. The standard deviation is about 1.71, so a typical roll lands between one and two pips from the mean.

Three rules cover almost every calculation in this chapter. The first rule says that the mean of a sum is the sum of the means. It holds always, even when the quantities depend on each other, because an average over outcomes splits term by term. The second rule says what scaling does to the spread. Multiplying \(X\) by a constant \(c\) multiplies every distance from the mean by \(c\). Squaring turns that factor into \(c^2\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(cX) = c^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\).

The third rule says that variances add, but only under a condition. For two quantities \(X\) and \(Y\), the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. In general the variance of a sum has an extra term, the covariance, which measures how much \(X\) and \(Y\) tend to move together. Independence makes the covariance zero, and only then do the variances simply add. Without independence the rule fails. If \(Y\) is an exact copy of \(X\), the sum is \(2X\), and by the second rule its variance is four times that of \(X\), not twice.

The second and third rules together say how fast an average becomes precise. Take \(n\) independent draws of \(X\) and average them. Their sum has \(n\) times the variance of one draw, by the third rule. Dividing the sum by \(n\) divides that variance by \(n^2\), by the second rule. So the average has variance \(\operatorname{Var}(X)/n\), and its standard deviation is that of one draw divided by \(\sqrt n\). Four times as much data therefore only halves the error.

1.3 Tails and the moment generating function

The mean and the variance describe the bulk of a distribution, where most draws land. A guarantee that must hold except in rare cases depends instead on the tail. The tail is the chance \(\Pr[X \ge t]\) that the quantity lands at or above a threshold \(t\) far from the mean. What matters is how fast the tail shrinks as the threshold grows, and three shapes recur in this book. Evaluate each of them at \(t = 5\). A polynomial decay such as \(1/t^2\) gives 0.04. An exponential decay such as \(e^{-t}\) gives about \(6.7\times10^{-3}\). A Gaussian decay such as \(e^{-t^2/2}\) gives about \(3.7\times10^{-6}\).

The gap widens quickly as \(t\) grows. The exponent of the Gaussian decay grows like \(t^2\), while that of the exponential decay grows only like \(t\). The exponential and Laplace distributions below have exponential tails. The normal distribution has a Gaussian tail. Which decay a distribution has decides which tail bounds apply to it.

One function connects a distribution to its tail, and also makes sums of independent quantities easy. The moment generating function (MGF) of \(X\) is the average of \(e^{\lambda X}\): \[M_X(\lambda) = \mathbb E\big[e^{\lambda X}\big]. \tag{1.2}\] The number \(\lambda\) is not a parameter of the distribution. It is a dial that you set. When \(\lambda\) is positive, the weight \(e^{\lambda X}\) grows exponentially with \(X\), so large values dominate the average. The larger you set \(\lambda\), the further into the right tail the MGF looks.

A coin shows where the formulas in this chapter come from. Take a quantity that is 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 otherwise. At the outcome 0 the weight is \(e^0 = 1\), and at the outcome 1 it is \(e^\lambda\). The MGF is the average of the two weights, \((1 - p) + pe^\lambda\), which is \(1 + p(e^\lambda - 1)\). The factor \(e^\lambda\) that keeps returning for counts is just the weight of the outcome 1. The function’s name comes from the moments \(\mathbb E[X^k]\), which its derivatives at \(\lambda = 0\) recover. The full edition derives every mean and variance in this chapter that way.

Two properties of the MGF matter in practice. The first is that adding independent quantities multiplies their MGFs: \[M_{X+Y}(\lambda) = M_X(\lambda)\,M_Y(\lambda) \quad\text{when $X$ and $Y$ are independent.} \tag{1.3}\] The exponential of a sum is the product of the exponentials. Independence then lets the average of that product split into the product of the averages. Without independence the average of a product does not split, and the rule fails. A related theorem, which the full edition cites rather than proves, says that two distributions with the same MGF near zero are the same distribution. So when the MGF of a sum matches a known family’s MGF, the sum belongs to that family.

The second property links the MGF to the tail. For every positive \(\lambda\), \[\Pr[X \ge t] \le M_X(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda t}. \tag{1.4}\] The reason takes one step. Whenever \(X\) is at least \(t\), the weight \(e^{\lambda X}\) is at least \(e^{\lambda t}\). The weight is never negative, so the other outcomes cannot pull its average down. The average is therefore at least \(e^{\lambda t}\) times the chance that \(X\) is at least \(t\). If the MGF is finite for some positive \(\lambda\), the tail must therefore shrink at least exponentially. For a heavy-tailed distribution, the MGF is infinite for every positive \(\lambda\), and this bound says nothing. Concentration inequalities turns the bound into usable error bars by choosing \(\lambda\) well.

1.4 Counts: Bernoulli and binomial

The simplest random quantity is a yes-or-no outcome. A test example is misclassified or not, and a record is included in a sample or not. A Bernoulli variable with probability \(p\) is 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 otherwise. Its mean is \(p\). Its square equals itself, so its variance is \(p - p^2\), which is \(p(1-p)\).

Count the yeses over \(n\) independent trials and you get a binomial count, written \(\mathrm{Binomial}(n, p)\). The chance of exactly \(k\) yeses has two parts. Any one sequence with \(k\) yeses has probability \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\), because the trials are independent and their probabilities multiply. There are \(\binom nk\) such sequences, one for each way to choose which trials say yes. So the probability of \(k\) yeses is \[\Pr[S = k] = \binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n, \tag{1.5}\] where \(S\) is the count.

You rarely need this formula to get the mean and the variance. Write the count as a sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials and apply the rules for sums. The mean is \(np\), the sum of \(n\) means. The trials are independent, so the variance is \(np(1-p)\). The MGF of the count is the product of \(n\) coin MGFs, \((1 + p(e^\lambda - 1))^n\).

The variance of one trial, \(p(1-p)\), is zero at \(p = 0\) and at \(p = 1\). It is largest halfway between, where it equals a quarter. So a rate near zero is measured much more precisely than a rate near one half. A bound that uses only the fact that each trial lies between 0 and 1 must assume the worst case of a quarter. Hoeffding’s inequality in Concentration inequalities is such a bound, and it pays for this blindness when rates are small.

Every measured rate, such as accuracy or a false-positive rate, is a binomial count divided by \(n\). Write \(\hat p\) for the measured rate. By the scaling rule its mean is \(p\) and its variance is \(p(1-p)/n\), so its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\).

Here is what this means for a test set. Suppose a classifier makes a mistake on each test example independently with probability 0.05. On 2,000 test examples it makes 100 mistakes on average. The standard deviation of that count is about 9.7. Divided by the 2,000 examples, this is about 0.005 of the rate. So the measured error rate is typically within about half a percentage point of the true 5%.

Now lower the true error rate to 0.001. The same test set expects only 2 mistakes, with a standard deviation of about 1.4. The error relative to the count is now about 70%. A single test set can easily show no mistakes at all. The chance of that is \(0.999^{2000}\), which is about \(e^{-2}\), or one test set in seven. What sets the relative precision is the expected number of mistakes, not the number of test examples.

1.5 Rare events: Poisson

Many counts in machine learning add up many trials that are each unlikely. Examples are the mistakes of an accurate model on a large test set, or the examples drawn into a minibatch when each is included with a small probability. When the number of trials is large and each success is rare, the binomial depends only on the expected count \(\mu = np\). Holding \(\mu\) fixed while the number of trials grows gives the Poisson distribution, written \(\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)\). Its probability of a count \(k\) is \[\Pr[N = k] = e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots \tag{1.6}\] The factor \(e^{-\mu}\) comes from the chance that every other trial fails, \((1 - \mu/n)^n\), which tends to \(e^{-\mu}\) as the number of trials grows. Figure 1.1 shows how quickly the binomial approaches its limit. At 10 trials the binomial is visibly narrower than the Poisson. At 100 trials the two almost coincide.

A Poisson count has its variance equal to its mean, both \(\mu\). The reason is visible in the binomial variance \(np(1-p)\). As the success probability \(p\) shrinks, the factor \(1 - p\) tends to 1, and only \(np = \mu\) is left. The standard deviation is therefore \(\sqrt\mu\), and the spread relative to the mean is \(1/\sqrt\mu\). A Poisson count becomes precise relative to its mean only once you expect many events. With \(\mu = 4\) the standard deviation is 2. A count of 0 or 8, two standard deviations from the mean, is then unremarkable.

The Poisson MGF is \(\exp(\mu(e^\lambda - 1))\). Its form makes sums easy. Multiplying the MGFs of two independent Poisson counts adds their exponents, which gives the Poisson MGF with the two means added. So the sum of independent Poisson counts is again a Poisson count, with the means added.

The same MGF also bounds every count of independent yes-or-no outcomes, which is why it reappears in the Chernoff bound. The inequality \(1 + x \le e^x\) holds for every \(x\), because the line \(1 + x\) touches the curve \(e^x\) at zero and the curve bends upward away from it. Applied to the coin, it says that the MGF \(1 + p(e^\lambda - 1)\) is at most \(\exp(p(e^\lambda - 1))\). Multiplying over independent outcomes gives the result. A sum of independent outcomes with expected total \(\mu\) has an MGF no larger than the Poisson MGF with mean \(\mu\). This holds even when each outcome has its own probability. Concentration inequalities starts its Chernoff bound from this fact.

Counts are skewed to the right. A count cannot go below zero, but it can run far above its mean. For large \(k\), the probability of a count \(k\) is at most \((e\mu/k)^k\). This falls faster than any exponential decay, but more slowly than the tail of a normal distribution with the same variance.

Here is a count you will meet again. Some training procedures form each minibatch by including each of 60,000 examples independently with probability 0.01. The batch size is then a binomial count with many trials, each unlikely, so it is very close to \(\mathrm{Poisson}(600)\). Its mean is 600 and its standard deviation is about 24.5. Batch sizes between 550 and 650, about two standard deviations on either side, are typical. Concentration inequalities shows how rarely a batch leaves the range of 600 plus or minus 20%.

1.6 Waiting times: exponential

The Poisson counts how many rare events fall in a fixed window. The exponential answers the reverse question: starting now, how long until the first event? A deployed model makes a rare mistake, or a request arrives at a server. For this book the exponential matters mainly as a building block, because the Laplace distribution is made from two of them.

The exponential comes out of the same construction as the Poisson. Chop time into many short slots, and let each slot hold an event independently with a small probability. Write \(b\) for the average time between events, the scale. With \(n\) slots per unit of time, each slot holds an event with probability \(1/(bn)\). That keeps the expected number of events per unit of time at \(1/b\), however finely you chop. The first \(j\) slots are all empty with probability \((1 - 1/(bn))^j\), since the slots are independent. As the slots shrink, the chance that the wait exceeds a time \(t\) tends to a simple limit: \[\Pr[T > t] = e^{-t/b}, \qquad t \ge 0. \tag{1.7}\] The same answer comes from the Poisson count. The first event comes after time \(t\) exactly when no event falls before \(t\). The number of events before \(t\) is a Poisson count with mean \(t/b\), and it is zero with probability \(e^{-t/b}\).

Figure 1.2 shows the limit for a scale of 2. With one slot per unit of time, each slot holds an event with probability one half. The chance of waiting past \(t\) is then a staircase that halves at every whole number. With five slots per unit of time, the staircase already hugs the curve \(e^{-t/2}\). At \(t = 4\), the full edition’s script finds 0.0625 with one slot per unit of time. With five slots it finds 0.1216, and with a hundred slots 0.1347. The limit is 0.1353.

The exponential distribution \(\mathrm{Exp}(b)\) has the density \(\frac1be^{-y/b}\) for \(y \ge 0\). Its mean is the scale \(b\), so \(b\) really is the average wait. Its variance is \(b^2\). Some texts and libraries use the rate \(1/b\) instead of the scale. NumPy’s rng.exponential(b) takes the scale, so check which one a library expects before you trust a noise level.

The exponential’s MGF is \(1/(1 - b\lambda)\), but only for \(\lambda < 1/b\). The MGF averages \(e^{\lambda y}\) against the density \(e^{-y/b}\). Once \(\lambda\) reaches \(1/b\), the growing weight cancels or beats the shrinking density, and the average is infinite. An MGF that exists only on part of the line is the mark of an exponential tail.

The exponential also forgets how long you have waited. Suppose you have already waited a time \(s\) without an event. The chance of waiting at least \(t\) more is the same as the chance of waiting \(t\) from scratch: \[\Pr[Y \ge s + t \mid Y \ge s] = \Pr[Y \ge t]. \tag{1.8}\] The slots explain why. The slots after time \(s\) are independent of the empty slots before it, so the wait starts afresh. This memorylessness depends on that independence. If one event made the next more or less likely, a long quiet spell would carry information about the future, and the exponential would be the wrong model. The exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property.

Here is an example. A deployed classifier serves 50 requests per second. It misclassifies each request independently with probability \(10^{-4}\). Each request is a slot, so mistakes arrive at a rate of 0.005 per second. The time to the first mistake is then approximately exponential with a scale of 200 seconds. A 10-minute window lasts 600 seconds, so the chance of a window with no mistake is \(e^{-600/200} = e^{-3}\), about 0.050. The exact answer, \((1 - 10^{-4})^{30000}\), has one factor for each of the 30,000 requests. It agrees to three decimals, because each request’s chance of a mistake is tiny. By memorylessness, after 10 clean minutes you still expect to wait 200 seconds for the first mistake.

1.7 Laplace

The Laplace distribution is symmetric noise with exponential tails. Compared with a normal distribution of the same variance, it puts more probability near its centre and more far out. It is one of the two standard noise distributions in later parts of this book.

The Laplace density is an exponential density on each side of a centre \(\mu\), mirrored, with each side carrying half the probability. With scale \(b\), written \(\mathrm{Lap}(\mu, b)\), the density is \[f(x) = \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x - \mu|/b}. \tag{1.9}\] This chapter writes \(\mathrm{Lap}(b)\) when the centre is zero.

Most facts about the Laplace follow from the exponential. Take the centre to be zero. The density takes the same value at \(x\) and at \(-x\), so the size \(|X|\) collects the density from both sides. Its density is then \(\frac1be^{-x/b}\), which is exactly \(\mathrm{Exp}(b)\). Three facts follow at once. The two-sided tail is the exponential tail: \[\Pr[|X| \ge t] = e^{-t/b}. \tag{1.10}\] Each side carries half of it, so the chance of landing at or above \(t\) is \(\frac12e^{-t/b}\). The average size of a draw is \(b\).

The variance is \(2b^2\). The square of \(X\) is the square of \(|X|\), and the exponential’s second moment is \(2b^2\). The mean is zero by symmetry, so nothing is subtracted. Turned around, a Laplace distribution with standard deviation \(\sigma\) has scale \(b = \sigma/\sqrt2\). The MGF is \(1/(1 - b^2\lambda^2)\), again only for \(|\lambda| < 1/b\), because each side has an exponential tail.

The picture of two exponentials back to back suggests a way to build a Laplace draw. Subtract one exponential draw from another, independent one. The positive draw supplies the right half, and the negated draw supplies the left half. The MGFs confirm it. The difference has the MGF \(1/(1 - b\lambda)\) times \(1/(1 + b\lambda)\), which is the Laplace MGF. In the full edition’s script, the difference of a million pairs of \(\mathrm{Exp}(1)\) draws has a cumulative distribution function (CDF) within 0.0006 of the \(\mathrm{Lap}(1)\) CDF everywhere between \(-5\) and 5 (Figure 1.3).

Figure 1.4 puts a Laplace and a normal distribution side by side, both with variance 2. That means a Laplace scale of 1 and a normal standard deviation of \(\sqrt2\). On a linear scale, the Laplace has a sharp peak at zero and fatter tails. On a log scale the difference is structural. The logarithm of each density removes the exponential and leaves its exponent, plus a constant. The normal’s log density is a parabola, \(-x^2/(2\sigma^2)\). The Laplace’s log density is two straight lines, \(-|x|/b\). Far enough out, a parabola always falls below a line, so the normal’s tails are much lighter.

The numbers show the gap opening up. A Laplace draw lands at least three scales from zero with probability \(e^{-3}\), about 0.050. A normal draw with the same variance travels that far with probability about 0.034. Further out the gap grows quickly. The same shapes explain the MGFs. The Laplace’s straight-line decay can only beat the weight \(e^{\lambda x}\) when \(\lambda\) is small. The normal’s parabola beats it for every \(\lambda\).

The Laplace distribution also has a use in fitting models. Suppose each target is a linear function of its features plus independent Laplace noise. The log-likelihood of the data is then a constant minus the sum of absolute errors divided by \(b\). Maximizing the likelihood therefore minimizes the sum of absolute errors, just as normal noise leads to least squares. With no features, the fit estimates the centre by the median. Moving a candidate centre to the right lowers the error of every point above it and raises the error of every point below it. The sum stops falling exactly when half the points lie on each side. The median ignores how far out an extreme point lies, so it resists outliers far better than the mean does. Under a Laplace model, large errors are not surprising enough to dominate the fit.

1.8 Normal

The normal distribution appears whenever many small independent effects add up. It is the default model for measurement noise, and it is the light-tailed counterpart of the Laplace. Its log density is a downward parabola centred at the mean \(\mu\), with its width set by the standard deviation \(\sigma\). The normal distribution \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) has the density \[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}. \tag{1.11}\]

Every normal probability reduces to one function. Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation turns any normal quantity into a standard normal, with mean 0 and variance 1. Write \(\Phi(t)\) for the chance that a standard normal lands below \(t\). Then the chance that \(X\) lands below \(x\) is \(\Phi((x - \mu)/\sigma)\). The function \(\Phi\) has no closed form. In code you compute it from the complementary error function, \(\operatorname{erfc}\), which every maths library provides. That matters in the tails, as the code at the end of this chapter shows.

The normal MGF is \[M_X(\lambda) = e^{\mu\lambda + \sigma^2\lambda^2/2}, \tag{1.12}\] and it is finite for every \(\lambda\). Inside the MGF the weight \(e^{\lambda x}\) grows like a straight line in the exponent. The density shrinks like a parabola in the exponent, and a parabola always wins. The exponent of the MGF is a quadratic in \(\lambda\), which is what makes normal noise easy to combine.

Scaling and adding independent normal quantities gives another normal quantity. Scaling a quantity by \(a\) evaluates its MGF at \(a\lambda\). Adding independent quantities multiplies their MGFs, which adds the exponents. Both operations keep the exponent quadratic in \(\lambda\). So for independent normal quantities \(X_i\) with means \(\mu_i\) and variances \(\sigma_i^2\), \[\sum_i a_iX_i \sim \mathcal N\Big(\sum_i a_i\mu_i,\ \sum_i a_i^2\sigma_i^2\Big). \tag{1.13}\] Among distributions with finite variance, only the normal stays in its own family under every such combination. This is a classical result of Lévy, which the full edition cites. The Laplace fails the test. The sum of two independent \(\mathrm{Lap}(b)\) draws has the MGF \(1/(1 - b^2\lambda^2)^2\), which is not a Laplace MGF.

1.9 Normal noise in many dimensions

Noise is often added to a whole vector at once, such as a model’s parameters. Write \(X \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2I_d)\) for a vector of \(d\) independent normal coordinates, each with mean 0 and standard deviation \(\sigma\). Two facts about such a vector are used constantly, and they seem to pull in opposite directions.

The first fact is that the noise looks the same in every direction. Project \(X\) onto a fixed direction, given by a unit vector \(u\) whose squared entries add up to 1. The projection is a weighted sum of independent normal coordinates. By the rule for sums, it is normal with variance \(\sigma^2\) times the sum of the squared weights, which is just \(\sigma^2\). So along any direction, the noise is a single \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) draw. This needs the direction to be fixed in advance, independently of the noise. Along the noise’s own direction, the projection is the vector’s full length.

The second fact is that the length of the vector is large and predictable. Its squared length is a sum of \(d\) independent squared coordinates, each with mean \(\sigma^2\). So the squared length has mean \(d\sigma^2\). Its spread relative to that mean shrinks like \(1/\sqrt d\), as for any average of independent terms. The length itself therefore sits close to \(\sigma\sqrt d\), with a relative spread of about \(1/\sqrt{2d}\). A normal vector in high dimension is almost never near the origin. It lives on a thin shell. Table 1.1 shows the shell forming as \(d\) grows.

Put the two facts together on a model with a million parameters and noise of standard deviation 0.01 on each. The noise vector has a length of about \(0.01 \times 1000 = 10\). Its relative spread is under 0.1%, so the length is almost exactly 10. Yet its projection onto any fixed direction is only a \(\mathcal N(0, 0.01^2)\) draw. The noise is large in length and small in every fixed direction (Figure 1.5).

1.10 The central limit theorem

The central limit theorem (CLT) explains why the normal distribution is everywhere. Take a sum of \(n\) independent draws from the same distribution, with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). The sum has mean \(n\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt n\). Subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation, and the result always has mean 0 and variance 1. The CLT says more. Whatever the distribution of each draw, the shape of this standardized sum approaches the standard normal as \(n\) grows. The theorem needs the variance to be finite, because the standardization divides by it. The full edition states the theorem and cites its proof.

The best way to see the theorem work is to start from a distribution that looks nothing like a normal. The exponential with scale 1 is badly skewed. It cannot go below zero, its density is highest at zero, and it has a long right tail. Figure 1.6 shows the standardized sum of \(n\) such draws. At \(n = 1\) it is the exponential itself, with a hard wall where the draw would be zero. At \(n = 2\) the peak rounds off, because two draws are rarely both near zero. At \(n = 5\) the bump is in the right place but still leans. At \(n = 30\) the curve is close to the standard normal, though a small lean remains.

The leftover lean is measured by the skewness, the average cubed distance from the mean in units of the standard deviation. Cubing keeps the sign, so a long right tail makes the skewness positive. A normal distribution is symmetric, so its skewness is zero. For a sum of \(n\) exponential draws, the skewness is \(2/\sqrt n\). It falls, but only like \(1/\sqrt n\). The largest gap between the sum’s CDF and the normal CDF falls at the same rate, as Table 1.2 shows.

Multiply each gap by \(\sqrt n\) and the product settles near 0.133, so the error decays like \(1/\sqrt n\). This rate holds in general. The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the gap by a constant times the third moment of the draws, divided by \(\sigma^3\sqrt n\). The constant is below 0.48 (Shevtsova, 2011). The third moment measures skew, so the more skewed the draws, the more of them you need. A common rule of thumb says that the normal approximation to a binomial count is reasonable once its variance \(np(1-p)\) is about 10 or more. The normal curve fits the Poisson in Figure 1.1 for the same reason, because a Poisson count with mean 5 is a sum of five independent Poisson counts with mean 1.

The CLT describes the bulk of the distribution, not its far tails. The error it controls is an absolute error of order \(1/\sqrt n\). A tail probability that a guarantee depends on can be far smaller than that error. In the far tail the normal approximation can then be wrong by a large factor. Bounds on tails are the job of Concentration inequalities.

1.11 A density against its shifted copy

Here is the question that later chapters build on. You see a noisy measurement \(x = \mu + X\) of an unknown value \(\mu\). The noise \(X\) has density \(f\) centred at zero. The unknown value can be only one of two candidates, zero or a known shift \(s\). How strongly can one observation favour one candidate over the other?

Each candidate gives \(x\) its own density. If the value is zero, the density of \(x\) is \(f(x)\). If the value is \(s\), the density is \(f(x - s)\), the same curve moved sideways by \(s\). Its value at \(x\) is the value that \(f\) had at \(x - s\). With a shift of 1, for example, the shifted density at 1 equals \(f(0)\), so its peak sits at 1.

To compare the candidates, compare the two densities at the observation. Their ratio says how many times more likely \(x\) is under the first candidate than under the second. Its logarithm is the log ratio: \[L(x) = \ln f(x) - \ln f(x - s). \tag{1.14}\] It is positive when \(x\) favours zero, negative when \(x\) favours \(s\), and zero when \(x\) says nothing. If the log ratio is bounded, no single observation can make one candidate much more likely than the other. If it is unbounded, some observations settle the question almost for certain.

The answer comes from the shapes of the two log densities in Figure 1.4. The normal’s log density is a parabola, and both copies have the same curvature. Subtracting them cancels the squared terms and leaves a straight line: \[L(x) = \frac{s^2 - 2sx}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{1.15}\] A line with a nonzero slope has no bound, so for normal noise the log ratio grows without limit as \(x\) moves out.

The Laplace’s log density is \(-|x|/b\), a distance from the centre divided by the scale. Moving the centre by \(s\) changes the distance from any point by at most \(|s|\). So the log ratio can never exceed \(|s|/b\) in size: \[L(x) = \frac{|x - s| - |x|}{b}, \qquad |L(x)| \le \frac{|s|}{b}. \tag{1.16}\] The ratio of the two densities therefore never exceeds \(e^{|s|/b}\). The bound is reached, not just approached. For a positive shift, every observation at or below zero is exactly \(s\) closer to one centre than to the other. The same holds for every observation at or above \(s\). Between the two centres, the log ratio falls along a straight line from one limit to the other.

Table 1.3 puts numbers on both formulas, with a shift of 1 and both noise scales set to 1. Both log ratios are zero at the midpoint, 0.5, where the two candidates are equally plausible. Far out they part ways. At \(x = 10\) the normal log ratio is \(-9.5\). The observation is then about 13,360 times more likely under the shifted candidate. The Laplace never says more than about 2.72 times, however far out \(x\) lies (Figure 1.7).

For the Laplace, the two copies stay within a fixed factor of each other everywhere. For the normal, they are close in the bulk, but the ratio is unbounded in the tails. Unbounded does not mean often large, though. The observations that give extreme ratios sit in tails that carry very little probability. The next three chapters make this precise. Concentration inequalities bounds how rare those observations are. Divergences averages the log ratio over \(x\). Hypothesis testing says what an observer can decide from it.

1.12 Code

Normal tails need care in code. The obvious way to get the chance of exceeding \(t\) is \(1 - \Phi(t)\). Far out, \(\Phi(t)\) is so close to 1 that the subtraction loses every digit and returns zero. The complementary error function computes the small tail directly. The snippet also checks the Laplace and normal tails from Section 1.7.

from math import erfc, exp, sqrt


def normal_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Chance that a standard normal lands below x."""
    return 0.5 * erfc(-x / sqrt(2))


def normal_tail(t: float) -> float:
    """Chance that a standard normal lands at or above t, computed without subtraction."""
    return 0.5 * erfc(t / sqrt(2))


def laplace_two_sided_tail(t: float, b: float) -> float:
    """Chance that a Lap(b) draw lands at least t from zero."""
    return exp(-t / b)


print(f"{1 - normal_cdf(10.0):.3e}")  # 0.000e+00
print(f"{normal_tail(10.0):.3e}")  # 7.620e-24
# Same variance 2: Laplace scale 1 against normal sigma sqrt(2), at three Laplace scales.
print(f"{laplace_two_sided_tail(3.0, 1.0):.3f} {2 * normal_tail(3.0 / sqrt(2)):.3f}")  # 0.050 0.034

1.13 Cheat sheet

Rules of thumb

  • Means always add. Variances add only for independent quantities. An average of \(n\) independent draws has its standard deviation divided by \(\sqrt n\).
  • A measured rate has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). Its relative precision is set by the expected count \(np\), not by \(n\).
  • Many unlikely trials give a Poisson count, whose variance equals its mean. The waiting time for the first event is exponential, and two exponentials back to back make a Laplace.
  • Normal noise in \(d\) dimensions has length about \(\sigma\sqrt d\), but it is only \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) along any direction fixed in advance.
  • The CLT describes the bulk, with an error of order \(1/\sqrt n\). Never use it for small tail probabilities.
  • For a shift \(s\), the Laplace log ratio is at most \(|s|/b\). The normal log ratio is unbounded, but large only in tails that carry very little probability.
