1  Probability distributions

1.1 Why this matters

Later parts keep asking two questions about a random output: how wide is it, and how hard is it to tell apart from a slightly different one? Both answers depend on its distribution, and a handful of families cover almost every case: counts of events, such as errors on a test set, and additive noise, usually normal or Laplace.

These families are built from one another. A binomial is a sum of coin flips, a Poisson is a binomial with many unlikely trials, a Laplace is two exponential waiting times back to back, and a normal is what many small independent pieces add up to. Each family’s spread and tail follow from how it is built, and so does what matters most later: how far a density drifts from a shifted copy of itself.

1.2 Counts: binomial and Poisson

Every measured rate (accuracy, error rate, false positive rate) is a count of yes/no outcomes divided by the number of trials. With \(n\) independent trials, each a yes with probability \(p\), the count is \(\mathrm{Binomial}(n, p)\). Its mean is \(np\) and its variance \(np(1-p)\), because one trial has variance \(p(1-p)\) and variances add over independent terms. Dividing the count by \(n\), the measured rate \(\hat p\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\): four times the data only halves the error.

The variance vanishes near \(p = 0\), which sounds like good news for rare events but is not. What matters is the error relative to the rate, and the count’s standard deviation, about \(\sqrt{np}\), divided by its mean \(np\) is about \(1/\sqrt{np}\). So relative precision is set by the expected count, not by the number of trials. A classifier that errs with probability 0.05 on 2,000 test examples makes about 100 errors, with standard deviation about 9.7, and the measured rate is typically within about half a point of 5%. At an error rate of 0.001 the same test set expects 2 errors, with standard deviation about 1.4, a relative error of about 70%, and one test set in seven shows no error at all.

When the trials are many and each is unlikely, the binomial depends only on the expected count \(\mu = np\), and the limit is the Poisson distribution \(\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)\). Its variance equals its mean, \(\mu\), because the factor \(1 - p\) in the binomial variance tends to 1. A training loop that includes each of 60,000 examples independently with probability 0.01 gets batches whose size is close to \(\mathrm{Poisson}(600)\): mean 600, standard deviation about 24.5 (Figure 1.1).

1.3 Laplace and exponential

The waiting time for a rare event, with many independent chances each unlikely, follows the exponential distribution \(\mathrm{Exp}(b)\): density \(\frac1be^{-y/b}\) on \(y \ge 0\), mean \(b\), tail \(e^{-t/b}\). The Laplace distribution puts one exponential on each side of a centre. \(\mathrm{Lap}(\mu, b)\), with centre \(\mu\) and scale \(b\), has density \[f(x) = \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x - \mu|/b}. \tag{1.1}\] So the size \(|X - \mu|\) is exactly \(\mathrm{Exp}(b)\): the average absolute error is \(b\), the variance is \(2b^2\) (so a standard deviation \(\sigma\) means \(b = \sigma/\sqrt2\)), and the two-sided tail is exact, \[\Pr[|X - \mu| \ge t] = e^{-t/b}. \tag{1.2}\]

The Laplace has heavier tails than a normal of the same variance, and the log scale shows why (Figure 1.2). The logarithm of each density is its exponent: two straight lines \(-|x|/b\) for the Laplace, a parabola \(-x^2/(2\sigma^2)\) for the normal. Far enough out a parabola always falls below a line, so the normal’s tail eventually becomes far lighter. With the same variance, the Laplace exceeds three times its scale with probability \(e^{-3} \approx 0.050\), the normal only with probability about 0.034, and the gap widens. So a tail bound shaped for the normal fails for Laplace noise far enough out.

Libraries differ in whether they take the scale \(b\) or the rate \(1/b\) (NumPy’s rng.exponential(b) takes the scale), so check before trusting a noise level.

1.4 Normal

\(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) has density \(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\), and every probability reduces to the standard normal cumulative distribution function (CDF) \(\Phi\), through \(\Pr[X \le x] = \Phi((x - \mu)/\sigma)\). It stays normal under sums: \(\sum_ia_iX_i\) for independent normals is normal with mean \(\sum_ia_i\mu_i\) and variance \(\sum_ia_i^2\sigma_i^2\).

That rule explains normal noise in many dimensions. A vector \(X \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2I_d)\) has \(d\) independent coordinates, so its squared length is a sum of \(d\) terms of mean \(\sigma^2\), and like any sum of many independent terms its relative spread shrinks: the length sits at \(\sigma\sqrt d\), with relative spread about \(1/\sqrt{2d}\) (Table 1.1). Yet the projection onto a fixed unit vector is a weighted sum whose squared weights add to 1, so it is just \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\). Noise \(\mathcal N(0, 0.01^2I_d)\) on a model with \(d = 10^6\) parameters has length about \(0.01 \times 1000 = 10\), yet along any direction fixed in advance it is only \(\mathcal N(0, 0.01^2)\): large in norm, small in every direction.

The central limit theorem (CLT) explains why the normal is everywhere: the standardized sum of \(n\) independent draws with finite variance approaches \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\). But the skew of the summands averages out only like \(1/\sqrt n\): for sums of exponentials the largest error in the CDF is still 0.0243 at \(n = 30\). That is fine for the bulk and useless in the far tail, whose probabilities are much smaller than that error; tails need Concentration inequalities. Small tails need care in code too: \(1 - \Phi(x)\) subtracts two nearly equal numbers and loses every digit, so compute the tail directly as \(\frac12\operatorname{erfc}(x/\sqrt2)\).

1.5 A density against its shifted copy

You observe \(x = \mu + X\) with noise density \(f\), and \(\mu\) is either 0 or a known shift \(s\). The two candidates give \(x\) the densities \(f(x)\) and \(f(x - s)\), the same curve moved sideways, and how much one observation favours the first is the log ratio \[L(x) = \ln f(x) - \ln f(x - s). \tag{1.3}\] If \(|L(x)|\) is bounded, no single observation can settle the question; if not, some observations settle it almost for certain.

The log of each density is its exponent, so the answer comes from the shapes in Figure 1.2. Two copies of the normal’s parabola have the same curvature, so subtracting them cancels the \(x^2\) terms and leaves a straight line, unbounded: \[L(x) = \frac{s^2 - 2sx}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{1.4}\] The Laplace’s log density is \(-|x|/b\), a distance from the centre divided by \(b\). Moving the centre by \(s\) changes the distance from any point by at most \(|s|\), so \[L(x) = \frac{|x - s| - |x|}{b}, \qquad |L(x)| \le \frac{|s|}{b}, \tag{1.5}\] and the bound is reached on whole half-lines, every \(x \le 0\) and every \(x \ge s\), where both distances change by exactly \(s\).

Take \(s = 1\), \(\sigma = 1\) and \(b = 1\) (Table 1.2 and Figure 1.3). At \(x = 10\) the normal log ratio is \(-9.5\): the observation is about 13,360 times more likely under \(\mu = 1\). The Laplace never says more than \(e \approx 2.72\) times, however far out \(x\) lies.

But “unbounded” does not mean “often large”: the normal’s extreme ratios sit in tails with very little probability. How little, and what it buys an observer, is what Divergences and Hypothesis testing measure. And only shift over scale matters: doubling the noise is the same as halving the shift.

1.6 Cheat sheet

Rules of thumb

  • A measured rate has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\); its relative precision is set by the expected count \(np\).
  • Normal noise in \(d\) dimensions has norm \(\approx \sigma\sqrt d\) and is \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) along every fixed direction.
  • Laplace shifted by \(s\): log ratio at most \(|s|/b\). Normal: unbounded, but large only in the tails, which carry very little probability.
