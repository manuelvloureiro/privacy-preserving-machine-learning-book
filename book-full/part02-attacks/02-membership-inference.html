10  Membership inference

A hospital trains a model to predict a disease and publishes it. You hold one patient’s record. Was that record in the training set? If it was, you have learned that the patient was treated at that hospital for that disease, although the model never output a diagnosis. This is membership inference, the simplest question an attacker can ask about a trained model and the standard measure of how much a model leaks. An attack is scored by two rates. Its true positive rate (TPR) is the fraction of members it flags as members, and its false positive rate (FPR) is the fraction of non-members it wrongly flags.

The one idea of this chapter is that membership inference is a test about one record. Training is random, because the rest of the training set and the optimization vary from run to run. So the model’s output on a record, such as its confidence in the record’s label, has one distribution over models trained with that record, its IN distribution, and another over models trained without it, its OUT distribution. The attacker sees one output and must say which of the two produced it. A typical record is predicted well either way, so its two distributions nearly coincide, while an atypical record is predicted badly unless the model saw it. An attack that says “member” whenever the model is confident therefore flags easy records that were never trained on, and the best attack compares each record’s output with that record’s own two distributions.

The running example scales down the setup of Carlini et al. (2022) to a linear model. A pool of 1,000 labelled points in \(\mathbb R^{100}\) holds 950 typical points and 50 outliers, whose labels nothing else in the data predicts. Logistic-regression models train on random halves of the pool. The attacker targets 128 of these models, and each of their 1,000 pairs with a pool point is one guess, so there are \(128 \times 500 = 64{,}000\) member pairs and as many non-member pairs. At an FPR of \(10^{-3}\), the attack that thresholds the loss flags a fraction 0.0009 of members, which is what flagging at random would do. The per-record test flags 0.0149, and 87% of the members it finds are outliers.

10.1 The loss attack and the generalization gap

A model is fitted to its training data, so its loss on a training point is usually lower than on a fresh one. This section asks how much membership that difference alone gives away, which is the natural first attack because it needs nothing but the model’s loss.

Attacks are scored in the membership game of Threat models and privacy games. A challenger draws a training set \(D\) and flips a fair coin for a hidden bit \(b\). If \(b = 1\), the target \(z = (x, y)\), a feature vector and its label, is drawn uniformly from \(D\); if \(b = 0\), it is a fresh record from the same population. The adversary sees \(z\) and the trained model \(\theta\), and guesses \(b'\). Half the games have \(b = 1\) and are won with probability TPR, and the other half are won with probability \(1 - \mathrm{FPR}\). So by Proposition 9.1 the adversary wins with probability \(\frac12 + \frac12\mathrm{Adv}\), where the advantage \(\mathrm{Adv} = \mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR}\) measures how much more often the attack says “member” to members than to non-members.

Yeom et al. (2018) turned the lower loss on members into an attack, whose cleanest version needs a loss of known range.

Definition 10.1 (Loss attacks) Let the loss \(\ell(\theta; z)\) of the model on the target take values in \([0, B]\). The bounded-loss attack says “member” with probability \(1 - \ell(\theta; z)/B\). The loss threshold attack says “member” when \(\ell(\theta; z) < \tau\), for a threshold \(\tau\) such as the mean training loss, which a model’s report often states.

The randomized rule makes the probability of saying “member” linear in the loss, so the advantage should be the difference between the average loss of non-members and of members, divided by \(B\). That difference is the generalization gap. Write \(\theta_D\) for the model trained on \(D\) and \(\mathcal D\) for the population. The gap is the model’s expected loss on fresh data, its population risk \(\mathcal L_{\mathcal D}(\theta_D)\), minus its average loss on its own training set, its empirical risk \(\mathcal L(\theta_D; D)\).

Proposition 10.1 (The loss attack’s advantage is the generalization gap) In the membership game, with expectations over the training set, the training run and the target, the bounded-loss attack has advantage \[\mathrm{Adv} = \frac{\mathbb E[\ell(\theta; z) \mid b = 0] - \mathbb E[\ell(\theta; z) \mid b = 1]}{B} = \frac{\mathbb E_D\big[\mathcal L_{\mathcal D}(\theta_D) - \mathcal L(\theta_D; D)\big]}{B}, \tag{10.1}\] the expected generalization gap divided by \(B\). For the 0-1 loss, which is 0 on a correct prediction and 1 on a wrong one, \(B = 1\), the attack says “member” exactly when the model classifies \(z\) correctly, and its advantage is the test error minus the training error.

Proof. The plan is to write the TPR and the FPR as average losses, subtract them, and then recognize the two averages as the test loss and the training loss.

  1. The TPR. Given \(b = 1\), the attack says “member” with probability \(1 - \ell/B\) for whatever loss the target has. Averaging over targets and training runs gives \(\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - \mathbb E[\ell \mid b = 1]/B\).
  2. The FPR. The same calculation with \(b = 0\) gives \(\mathrm{FPR} = 1 - \mathbb E[\ell \mid b = 0]/B\).
  3. Subtract. The advantage is \(\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR}\). The two 1s cancel, which leaves the first form of Equation 10.1.
  4. Name the two averages. When \(b = 1\), \(z\) is a uniformly chosen training point, so its expected loss is the expected empirical risk. When \(b = 0\), \(z\) is a fresh draw independent of \(D\), so its expected loss is the expected population risk. Substituting both gives the second form.
  5. The 0-1 loss. With \(B = 1\), the probability \(1 - \ell\) is 1 on a correct prediction and 0 on a wrong one. So \(\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - \text{train error}\) and \(\mathrm{FPR} = 1 - \text{test error}\), and their difference is the test error minus the training error.

So overfitting implies leakage, by an amount you can read off the training and test errors without running an attack. By Theorem 7.1, the expected gap is the average self-influence of the training records (Definition 7.3): how much each record’s own loss rises when it is replaced in training by a fresh record. The advantage is therefore an average of how much each record is memorized, and Section 10.5 looks at the records one by one. But a small gap does not mean a private model. The loss attack ignores which record it is looking at, and Section 10.3 beats it by a wide margin.

An attack can read three numbers from the model’s output on \(z\). The confidence \(p_y\) is the probability the model gives to the true label, and the usual classification loss is the cross-entropy \(\ell = -\ln p_y\). The logit of the confidence is the log of the odds the model gives to the true label, \[\phi = \ln\frac{p_y}{1 - p_y}. \tag{10.2}\] The rest of the chapter works with \(\phi\). The confidence crowds against 1, while \(\phi\) keeps confident outputs apart, and that matters once values are fitted with a bell curve. The next lemma shows that a threshold on \(\phi\) is a threshold on the loss, and that for logistic regression \(\phi\) is linear in the weights, which is why a bell curve fits it (Section 10.3).

Lemma 10.1 (For logistic regression, the logit of the confidence is the margin) Let a logistic-regression model with weights \(\theta\) give the true label \(y \in \{-1, 1\}\) of a record \(z = (x, y)\) the probability \(p_y = 1/(1 + e^{-y\langle \theta, x\rangle})\). Then \[\phi = \ln\frac{p_y}{1 - p_y} = y\langle \theta, x\rangle \qquad\text{and}\qquad \ell = -\ln p_y = \ln\big(1 + e^{-\phi}\big). \tag{10.3}\] So a threshold on any of the confidence, the loss and \(\phi\) is a threshold on the others.

Proof.

  1. The odds. Write \(t = y\langle \theta, x\rangle\). Then \(1 - p_y = e^{-t}/(1 + e^{-t})\), so the factor \(1 + e^{-t}\) cancels in the ratio \(p_y/(1 - p_y)\), which leaves \(e^t\). Its logarithm is \(\phi = t\), the margin: how far the point lies on the correct side of the decision boundary.
  2. The loss. The reciprocal of the confidence is \(1/p_y = 1 + e^{-\phi}\), so the loss \(-\ln p_y\) is \(\ln(1 + e^{-\phi})\).
  3. Monotone. The confidence rises with \(\phi\), and by step 2 the loss falls as \(\phi\) rises. So “\(\phi\) above a cut” is the same event as “loss below the matching cut”.

Example 10.1 In the running example, each of the 950 typical points has features \(x\) drawn from the normal distribution \(\mathcal N(0, I/100)\), whose 100 independent coordinates each have variance \(1/100\), so \(\|x\| \approx 1\). Its label \(y = \pm1\) follows the first coordinate with some noise: \[\Pr[y = 1 \mid x] = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-30x_1}}. \tag{10.4}\] The 50 outliers have the same kind of \(x\) scaled by 5 and a label drawn by a fair coin, so a model gets an outlier’s label right only by fitting that point itself. Each model is logistic regression with the penalty \(\frac\lambda2\|\theta\|^2\), \(\lambda = 10^{-3}\), trained on a random half of the pool (\(n = 500\)). Each model trained on a random half is followed by one trained on the other half, so every point is a member of exactly half the models.

Over the 128 target models, the training error is 0.1495, and the test error, on the half of the pool each model did not see, is 0.2663. “Member if correctly classified” therefore has \(\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - 0.1495 = 0.8505\) and \(\mathrm{FPR} = 1 - 0.2663 = 0.7337\). Its advantage, \(0.8505 - 0.7337 = 0.1168\), is exactly the gap \(0.2663 - 0.1495\).

The logistic loss is unbounded, so the bounded-loss attack needs it clipped: any loss above \(B = 2\) is replaced by 2. The mean clipped loss is 0.4158 on members and 0.5433 on non-members. So Equation 10.1 predicts an advantage of \((0.5433 - 0.4158)/2 = 0.0638\), and simulating the randomized attack gives 0.0639. The loss threshold attack at the mean unclipped training loss, \(\tau = 0.4159\), has \(\mathrm{TPR} = 0.5936\), \(\mathrm{FPR} = 0.4834\) and advantage 0.1102.

By the win rate \(\frac12 + \frac12\mathrm{Adv}\), the three attacks win between 53% and 56% of the games. None of them identifies anyone with confidence, because an FPR near one half flags half of the non-members too. Figure 10.1 shows why: the two distributions of \(\phi\) are shifted, but they almost entirely overlap.

10.2 Shadow models

An attacker who wants a better rule than “low loss means member” has to know what members’ outputs look like. It has no labelled examples, because it does not know who is in the target’s training set. This section shows how the attacker can learn that from models of its own, whose training sets it chooses (Shokri et al. 2017), and what the best rule learned this way can achieve.

Definition 10.2 (Shadow models and the attack model) Suppose the attacker holds records from the target’s population. A shadow model is a model that the attacker trains like the target, with the same recipe, on a training set that includes each of its records with probability \(\frac12\). For each shadow model and record, the attacker keeps the shadow model’s output \(o\) on the record and the membership label \(m\), with \(m = 1\) for “in”. The attack model \(h(o) \in (0, 1)\) is a classifier trained on these pairs by minimizing the cross-entropy, \(-\ln h\) on “in” pairs and \(-\ln(1 - h)\) on “out” pairs. It says “member” when \(h(o)\) exceeds a threshold.

In the running example the output is \(o = (y, \phi)\), the class and the logit of the confidence. With enough shadow data, the attack model learns the best rule available from \(o\) alone. The reason is that cross-entropy is minimized by the true probability of “in”, and that probability ranks outputs by their likelihood ratio.

Proposition 10.2 (The attack model is the best record-blind attack) Let \(q(o)\) and \(p(o)\) be the densities of the output for members and for non-members, each a mixture over all records, and let the attack model range over all functions of \(o\). As the number of shadow pairs grows, \(h(o)\) tends to the posterior probability of membership, and with a fair prior the posterior odds are the likelihood ratio: \[h(o) \to \Pr[b = 1 \mid o], \qquad \frac{\Pr[b = 1 \mid o]}{\Pr[b = 0 \mid o]} = \frac{q(o)}{p(o)}. \tag{10.5}\] So no attack that sees only \(o\) has a higher TPR, at any FPR, than a threshold on \(h\).

Proof. The proof has four links: the shadow pairs look like the real game, cross-entropy recovers the posterior, the posterior ranks outputs by the likelihood ratio, and a threshold on the likelihood ratio is optimal.

  1. The shadow pairs have the right distribution. A shadow model is trained like the target, and each record is in or out with probability \(\frac12\). So a shadow pair \((o, m)\) has the same joint distribution as a pair of target output and membership bit in the game.
  2. Cross-entropy is minimized by the posterior. Fix one output \(o\), and let \(\rho = \Pr[m = 1 \mid o]\) be the probability of “in” given that output. If the attack model predicts \(h\) there, its expected loss is \(-\rho\ln h - (1 - \rho)\ln(1 - h)\). This is convex in \(h\), and its derivative \(-\rho/h + (1 - \rho)/(1 - h)\) is zero at \(h = \rho\), so that is the minimum. The same holds at every \(o\) separately, so the best function is \(h(o) = \Pr[m = 1 \mid o]\), which by step 1 is \(\Pr[b = 1 \mid o]\).
  3. Bayes’ rule. The joint probability of a world, \(b = 1\) or \(b = 0\), and an output is the prior of the world times the density of the output in that world. So the posterior odds are the prior odds times \(q(o)/p(o)\), and with a fair prior the prior odds are 1. The odds \(h/(1 - h)\) rise with \(h\), so a threshold on \(h\) is a threshold on \(q(o)/p(o)\).
  4. Neyman–Pearson. The Neyman–Pearson lemma says that a threshold on the likelihood ratio has the highest TPR at its FPR among all tests that see the same observation.

The attack model is optimal for the wrong question. It sees \((y, \phi)\) and not which record produced it, so it cannot know that \(\phi = 2\) is high for one record and low for another. When members tend to have the higher \(\phi\) within each class, it is the loss attack with a threshold for each class (Exercise 7).

What shadow models really buy is calibration. The attacker picks the threshold at which the desired fraction of its own shadow non-members pass, and trusts that real non-members behave the same way. Salem et al. (2019) found that even one shadow model, or shadow data from another distribution, often works for this. Calibration is the job that shadow models keep in Section 10.3.

Example 10.2 For each class, the attack model is a logistic regression of “in” on \(\phi\) with an intercept, trained on the roughly 32,000 shadow pairs of that class (64 models times the roughly 500 pool points with that label). Both slopes are positive, 0.238 for \(y = -1\) and 0.158 for \(y = +1\). So within each class the predicted probability of “in” rises with \(\phi\), and the attack is a threshold on the loss for each class.

To compare it with the loss attack over all thresholds, draw each attack’s receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, its TPR against its FPR as the threshold sweeps. The area under the curve (AUC) equals the probability that a random member scores higher than a random non-member (Hypothesis testing). The attack model and the loss attack both have AUC 0.577, and the same TPR at every FPR to within sampling noise (Table 10.3).

Calibration works, since a threshold set on the shadow models for an FPR of 1% gives an FPR of 0.0095 on the 128 target models the attacker never saw. But the TPR there is 0.0098, no better than chance, because a global threshold on \(\phi\) cannot single out any member at a low FPR.

10.3 LiRA: a likelihood-ratio test per record

The attacks of the last two sections compare a record’s output with everyone else’s outputs. The right comparison is with the same record’s output under models that did and did not train on it. This section builds that test and proves that it is the best one.

Carlini et al. (2022) built the likelihood ratio attack (LiRA) on that comparison. For a record \(z\), the attacker has a few dozen shadow values of \(\phi_z\), the logit of the confidence on \(z\), and summarizes them by bell curves, a shape justified after the theorem below.

Definition 10.3 (LiRA, online and offline) For a record \(z\), let \(\mu_{\mathrm{in}, z}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{in}, z}\) be the mean and standard deviation of \(\phi_z\) over the shadow models that trained on \(z\), and \(\mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{out}, z}\) those over the shadow models that did not. Write \(\mathcal N(\phi; \mu, \sigma^2)\) for the normal density at \(\phi\). Online LiRA scores the target model’s \(\phi_z\) by the log of the ratio of the IN fit to the OUT fit, \[\ln\Lambda_z(\phi_z) = \ln\mathcal N\big(\phi_z;\, \mu_{\mathrm{in}, z}, \sigma_{\mathrm{in}, z}^2\big) - \ln\mathcal N\big(\phi_z;\, \mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}, \sigma_{\mathrm{out}, z}^2\big). \tag{10.6}\] Offline LiRA uses only the OUT fit, and scores how many OUT standard deviations the output lies above its usual value, \[\frac{\phi_z - \mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{out}, z}}. \tag{10.7}\] Each says “member” when its score exceeds a threshold that is the same for every record.

Online LiRA needs shadow models that trained on each new target, while offline LiRA’s OUT models can be trained once and reused. Why one threshold on the ratio for all records? The adversary’s observation is the pair of record and output, and the Neyman–Pearson lemma (Theorem 4.1) says the best test thresholds its likelihood ratio. The record itself carries no evidence, so it drops out.

Theorem 10.1 (One threshold on the per-record likelihood ratio is optimal) In the membership game, suppose the target record \(z\) is drawn from the same distribution whether \(b = 1\) or \(b = 0\), and let \(q_z\) and \(p_z\) be the IN and OUT densities of \(\phi_z\). Among all attacks that see \(z\) and \(\phi_z\), the highest TPR at each FPR is reached by saying “member” when \(\Lambda_z(\phi_z) = q_z(\phi_z)/p_z(\phi_z)\) exceeds one threshold, the same for every record. When the Gaussian fits are exact, this test is online LiRA.

Proof. The plan is to write the density of the adversary’s view in each world, see that the record’s part cancels in the likelihood ratio, and apply the Neyman–Pearson lemma.

  1. The view is a pair. The density of the pair is the probability of drawing the record times the density of the output given the record. Write it as \(g(z)\,q_z(\phi)\) when \(b = 1\) and as \(g(z)\,p_z(\phi)\) when \(b = 0\).
  2. The record’s factor is shared. By hypothesis, the record is drawn the same way whether or not it ends up in the training set, so \(g\) is the same function in both worlds. The running example satisfies this, since every pool point is a member of each model with probability \(\frac12\).
  3. The ratio. Since the factor \(g(z)\) is the same in both worlds, it cancels: \[\frac{g(z)\,q_z(\phi)}{g(z)\,p_z(\phi)} = \frac{q_z(\phi)}{p_z(\phi)} = \Lambda_z(\phi). \tag{10.8}\]
  4. Neyman–Pearson. The best test of the pair thresholds its likelihood ratio at one value, by Theorem 4.1. So different records are compared through their ratios, never through their raw \(\phi\). With exact Gaussian fits, the log of the ratio is Equation 10.6.

The hypothesis is needed, since if members came from one hospital and non-members from another, the record alone would reveal \(b\). The theorem is an instance of Lemma 9.2 in Threat models and privacy games. An attacker that ignores the record sees the IN and OUT distributions averaged over all records, where the large shift of a hard record is diluted among the small shifts of the easy ones. An attacker that knows the record can use that record’s own shift.

The bell-curve shape is justified because, by Lemma 10.1, \(\phi_z\) is linear in the weights. The regularized estimate \(\theta\) solves an equation that averages many independent gradient terms, so it is approximately Gaussian over the training randomness, by the central limit theorem that Section G.5 proves for such estimates. So \(\phi_z\) is close to normal, and for deep networks Carlini et al. found the same.

Offline LiRA is a weaker test, because without IN models it cannot write down \(q_z\). It only asks whether \(\phi_z\) lies unusually far above its OUT values, which is the direction in which training on \(z\) moves it. The two scores can be compared exactly in the simplest case, where the IN and OUT fits have the same standard deviation \(\sigma_z\). Write \(t_z\) for the offline score, the output measured in units of \(\sigma_z\) from the OUT mean. Then \(t_z\) is standard normal under OUT, and under IN it is normal with variance 1 and mean the separation \[s_z = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{in}, z} - \mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}}{\sigma_z}. \tag{10.9}\] Substituting the two normal densities into Equation 10.6 gives \(\ln\Lambda_z = s_zt_z - s_z^2/2\), as Exercise 2 shows. For one record \(s_z\) is a fixed positive number, so both scores rank that record’s outputs alike. Across records they differ, because online LiRA weights each \(t_z\) by \(s_z\), while offline LiRA treats every \(t_z\) the same.

How much each record can be caught at a given FPR is then a question about two unit-variance Gaussians. Write \(\Phi\) for the standard normal distribution function, so that \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha)\) is the point a standard normal exceeds with probability \(\alpha\). Theorem 4.3 in Hypothesis testing shows that when an observation is \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\) in one world and \(\mathcal N(s, 1)\) in the other, the most members any test can catch at FPR \(\alpha\) is \[\mathrm{TPR}(\alpha) = 1 - \Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha) - s\big). \tag{10.10}\] At \(\alpha = 10^{-3}\) the cut-off is 3.0902. With a separation of 0.5, a member must still clear \(3.0902 - 0.5 = 2.59\) standard deviations above its own mean, so the TPR is \(1 - \Phi(2.59) = 0.0048\). With a separation of 4, the cut-off sits below the member mean, and the TPR is \(1 - \Phi(-0.91) = 0.8185\).

Example 10.3 This formula lets a population with two kinds of record show in closed form how much the per-record comparison matters (Table 10.1). A typical record is predicted confidently whether or not the model trained on it. An atypical record has a negative \(\phi\), which means it is misclassified, unless the model trained on it.

Now set three attacks to an FPR of \(10^{-3}\) pooled over both kinds.

One threshold on \(\phi\) is the loss attack. Typical records make up 95% of non-members and their OUT values are high, so the threshold must sit high. Solving for the point that 0.1% of all non-members exceed puts it at \(\phi = 6.075\). Atypical members score around 2 and almost never pass it. Only typical members occasionally do, and the pooled TPR is 0.0048, which happens to round to the typical kind’s best TPR alone.

One threshold on the z-score is offline LiRA. Each record is standardized against its own OUT distribution, so every record gets FPR \(10^{-3}\), at \(t \ge 3.0902\). Each kind then gets its own best TPR from the table, and the pooled TPR is their weighted average, \(0.95 \times 0.0048 + 0.05 \times 0.8185 = 0.0455\).

One threshold on \(\ln\Lambda\) is online LiRA. Solving for a pooled FPR of \(10^{-3}\) puts the threshold at 1.497. Since \(\ln\Lambda = st - s^2/2\), the cut \(\ln\Lambda \ge 1.497\) is the z-score cut \(t \ge (1.497 + s^2/2)/s\). That is \(t \ge 3.244\) for typical records, with an FPR of only \(5.90\times10^{-4}\). For atypical ones it is \(t \ge 2.374\), with FPR 0.0088. The pooled FPR is \(0.95 \times 5.90\times10^{-4} + 0.05 \times 0.0088 = 10^{-3}\), and the pooled TPR is 0.0503, the best possible at that FPR by Theorem 10.1.

The global threshold is ten times worse than either per-record test. The likelihood ratio beats the z-score because it spends its allowance of false positives where they pay. A typical record’s IN and OUT curves overlap, so admitting more of its false positives admits almost no extra members. An atypical record’s curves lie far apart, so a few more false positives there admit many members.

Example 10.4 Figure 10.2 shows the IN and OUT distributions of a typical point and an outlier of the running example. On the 128 target models, online LiRA reaches a TPR of 0.0149 at FPR \(10^{-3}\), against 0.0009 for the loss attack (Table 10.3).

With few shadow models, each record’s estimated standard deviations are noisy, and the noise hurts most at a low FPR. A record whose spread is underestimated has its OUT curve drawn too narrow, so an ordinary output looks far out in the tail and becomes a false positive at a strict threshold. Carlini et al. therefore recommend a global variance when shadow models are few: one IN variance and one OUT variance, each the average over all records (Figure 10.3, where the four variants are online or offline, with variances per record or global). With 16 shadow models, online LiRA with variances per record reaches 0.0023, barely above chance, while with a global variance it reaches 0.0151.

10.4 Evaluating attacks: TPR at a low FPR

Older papers report an attack’s accuracy, which is \(\frac12 + \frac12\mathrm{Adv}\) in the membership game, or its AUC, and both average over all members and non-members. This section asks which number says whether an attack is a threat. A privacy breach is not an average. It happens when an attacker can say with confidence that a particular person was in the data, and that needs a low FPR, where almost every “member” verdict is right.

Definition 10.4 (TPR at a low FPR) An attack’s TPR at FPR \(\alpha\) is the height of its ROC curve at \(\alpha\), the largest fraction of members it flags with a threshold that flags at most a fraction \(\alpha\) of non-members. Carlini et al. (2022) report it at a low \(\alpha\) such as \(10^{-3}\), together with the whole ROC curve on log axes.

Two attacks can have the same AUC and best advantages within 0.015 of each other, yet TPRs at FPR \(10^{-3}\) that differ by a factor of 38. In the pair below, attack A exposes a few members completely and learns nothing about anyone else, while attack B learns a little about everyone.

Proposition 10.3 (Same AUC, different exposure) Let \(r \in (0, 1)\) be a fraction of members. Attack A recognizes a fraction \(r\) of members with certainty, and gives every other record, member or not, a random score from one common distribution. Attack B gives members a score \(\mathcal N(\mu, 1)\) and non-members a score \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\), with \(\mu > 0\). Then A has AUC \(\frac12 + \frac r2\), best advantage \(r\) and \[\mathrm{TPR}_A(\alpha) = r + (1 - r)\alpha, \tag{10.11}\] while B has AUC \(\Phi(\mu/\sqrt2)\), best advantage \(2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1\), and the TPR of Equation 10.10 with \(s = \mu\). The two AUCs are equal when \(\Phi(\mu/\sqrt2) = \frac12 + \frac r2\).

Proof. The plan is to compute each attack’s AUC from its definition as a ranking probability, A’s TPR by splitting members into recognized and not, and B’s best advantage from the total variation distance.

  1. AUC of A. The AUC is the probability that a random member outscores a random non-member, with ties counted as one half (Proposition 4.2). With probability \(r\) the member is a recognized one, and it wins. Otherwise both scores have the same distribution, so the member wins with probability \(\frac12\). The total is \(r + (1 - r)\frac12 = \frac12 + \frac r2\).
  2. TPR of A. At a threshold with FPR \(\alpha\), the recognized members always pass. The other members have the same score distribution as non-members, so they pass with the same probability \(\alpha\), which gives \(\mathrm{TPR}_A(\alpha)\). Then \(\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR} = r(1 - \alpha)\), which is at most \(r\).
  3. AUC of B. A member outscores a non-member when the difference of their scores is positive. That difference is \(\mathcal N(\mu, 1)\) minus an independent \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\), so it is normal with mean \(\mu\) and variance 2, and it is positive with probability \(\Phi(\mu/\sqrt2)\).
  4. Advantage of B. The best advantage of a test between two distributions is their total variation distance, the most by which the two can differ in the probability of one set of scores (Theorem 4.5). For two unit-variance Gaussians at distance \(\mu\), the densities cross halfway, so the best set is the scores above \(\mu/2\). Its TPR is \(\Phi(\mu/2)\) and its FPR is \(1 - \Phi(\mu/2)\), and their difference is \(2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1\).

With \(r = 0.05\), both AUCs are 0.5250 when \(\mu = 0.0887\) (Table 10.2). At FPR \(10^{-3}\), A has TPR \(0.05 + 0.95 \times 10^{-3} = 0.05095\), while B has \(1 - \Phi(3.0902 - 0.0887) = 0.00134\), which is 38 times less.

Who is exposed matters more than how many. Attack A is a severe breach for one person in twenty, and B a negligible one for everyone. Only the low-FPR end of the ROC curve shows the difference, and only on log axes, since linear axes squeeze everything below FPR 0.01 into one pixel column (Figure 10.4).

Measuring a low FPR takes many non-members. An FPR is estimated by counting false positives, and a count of \(k\) events has a relative error of about \(1/\sqrt k\) (Hypothesis testing). To see \(k\) false positives at FPR \(\alpha\) you need about \(k/\alpha\) non-members, which is why the running example uses 64,000 non-member pairs. A low FPR is also what makes the verdicts reliable. With as many members as non-members, the precision, the fraction of “member” verdicts that are right, is \(\mathrm{TPR}/(\mathrm{TPR} + \mathrm{FPR})\), so a TPR of 0.05 at FPR \(10^{-3}\) gives \(0.05/0.051 = 0.98\).

Example 10.5 Table 10.3 scores every attack on the 128 target models, and Figure 10.5 draws their ROC curves on log axes. “Best advantage” is the largest TPR − FPR over all thresholds.

By AUC the attacks are close, 0.577 against about 0.63, but at FPR \(10^{-3}\) they are not. The loss and shadow attacks are at chance, since their TPRs of 0.0009 and 0.0008 are no higher than the FPR of 0.001. Online LiRA finds 952 of the 64,000 members, a TPR of 0.0149 and 17.0 times the loss attack.

The Clopper–Pearson interval, an exact 95% confidence interval for a proportion estimated from a count, is \([0.0140, 0.0158]\) for that TPR. The FPR rests on 64 false positives among 64,000 non-members, and its interval is \([0.00077, 0.00128]\). That is the \(1/\sqrt k\) rule at work, since 64 counts give a relative error of about an eighth, and the interval spans about two of those on each side. One caveat is that the threshold was chosen on the same data that measure the rates, as is usual in this literature, while a real attacker would set it on its shadow models.

10.5 Why outliers are most exposed

Example 10.3 showed that the low-FPR end of the ROC curve is made of the records with a large separation between their IN and OUT distributions. This section asks which records those are, and finds that they are the ones the model cannot predict without having seen them. For one record the best test is the two-Gaussian test of Equation 10.10, so the best TPR is set by the record’s own separation, whose numerator turns out to be a self-influence.

Proposition 10.4 (Exposure of one record) Let the IN and OUT distributions of \(\phi_z\) for a record \(z\) be Gaussian, with means \(\mu_{\mathrm{in}, z}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\) and a common standard deviation \(\sigma_z\). The best TPR of any test on \(z\) alone at FPR \(\alpha\) is \[\mathrm{TPR}_z(\alpha) = 1 - \Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha) - s_z\big), \qquad s_z = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{in}, z} - \mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}}{\sigma_z}, \tag{10.12}\] and the numerator \(\mu_{\mathrm{in}, z} - \mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\) is the self-influence of \(z\) measured in \(\phi\): the average amount by which training on \(z\) raises the model’s \(\phi\) on \(z\).

Proof.

  1. The test. Standardizing the output, as in Section 10.3, gives a standard normal under OUT and \(\mathcal N(s_z, 1)\) under IN. So Equation 10.10 with \(s = s_z\) is the best TPR.
  2. Self-influence. Definition 7.3 defines the self-influence of a training record \(z_i\) as \(\ell(\theta_{D^{(i)}}; z_i) - \ell(\theta_D; z_i)\). That is the loss of the model trained on \(D^{(i)}\), the training set with \(z_i\) replaced by a fresh record, minus the loss of the model trained on \(D\) itself. Measured with \(\phi\) instead of \(\ell\), the sign flips, because \(\phi\) rises where the loss falls.
  3. Average it. Take \(z_i = z\) and average over the rest of the training set, the fresh record and the training run. The models trained on \(D\) contain \(z\), so they give \(\phi_z\) the mean \(\mu_{\mathrm{in}, z}\). The models trained on \(D^{(i)}\) do not, so they give \(\mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\). The averaged self-influence in \(\phi\) is the difference of the two means.

Which records have a large self-influence? If the rest of the data already predict \(z\)’s label, \(\mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\) is high and training on \(z\) adds little. If they predict the opposite label, \(\mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\) is low, and the model must move toward \(z\) to fit it. The regularization limits how far the model can move. By Theorem 7.2, one record moves the weights of a regularized fit by at most \(2L/(\lambda n)\), where \(L\) bounds the length of one record’s loss gradient. For logistic regression that gradient is \(x\) times a probability, so its length is at most \(\|x\|\). A move of the weights changes the margin \(\phi_z = y\langle \theta, x_z\rangle\) by at most \(\|x_z\|\) times the length of the move. So a long \(x_z\) both pulls the weights harder and turns that pull into a larger change in its own margin, and the outliers of the running example have long features and unpredictable labels.

Leakage is concentrated. The loss attack’s advantage reflects the average self-influence, while the low-FPR end of the ROC curve is governed by the largest ones, because a strict threshold is passed only by the records with the largest separations. So a model with a small generalization gap can still expose a few records completely. Memorizing them can even be what makes it accurate: Feldman (2020) shows that on long-tailed data, a rare subpopulation seen once in training must be memorized for good test accuracy.

Removing the outliers does not fix it. Carlini et al. (2022b) removed the most exposed records and retrained, and the next layer of records became exposed, which they call the privacy onion effect. Exposure is relative to the rest of the dataset, because a record’s OUT distribution depends on what the other records predict about it, and Exercise 6 reproduces this on the running example. A guarantee that protects every record, the subject of Part 3, has to bound the self-influence of the worst record, not the average.

Example 10.6 Table 10.4 splits the running example by kind of point.

Outliers carry the gap. The mean loss self-influence over all points weights the two kinds by their shares, \(0.95 \times 0.0897 + 0.05 \times 2.0999 = 0.1902\). This is a gap in logistic loss on the shadow models, not the error gap of Example 10.1. The outliers’ part of that is \(0.05 \times 2.0999 \approx 0.105\), a share of 0.552 of the total, from 5% of the points.

Outliers are the true positives. At FPR \(10^{-3}\), online LiRA flags 26% of outlier members and 0.2% of typical members. Each target model has about 25 outlier members and 475 typical members, so it finds about 6.5 outliers for every 0.95 typical points, and over the 128 target models a share of 0.873 of the members it finds are outliers. As in Example 10.3, the likelihood ratio spends its false positives on them. Typical points get an FPR of 0.00086 and outliers 0.00375, whose weighted average is the pooled \(10^{-3}\).

Not every outlier is exposed. Figure 10.6 plots each point’s separation against \(\mu_{\mathrm{out}, z}\). About half the outliers have a coin-flip label that happens to agree with the trend, so they are predicted correctly without being seen and are no more exposed than typical points.

10.6 The full-size pipeline: LiRA on CIFAR-10

The usual full-scale experiment runs LiRA with 16 shadow models on an image classifier for CIFAR-10, a benchmark of small colour images in ten classes, and takes hours on a graphics card. This section states the protocol, which later chapters reuse for auditing, and what changes at full size. The running example is this protocol at small scale, with logistic regression in place of a ResNet, a deep convolutional network, so the section has no separate example.

Definition 10.5 (The LiRA evaluation protocol) The protocol has six steps.

  1. Data split. Take the 50,000 CIFAR-10 training images as the pool, and give each of the models, a target and the shadows, a random half of it. One model plays the target and the others the shadows, and rotating the target gives many pairs to evaluate.
  2. Training. Train every model with the target’s recipe, including its architecture (wide ResNets), optimizer, epochs and data augmentation, which is what gives the shadow pairs the target’s distribution (Proposition 10.2, step 1).
  3. Statistic. For each model and image, compute the network’s raw class scores \(f\) on the image and on a few augmented copies, such as horizontal flips and small shifts. Convert each to \(\phi\), computed as \(f_y\) minus the log of the sum of \(e^{f_{y'}}\) over the other classes, which never forms \(1 - p_y\), a number that rounds to 0 for a confident model.
  4. Fits. For each image, fit IN and OUT Gaussians over the shadow models, with one mean per augmented copy and variances per record or global.
  5. Scores. For each target model and image, compute the online score Equation 10.6 or the offline score Equation 10.7.
  6. Evaluation. Pool all pairs of target model and image, sweep one threshold, and plot the ROC curve on log axes. Report the TPR at FPR \(10^{-3}\), with the number of non-member pairs it rests on.

Step 3 is exact because the softmax makes the odds \(p_y/(1 - p_y)\) equal to \(e^{f_y}\) over the sum of the other classes’ \(e^{f_{y'}}\). With 16 shadow models, use a global variance, because each image is then out of only about 8 shadows, and Figure 10.3 shows that variances per record from so few values fail at the low-FPR end.

Carlini et al. report that online LiRA is about ten times more powerful than earlier attacks at a low FPR on CIFAR-10, and that the loss attack is close to chance there, which is what Table 10.3 shows in miniature. The auditing part of the book reuses this pipeline to measure privacy leakage. A privately trained model takes the place of the target, and inserted canaries, records such as mislabelled images chosen to be as exposed as possible, play the members.
