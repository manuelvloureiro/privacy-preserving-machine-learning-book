Appendix J — Mechanisms and their limits

Author

Manuel V. Loureiro

Published

Version 2026-10-30

This appendix proves eight results that the chapters on differential privacy (DP) state about specific mechanisms but do not derive in full. Three are about what the slack \(\delta\) of approximate DP means. Every \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP pair of output distributions splits into an \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair and a leftover of mass \(\delta\), so it is a post-processing of the worst \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP pair, four-point randomized response. The same split shows that \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP implies an approximate form of zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP). And the stability-based histogram shows what the slack buys, an error that does not grow with the number of bins. The other five results pin down single mechanisms: a lower bound for estimating means of heavy-tailed data, three privacy proofs for selection (the sparse vector technique with several answers above the threshold, Gumbel top-\(c\) selection, and a direct proof for the Gumbel noisy max), and a check that the “alarm” mechanism of the group-privacy chapter really is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP. Each proof fixes a neighbouring pair, writes down the two output distributions, and compares them directly. Table J.1 maps each derivation to the chapter section that states or uses it.

J.1 Every approximate-DP pair is post-processed four-point randomized response

J.1.1 What the chapter uses

Fix two neighbouring datasets \(D\) and \(D'\) and write \(P\) and \(Q\) for the distributions of an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP mechanism’s output on them, with probabilities \(p(y)\) and \(q(y)\) at an output \(y\). (The last part of this section extends the argument to densities.) Approximate DP says that the hockey-stick divergence of Equation 18.13, the mass of one distribution that sticks out above \(e^\varepsilon\) times the other, is at most \(\delta\) in both directions: \[E_{e^\varepsilon}(P\|Q) = \sum_y\big(p(y) - e^\varepsilon q(y)\big)_+ \le \delta, \qquad E_{e^\varepsilon}(Q\|P) = \sum_y\big(q(y) - e^\varepsilon p(y)\big)_+ \le \delta, \tag{J.1}\] where the positive part \((x)_+\) equals \(x\) when \(x\) is positive and 0 otherwise. The four-point pair \((P^\star, Q^\star)\) of Table 21.3 has four outputs: “flag \(D\)”, 0, 1 and “flag \(D'\)”. With probability \(\delta\) it announces the dataset. Otherwise it runs randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\), which reports 0 with probability \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\) on \(D\) and \(1 - t\) on \(D'\), and 1 otherwise. So \(P^\star\) gives the four outputs the probabilities \(\delta\), \((1 - \delta)t\), \((1 - \delta)(1 - t)\) and 0, and \(Q^\star\) gives them 0, \((1 - \delta)(1 - t)\), \((1 - \delta)t\) and \(\delta\). A channel \(K\) is a table of probabilities \(K(y \mid z)\) that turns an input \(z\) into an output \(y\), and some channel produces the pair \((P, Q)\) from the four-point pair: \[p(y) = \sum_z P^\star(z)K(y \mid z), \qquad q(y) = \sum_z Q^\star(z)K(y \mid z) \qquad\text{for every output } y. \tag{J.2}\] The composition chapter derives the channel for \(\delta = 0\) (Equation 21.12) and cites Kairouz, Oh & Viswanath (2015) for \(\delta > 0\), on which optimal composition Equation 21.15 and the composition theorems of Section 21.5 with \(\delta > 0\) rest.

J.1.2 The idea

Split each distribution into a pure part and a leftover of mass exactly \(\delta\), so that the two pure parts form an \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair. The chapter’s \(\delta = 0\) channel then produces the pure parts from plain randomized response, and the flags produce the leftovers. A small case shows every step. Take three outputs with \(P = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1)\), \(Q = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)\) and \(e^\varepsilon = 2\). The mass of \(P\) above \(2Q\) is \(0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2\), and the mass of \(Q\) above \(2P\) is \(0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3\), so the pair is \((\ln 2, 0.3)\)-DP. Table J.2 follows the construction.

The first guesses \(m_P\) and \(m_Q\) for the pure parts have different masses, \(A = 0.8\) and \(B = 0.7\), while the four-point pair needs both to have mass \(1 - \delta = 0.7\). So step 4 below mixes the heavier one, \(m_P\), with \(m_Q/2\), with weight \(\omega^* = \frac29\), which keeps the factor 2 and lowers the mass to \(B\). On output 1, for example, \(\hat m_P = \frac79 \times 0.4 + \frac29 \times 0.1 = \frac13\). The ratios \(\hat m_P/\hat m_Q\) are \(\frac53\), \(\frac89\) and \(\frac12\), all within a factor 2, and what is left over has mass \(0.3\) on each side.

J.1.3 Derivation

Assume \(0 < \delta < 1\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\), and write \(\gamma = e^\varepsilon\) to keep the formulas short.

  1. Pure parts. Define, for every output \(y\), \[m_P(y) = \min\{p(y), \gamma q(y)\}, \qquad m_Q(y) = \min\{q(y), \gamma p(y)\}. \tag{J.3}\] Each is non-negative and at most its own distribution, \(m_P \le p\) and \(m_Q \le q\).
  2. They are within a factor \(\gamma\). The claim is \(m_P \le \gamma m_Q\) at every output. The minimum \(m_P\) is at most \(\gamma q\) by definition. It is also at most \(p\), which is at most \(\gamma^2 p\) because \(\gamma \ge 1\). So \(m_P\) is at most both \(\gamma q\) and \(\gamma \cdot \gamma p\), and hence at most \(\gamma\) times the smaller one, which is \(\gamma m_Q = \min\{\gamma q, \gamma \cdot \gamma p\}\). Swapping the roles gives \(m_Q \le \gamma m_P\).
  3. Their masses. At each output, \(p - \min\{p, \gamma q\} = (p - \gamma q)_+\): either the minimum is \(p\) and both sides are 0, or the minimum is \(\gamma q < p\) and both sides are \(p - \gamma q\). Summing over outputs, the mass of \(m_P\) is \[A = \sum_y m_P(y) = 1 - E_\gamma(P\|Q) \ge 1 - \delta, \tag{J.4}\] and in the same way the mass of \(m_Q\) is \(B = 1 - E_\gamma(Q\|P) \ge 1 - \delta\).
  4. Move the heavier part down. Say \(A \ge B\), or else swap the names \(P\) and \(Q\). For a weight \(\omega\) between 0 and 1, mix \(m_P\) with the scaled-down \(\gamma^{-1}m_Q\): \[m_P^\omega = (1 - \omega)\,m_P + \omega\,\gamma^{-1}m_Q. \tag{J.5}\] A mix of two numbers lies between them, so three properties hold for every \(\omega\), because each holds for both pieces. First, the mix fits inside \(p\): \(m_P \le p\) by step 1, and \(\gamma^{-1}m_Q \le \gamma^{-1}\gamma p = p\) because \(m_Q \le \gamma p\). Second, it is at most \(\gamma m_Q\): \(m_P \le \gamma m_Q\) by step 2, and \(\gamma^{-1}m_Q \le \gamma m_Q\) because \(\gamma \ge 1\). Third, it is at least \(\gamma^{-1}m_Q\), which is the reverse bound \(m_Q \le \gamma m_P^\omega\): step 2 gives \(m_P \ge \gamma^{-1}m_Q\), and the second piece equals \(\gamma^{-1}m_Q\). The mass of the mix is \((1 - \omega)A + \omega\gamma^{-1}B\). It falls in a straight line from \(A\) at \(\omega = 0\) to \(\gamma^{-1}B\) at \(\omega = 1\), and \(\gamma^{-1}B \le B \le A\). So it passes through \(B\), at \[\omega^* = \frac{A - B}{A - \gamma^{-1}B} \tag{J.6}\] (and \(\omega^* = 0\) when \(A = B\)). Now \(m_P^{\omega^*}\) and \(m_Q\) both have mass \(B\).
  5. Scale both to mass \(1 - \delta\). Multiply both by \((1 - \delta)/B\), which is at most 1 by step 3: \[\hat m_P = \frac{1 - \delta}{B}\,m_P^{\omega^*}, \qquad \hat m_Q = \frac{1 - \delta}{B}\,m_Q. \tag{J.7}\] Scaling both by the same factor keeps the ratio bounds of step 4, and scaling down keeps \(\hat m_P \le p\) and \(\hat m_Q \le q\). Dividing by \(1 - \delta\) then gives two distributions, \[\bar P = \frac{\hat m_P}{1 - \delta}, \qquad \bar Q = \frac{\hat m_Q}{1 - \delta}, \tag{J.8}\] with \(\bar P(y) \le \gamma\bar Q(y)\) and \(\bar Q(y) \le \gamma\bar P(y)\) at every output. So \((\bar P, \bar Q)\) is an \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair.
  6. The leftovers are distributions. Define the leftovers \(R_D = (p - \hat m_P)/\delta\) and \(R_{D'} = (q - \hat m_Q)/\delta\). Each is non-negative because of step 5, and each sums to \((1 - (1 - \delta))/\delta = 1\), so each is a distribution. Rearranging the definitions gives the split \[P = (1 - \delta)\bar P + \delta R_D, \qquad Q = (1 - \delta)\bar Q + \delta R_{D'}. \tag{J.9}\]
  7. The channel. The channel sends “flag \(D\)” to \(R_D\) and “flag \(D'\)” to \(R_{D'}\). It sends the reports 0 and 1 to the two rows of the \(\delta = 0\) channel Equation 21.12, built for the pure pair \((\bar P, \bar Q)\): \[K(y \mid 0) = \frac{\gamma\bar P(y) - \bar Q(y)}{\gamma - 1}, \qquad K(y \mid 1) = \frac{\gamma\bar Q(y) - \bar P(y)}{\gamma - 1}. \tag{J.10}\] The composition chapter shows that these rows are distributions when the pair is \(\varepsilon\)-DP: each entry is non-negative because no output is more than \(\gamma\) times likelier under one side, and each row sums to \((\gamma - 1)/(\gamma - 1) = 1\). It also shows that mixing the rows with weights \(t\) and \(1 - t\) gives \(\bar P\), and with weights \(1 - t\) and \(t\) gives \(\bar Q\).
  8. Check Equation J.2. Feed \(P^\star\) through \(K\), one input at a time. The input “flag \(D\)” has probability \(\delta\), and the channel sends it to \(R_D\). The reports 0 and 1 have probabilities \((1 - \delta)t\) and \((1 - \delta)(1 - t)\), so by step 7 their rows mix to \((1 - \delta)\bar P\). The input “flag \(D'\)” has probability 0 under \(P^\star\) and adds nothing. So the total is \(\delta R_D + (1 - \delta)\bar P\), which is \(P\) by Equation J.9. The same steps with \(Q^\star\), whose report 1 has probability \((1 - \delta)t\), give \((1 - \delta)\bar Q + \delta R_{D'} = Q\).

J.1.4 Checking it

On the small example of Table J.2, the script finds \(\omega^* = 0.2222\) and the channel rows \(K(\cdot \mid 0) = (0.6667, 0.3333, 0)\) and \(K(\cdot \mid 1) = (0.0952, 0.4762, 0.4286)\), and feeding \(P^\star\) and \(Q^\star\) through them returns \(P\) and \(Q\). It then draws 2,000 random pairs for each of three settings, 3 outputs at \(\varepsilon = 0.5\), 8 at \(\varepsilon = 1\) and 20 at \(\varepsilon = 2\), each with its own exact \(\delta\). A quarter of the pairs have an output that only one side can produce, whose infinite privacy loss sends all of its mass into the leftover. Over all 6,000 pairs, \(P^\star K\) and \(Q^\star K\) differ from \(P\) and \(Q\) by at most \(2.2\times10^{-16}\), and every ratio bound of step 5 holds to within rounding.

Figure J.1 shows the split on a pair of normal distributions, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1 against mean 1 and standard deviation 0.8, each reported in eight bins. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) the two hockey-stick divergences are \(0.2259\) and \(0.0844\), so \(\delta = 0.2259\). The leftover of \(P\) sits in the left bins, where \(p\) exceeds \(e^\varepsilon q\), and the leftover of \(Q\) on the right. Figure J.2 shows the same pair as hypothesis tests: its trade-off curve lies above the boundary \(f_{\varepsilon,\delta}\) of Equation 18.18, which is the curve of the four-point pair, and its pure part lies above the boundary \(f_{\varepsilon,0}\) of plain randomized response.

The result is exact, so there is no slack to measure. Other splits work too, but none can use a \(\delta\) below the pair’s own, because a leftover smaller than the mass sticking out above \(\gamma\) times the other side would leave some of that mass in the pure part.

J.1.5 What it does not cover

The proof needs \(\delta > 0\) to divide by in step 6, and the case \(\delta = 0\) is the chapter’s own derivation. It also needs \(\varepsilon > 0\) to divide by \(\gamma - 1\) in step 7. With \(\varepsilon = 0\) the pure parts are equal, \(\bar P = \bar Q\), randomized response reports 0 with probability \(\frac12\) on both sides, and both rows of the channel are simply \(\bar P\). For continuous outputs, replace the probabilities by densities with respect to a common reference distribution, for example \((P + Q)/2\), with respect to which both densities lie between 0 and 2. Every step works output by output, so it goes through with sums replaced by integrals.

J.2 Approximate DP implies approximate zCDP

J.2.1 What the chapter uses

A mechanism \(M\) is \(\delta\)-approximately \(\rho\)-zCDP if every pair of neighbours \(D \sim D'\) has an event \(E\) for the run on \(D\) and an event \(E'\) for the run on \(D'\), each of probability at least \(1 - \delta\), such that for every order \(\alpha > 1\) the Rényi divergences of the conditioned outputs lie under the line \(\rho\alpha\) of Equation 31.1: \[D_\alpha\big(M(D)|_E\,\big\|\,M(D')|_{E'}\big) \le \rho\alpha \qquad\text{and}\qquad D_\alpha\big(M(D')|_{E'}\,\big\|\,M(D)|_E\big) \le \rho\alpha. \tag{J.11}\] Here \(M(D)|_E\) is the output distribution conditioned on \(E\), and the events may depend on how the run produced its output. Section 31.5 states, citing Bun & Steinke (2016, Proposition 1.13), that an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP mechanism is \(\delta\)-approximately \((\varepsilon^2/2)\)-zCDP, and that two approximately zCDP steps compose with slack \(\delta_1 + \delta_2 - \delta_1\delta_2\) (their Lemma 8.2). It uses both in an example pipeline that mixes stability-based steps with Gaussian steps.

J.2.2 The idea

The split Equation J.9 of the previous section says that you can produce \(M(D)\) in two stages. First flip a coin that says “pure” with probability \(1 - \delta\), then draw the output from the pure part \(\bar P\) if it does and from the leftover \(R_D\) otherwise. The output has exactly the distribution \((1 - \delta)\bar P + \delta R_D = P\). Conditioned on the coin saying “pure”, the outputs on \(D\) and \(D'\) form the \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair \((\bar P, \bar Q)\), which the chapter has already shown to be \((\varepsilon^2/2)\)-zCDP. These events are about how the output was produced, not only about the output, which is enough for the chapter, because its conversion back to \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP uses \(E\) only through its probability and the conditional distribution.

J.2.3 Derivation

  1. Split the pair. Fix neighbours \(D \sim D'\), with output distributions \(P\) and \(Q\). Because the mechanism is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, both hockey-stick divergences are at most \(\delta\). For \(0 < \delta < 1\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\), the previous section then gives Equation J.9, with \((\bar P, \bar Q)\) an \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair. If \(\delta = 0\), take \(\bar P = P\) and \(\bar Q = Q\). The previous section’s last part covers \(\varepsilon = 0\), and \(\delta = 1\) makes the claim empty.
  2. Realize the mechanism in two stages. Run \(M(D)\) by drawing a coin \(C\) that equals “pure” with probability \(1 - \delta\), then an output from \(\bar P\) if \(C\) is “pure” and from \(R_D\) otherwise. The output has distribution \((1 - \delta)\bar P + \delta R_D = P\). Run \(M(D')\) the same way with \(\bar Q\) and \(R_{D'}\).
  3. Pick the events. Let \(E\) be “\(C\) is pure” in the run on \(D\), and \(E'\) the same in the run on \(D'\). Each has probability exactly \(1 - \delta\). Conditioned on \(E\) the output is drawn from \(\bar P\), and conditioned on \(E'\) from \(\bar Q\).
  4. Apply the pure-DP bound. The pair \((\bar P, \bar Q)\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP, so by Equation 31.6 of Section 31.2, \[D_\alpha(\bar P\|\bar Q) \le \frac{\alpha\varepsilon^2}{2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad D_\alpha(\bar Q\|\bar P) \le \frac{\alpha\varepsilon^2}{2} \qquad\text{for every } \alpha > 1. \tag{J.12}\] That chapter proves it with Hoeffding’s lemma: the privacy loss of an \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair lies in \([-\varepsilon, \varepsilon]\), and a Rényi divergence is the logarithm of an exponential moment of the privacy loss, which Hoeffding’s lemma bounds by a normal one. Steps 3 and 4 together are the definition with \(\rho = \varepsilon^2/2\). The sharper Equation 31.10 gives \(\rho = \varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\) the same way.

Composition, in the case the chapter uses. The pipeline of Section 31.5 mixes steps that are \((\varepsilon_i, \delta_i)\)-DP with steps that are \(\rho_i\)-zCDP, each chosen after seeing the earlier outputs. For a zCDP step take \(\delta_i = 0\) and the coin always “pure”. For an \((\varepsilon_i, \delta_i)\)-DP step take \(\rho_i = \varepsilon_i^2/2\) and the two-stage run of step 2, built for the mechanism that the earlier outputs (the history) selected.

  1. The coins ignore the history. Whatever the earlier outputs were, step \(i\)’s coin says “pure” with probability exactly \(1 - \delta_i\). So the event “every coin says pure” has probability \(\prod_i(1 - \delta_i) = 1 - \delta_a\), which defines the slack \(\delta_a\).
  2. Condition on all coins. The probability of a sequence of outputs together with “every coin says pure” is a product over steps of \((1 - \delta_i)\) times the pure distribution of step \(i\) given the history. Dividing by \(\prod_i(1 - \delta_i)\) leaves the product of the pure distributions. So conditioned on all coins being pure, the run is an adaptive composition of the pure parts, on \(D\) and on \(D'\) alike.
  3. Add the slopes. Each pure part is \(\rho_i\)-zCDP for every history. Rényi divergences of an adaptive composition add, even when each step is chosen from the earlier outputs (Rényi DP; Theorem 31.1), so the conditioned run is \((\sum_i\rho_i)\)-zCDP. So the whole pipeline is \(\delta_a\)-approximately \((\sum_i\rho_i)\)-zCDP, with \(\delta_a = 1 - \prod_i(1 - \delta_i)\), which is \(\delta_1 + \delta_2 - \delta_1\delta_2\) for two steps.

J.2.4 Checking it

The script computes the Rényi divergences of the pure parts from the previous section on a grid of orders from 1.001 to 20.9, in both directions. For the three-output example, with \(\varepsilon = \ln 2\) and \(\rho = \varepsilon^2/2 = 0.2402\), the largest value of \(D_\alpha/(\alpha\rho)\) is \(0.4275\). For the binned normal pair, with \(\rho = 0.5\), it is \(0.4306\). Both are well under 1, the most the bound allows. Part of the slack is that the Rényi divergence of an \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair never exceeds \(\varepsilon\), so its curve flattens while the line \(\alpha\varepsilon^2/2\) keeps rising. The rest is that these pure parts are milder than randomized response, the worst \(\varepsilon\)-DP pair.

J.2.5 What it does not cover

The events here depend on the coin that chooses between the pure part and the leftover, not only on the output. If an event had to be a set of outputs, the argument would not apply as it stands, because the leftover and the pure part can share outputs. The composition argument needs each good event to have probability exactly \(1 - \delta_i\) given any history, which the two-stage run provides. Bun & Steinke’s Lemma 8.2 covers general approximately-zCDP steps, whose good events only have probability at least \(1 - \delta_i\) and may depend on the history, and that general case stays cited. The pipelines of the chapter use only the special case.

J.3 The stability-based histogram

J.3.1 What the chapter uses

A histogram over a universe of \(N\) bins releases a count for each bin. Under pure DP, the packing argument of Section 27.1 shows that the \(\ell_\infty\) error, the largest error over the bins, must grow like \(\ln N/\varepsilon\) (Equation 27.5). The stability-based histogram of Vadhan (2017), with parameters \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\), avoids this under approximate DP. It works on a dataset of \(n\) records, each in one bin, with \(c_x\) records in bin \(x\), and compares noisy counts with the threshold \[\tau = 1 + \frac{\ln\big(1/(2\delta)\big)}{\varepsilon}. \tag{J.13}\] For every bin with \(c_x \ge 1\), it draws a fresh Laplace noise \(Z_x \sim \mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) and releases the pair \((x, c_x + Z_x)\) if the noisy count exceeds \(\tau\). It releases nothing else, and a bin that is not released is read as 0. Under add/remove neighbours it is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP. And if \(k\) bins are non-empty, then for a failure probability \(\beta\), with probability at least \(1 - \beta\) every bin’s estimate is within \[\tau + \frac{\ln(k/\beta)}{\varepsilon} = 1 + \frac{\ln\big(1/(2\delta)\big) + \ln(k/\beta)}{\varepsilon} \tag{J.14}\] of its true count. Since \(k \le n\), this is of order \((\ln(1/\delta) + \ln(n/\beta))/\varepsilon\), with no \(N\) in it. Section 27.1 states this without proof, and Section 28.3 uses the same mechanism to locate the data before a mean is estimated.

J.3.2 The idea

Adding one record changes one bin, from count \(c\) to \(c + 1\). If the bin already had records, both datasets put Laplace noise on it, which is the ordinary Laplace mechanism on a count. If the bin was empty, the smaller dataset never looks at it, while the larger one releases it only if a count of 1 plus noise clears \(\tau\), which the threshold makes happen with probability exactly \(\delta\). That is the argument of Section 18.8 for the mechanism that releases each record with probability \(\delta\). For accuracy, only the \(k\) non-empty bins have any noise at all, so the union bound runs over \(k\) bins, not over \(N\).

J.3.3 Derivation

Privacy. Fix a dataset \(D\) and add one record, in bin \(x\), to get \(D'\).

  1. Only bin \(x\) differs. Every other bin has the same count in \(D\) and \(D'\). Its noise is drawn the same way, independently of bin \(x\), so its part of the output has the same distribution on both sides.
  2. Bin \(x\) was non-empty. Then \(c_x(D) \ge 1\), and both runs add \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) to a count that changes by 1. That is the Laplace mechanism with sensitivity 1, which is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Comparing the noisy count with \(\tau\) is post-processing, so bin \(x\)’s part of the output is \(\varepsilon\)-DP.
  3. Bin \(x\) was empty. On \(D\) nothing about bin \(x\) is ever printed, while on \(D'\) bin \(x\) has count 1 and is printed exactly when \(1 + Z_x > \tau\). A \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) variable exceeds a level \(s \ge 0\) with probability \(\frac12e^{-\varepsilon s}\). Here the level is \(\tau - 1\), which is \(\ln(1/(2\delta))/\varepsilon\) by Equation J.13, so bin \(x\) is printed with probability \[\Pr[Z_x > \tau - 1] = \tfrac12e^{-\ln(1/(2\delta))} = \delta. \tag{J.15}\]
  4. Couple the two runs in the empty case. Run the mechanism on \(D'\) by first running it on \(D\) with the same noise, then drawing \(Z_x\) for the new bin. The two outputs differ only if bin \(x\) is printed, which has probability \(\delta\). So for any set \(S\) of outputs, \(\Pr[M(D') \in S] \le \Pr[M(D) \in S] + \delta\). In the other direction, the outputs agree whenever bin \(x\) is not printed. That depends only on \(Z_x\), which is independent of everything else, so the outputs agree with probability \(1 - \delta\) whatever \(M(D)\) printed, and \(\Pr[M(D') \in S] \ge (1 - \delta)\Pr[M(D) \in S] \ge \Pr[M(D) \in S] - \delta\). Both directions are \((0, \delta)\)-DP.
  5. Put the bins back together. Step 4 already compares whole outputs, but step 2 compares only bin \(x\)’s part. To finish step 2, fix the output \(w\) of all the other bins. For each \(w\), bin \(x\)’s part satisfies the \(\varepsilon\)-DP inequality. The other bins’ output \(w\) has the same distribution on \(D\) and \(D'\), so averaging the inequality over \(w\) with these common weights keeps it. Either way the pair satisfies \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, in both orders.

Accuracy. Let the \(k\) non-empty bins have noises \(Z_x\).

  1. Error of one bin. An empty bin is never printed and its estimate 0 is exact. A printed bin has error \(|Z_x|\). A non-empty bin that is not printed has \(c_x + Z_x \le \tau\), so its error, the count \(c_x\) itself, is at most \(\tau - Z_x \le \tau + |Z_x|\). Either way the error is at most \(\tau + |Z_x|\).
  2. All \(k\) noises at once. A \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) variable exceeds \(s\) in absolute value with probability \(e^{-\varepsilon s}\). By the union bound, the chance that at least one of \(k\) events happens is at most the sum of their chances, so \[\Pr\Big[\max_x|Z_x| > s\Big] \le k e^{-\varepsilon s}. \tag{J.16}\] The right side equals \(\beta\) at \(s = \ln(k/\beta)/\varepsilon\). With step 6, the \(\ell_\infty\) error is at most Equation J.14 with probability at least \(1 - \beta\).

J.3.4 Checking it

Take \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\delta = 10^{-6}\), so \(\tau = 1 + \ln(5\times10^5) = 14.1224\). Figure J.3 shows the probability that a bin with true count \(c\) is printed. Below \(\tau\) it falls by a factor \(e^{\varepsilon}\) for each record removed, and at \(c = 1\) it is exactly \(10^{-6}\), which is the whole \(\delta\) the mechanism spends.

For \(k = 10\) non-empty bins and \(\beta = 0.05\), the bound Equation J.14 is \(14.1224 + \ln 200 = 19.4207\). The exact 95% quantile of the error, at the worst common count of the ten bins, is 18, and a simulation of 200,000 runs gives the same 18. So the bound is loose by about 1.4 records, partly because a bin is suppressed only when its noise is negative, so only one Laplace tail matters, and partly because counts are whole numbers. Figure J.4 compares the error with pure DP as the universe grows. The exact 95% error of the Laplace histogram, which adds noise to all \(N\) bins, is \(-\ln(1 - 0.95^{1/N})/\varepsilon\), which is \(16.79\) at \(N = 10^6\) and \(30.60\) at \(N = 10^{12}\). The stability-based histogram stays at \(19.42\) for every \(N\), so it beats the Laplace histogram from \(N = 1.394\times10^7\), and the packing lower bound Equation 27.5 from \(N = 2.114\times10^{17}\).

J.3.5 What it does not cover

The proof is for add/remove neighbours, the relation of the lower-bounds chapter. Under replace neighbours, as in Section 28.3, one record leaves one bin and joins another. Each of the two bins behaves as in step 2 or step 4, independently, and basic composition gives \((2\varepsilon, 2\delta)\)-DP, so run the mechanism with \(\varepsilon/2\) and \(\delta/2\). The threshold Equation J.13 is the smallest that makes step 3 cost exactly \(\delta\). A larger threshold is also private, at the price of a larger error.

J.4 A lower bound for heavy-tailed means

J.4.1 What the chapter uses

Fix a whole number \(k \ge 2\) and a bound \(m_k\) on the \(k\)-th central moment of the data, so that a record \(X\) with mean \(\mu\) has \(\mathbb E|X - \mu|^k \le m_k\). For the variance, \(k = 2\) and \(m_2 = \sigma^2\). Suppose an \(\varepsilon\)-DP mechanism \(M\) takes \(n\) records from any such distribution and returns an estimate within less than \(a\) of \(\mu\) with probability at least \(1 - \beta\), over the data and the noise, and that \(a \le m_k^{1/k}/2\). Then \[n \ge \frac{\ln\big((1 - \beta)/\beta\big)}{\ln\big(1 + p(e^\varepsilon - 1)\big)} \ge \frac{\ln\big((1 - \beta)/\beta\big)}{p(e^\varepsilon - 1)}, \qquad p = \frac{(2a)^{k/(k-1)}}{m_k^{1/(k-1)}}. \tag{J.17}\] For the variance the simpler bound reads \[n \ge \frac{\sigma^2\ln\big((1 - \beta)/\beta\big)}{4a^2(e^\varepsilon - 1)}. \tag{J.18}\] For small \(\varepsilon\), \(e^\varepsilon - 1\) is close to \(\varepsilon\), so this is of order \(\sigma^2/(a^2\varepsilon)\), the rate that the clipped mean of Section 28.5 reaches by Equation 28.15. For general \(k\) it is of order \(1/(\varepsilon a^{k/(k-1)})\). The chapter cites Kamath, Singhal & Ullman (2020) for both claims, and this section proves them for pure DP.

J.4.2 The idea

Build two distributions that a good estimator must tell apart but that DP says it cannot. The first, \(P_0\), puts all its mass at 0. The second, \(P_1\), moves a small probability \(p\) to a far point \(v\), so that its mean is \(pv = 2a\) while its moment stays within the bound. A sample from \(P_1\) is all zeros except for about \(np\) records equal to \(v\), so it is only about \(np\) replacements away from the all-zeros sample of \(P_0\), and group privacy forces the two outputs to look alike unless \(n\) is at least about \(1/(p\varepsilon)\). Figure J.5 draws the two distributions for \(k = 2\), \(\sigma = 1\) and \(a = 0.15\).

J.4.3 Derivation

  1. The far point. Let \(P_1\) put probability \(p\) on a value \(v > 0\) and \(1 - p\) on 0, with \(pv = 2a\) and \(pv^k = m_k\). (Step 2 shows that \(pv^k\) bounds the central moment.) Dividing the second equation by the first gives \(v^{k-1} = m_k/(2a)\), so \[v = \Big(\frac{m_k}{2a}\Big)^{1/(k-1)}, \qquad p = \frac{2a}{v} = \frac{(2a)^{k/(k-1)}}{m_k^{1/(k-1)}}. \tag{J.19}\] For \(p\) to be a probability you need \(v \ge 2a\), which is \(m_k \ge (2a)^k\), the condition \(a \le m_k^{1/k}/2\). For \(k = 2\) this is \(v = \sigma^2/(2a)\) and \(p = 4a^2/\sigma^2\).
  2. \(P_1\) meets the moment bound. Its mean is \(\mu = pv\). The value 0 is at distance \(pv\) from it and the value \(v\) at distance \((1 - p)v\), so \[\mathbb E|X - \mu|^k = (1 - p)(pv)^k + p\big((1 - p)v\big)^k = p(1 - p)v^k\big(p^{k-1} + (1 - p)^{k-1}\big). \tag{J.20}\] Both \(p\) and \(1 - p\) lie in \([0, 1]\), and raising such a number to the power \(k - 1 \ge 1\) cannot increase it. So the last bracket is at most \(p + (1 - p) = 1\), and the moment is at most \(p(1 - p)v^k \le pv^k = m_k\). \(P_0\) has moment 0.
  3. The datasets. A sample of \(n\) records from \(P_0\) is the all-zeros dataset \(D_0\). A sample \(D_1\) from \(P_1\) has some number \(J\) of records equal to \(v\), where \(J\) follows a binomial distribution with \(n\) trials and success probability \(p\). You get \(D_1\) from \(D_0\) by replacing \(J\) records, so the two are at distance \(J\).
  4. The test. Let \(S\) be the event “the estimate is at least \(a\)”. On \(P_0\) the mean is 0, and an estimate within less than \(a\) of it is below \(a\), so accuracy on \(P_0\) gives \(\Pr[M(D_0) \in S] \le \beta\). On \(P_1\) the mean is \(2a\), and an estimate within less than \(a\) of it is above \(a\), so accuracy on \(P_1\), averaged over the random dataset \(D_1\), gives \(\mathbb E\Pr[M(D_1) \in S] \ge 1 - \beta\).
  5. Group privacy. For pure DP, two datasets at distance \(J\) satisfy \(\Pr[M(D_1) \in S] \le e^{J\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D_0) \in S]\) (Equation 19.5 with \(\delta = 0\)), because the privacy loss adds up along the path of \(J\) single replacements, at most \(\varepsilon\) per step. With step 4, \(\Pr[M(D_1) \in S] \le e^{J\varepsilon}\beta\) for every possible \(D_1\).
  6. Average over the datasets. Combining steps 4 and 5 gives \(1 - \beta \le \beta\,\mathbb E[e^{\varepsilon J}]\). Each of the \(n\) records independently contributes a factor \(e^\varepsilon\) with probability \(p\) and 1 otherwise, so the average of \(e^{\varepsilon J}\) is \((1 - p + pe^\varepsilon)^n\). Hence \[1 - \beta \le \beta\big(1 + p(e^\varepsilon - 1)\big)^n. \tag{J.21}\]
  7. Solve for \(n\). Take logarithms and divide by \(\ln(1 + p(e^\varepsilon - 1))\) to get the first bound of Equation J.17. Since \(\ln(1 + x) \le x\), the denominator is at most \(p(e^\varepsilon - 1)\), which gives the second, simpler bound.

J.4.4 Checking it

Take \(\sigma^2 = 1\), \(\varepsilon = 1\), \(a = 0.05\) and \(\beta = 0.05\). Step 1 gives \(v = 10\) and \(p = 0.01\), and the variance of \(P_1\) is \(0.99\), within the bound. Equation J.17 then says that every \(\varepsilon\)-DP estimator needs at least \(172.8\) records, or \(171.4\) by the simpler form. The chapter’s clipped mean reaches a root-mean-square error of \(0.05\) with \(7{,}616\) records, by Equation 28.15. The two use different measures of error and the lower bound’s constants are loose, so the factor of 44 between them says little, and what matters is the rate. Figure J.6 plots the bounds against \(a\) on log–log axes. For \(k = 2\) the lower bound and the chapter’s estimator both have slope \(-2\), so they differ by a constant factor at every \(a\). A larger \(k\) gives a smaller slope, \(-k/(k - 1)\), which approaches \(-1\), the slope of bounded data. The script also checks step 2 on a grid of \(a\) for \(k = 2, 3, 4, 6\), and the \(k\)-th central moment of \(P_1\) never exceeds 1.

J.4.5 What it does not cover

The proof needs pure DP. Step 5 multiplies by \(e^{J\varepsilon}\), and under approximate DP group privacy adds a slack that grows like \(e^{J\varepsilon}\delta\), which ruins the averaging in step 6 once \(J\) is large. Lower bounds under approximate DP need different tools, for which Kamath, Singhal & Ullman (2020) is the place to start. The bound is also only the privacy part of the cost. The sampling part, about \(\sigma^2/a^2\) records, comes from the usual non-private lower bounds.

J.5 Sparse vector with \(c\) answers above

J.5.1 What the chapter uses

Theorem 22.6 in Section 22.6 proves that AboveThreshold, which halts at the first query above a noisy threshold, is \((\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)\)-DP, where \(\varepsilon_1\) is the budget spent on the threshold’s noise and \(\varepsilon_2\) the budget spent on the queries’ noise. To find \(c\) queries above the threshold, the chapter offers query noise that grows with \(c\) against one threshold (Lyu, Su & Li 2017, Algorithm 1). It takes a dataset \(D\), queries \(f_1, f_2, \dots\) of sensitivity \(\Delta\) that may be chosen adaptively, a threshold \(\tau\) and budgets \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 > 0\):

  1. Draw the threshold noise \(\xi \sim \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon_1)\) once.
  2. For each query \(i\), draw fresh noise \(\nu_i \sim \mathrm{Lap}(2c\Delta/\varepsilon_2)\). If \(f_i(D) + \nu_i \ge \tau + \xi\), output \(\top\), and halt if this is the \(c\)-th \(\top\). Otherwise output \(\bot\).

This is \((\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)\)-DP for any number of queries. If the queries are monotone, meaning one record moves all of them the same way, noise \(\nu_i \sim \mathrm{Lap}(c\Delta/\varepsilon_2)\) suffices.

J.5.2 The idea

The proof is the chapter’s AboveThreshold proof with \(c\) answers \(\top\) instead of one. All the \(\bot\) answers together are one condition, that the largest noisy \(\bot\) query is below the threshold, so one shift of the threshold noise by at most \(\Delta\) pays for all of them, at a factor \(e^{\varepsilon_1}\). Each \(\top\) answer needs its own noise shifted by at most \(2\Delta\), \(\Delta\) for its own query’s move and \(\Delta\) for the threshold’s. With noise scale \(2c\Delta/\varepsilon_2\), one such shift costs \(e^{\varepsilon_2/c}\), and the run halts at the \(c\)-th, so together they cost \(e^{\varepsilon_2}\).

J.5.3 Derivation

The proof reuses four facts of the selection chapter:

    1. pure DP can be checked one output at a time (Proposition 18.1);
    1. noise drawn the same way on both datasets can be fixed and averaged over at the end (Lemma 22.1);
    1. a Laplace density with scale \(b\) changes by at most a factor \(e^{|s|/b}\) when shifted by \(s\) (Lemma 22.3);
    1. a maximum of numbers that each move by at most \(\Delta\) moves by at most \(\Delta\) (Lemma 22.2).

Fix neighbours \(D \sim D'\).

  1. Fix an output and the \(\bot\) noises. An output is a sequence of answers that ends either at the \(c\)-th \(\top\) or at the end of the stream. Let \(B\) be the positions of its \(\bot\) answers and \(A\) those of its \(\top\) answers, so \(A\) has at most \(c\) elements. Fix the noises \(\nu_i\) for \(i \in B\). These noises do not depend on the data, so by fact (b) it is enough to bound the conditional probability. The queries are fixed too, since each can depend only on earlier answers, which the output fixes.
  2. Write the output as conditions on \(\xi\) and the \(\top\) noises. Let \(g(D) = \max_{i \in B}\big(f_i(D) + \nu_i\big)\) be the largest noisy \(\bot\) query. All \(\bot\) answers happen exactly when \(g(D) < \tau + \xi\). Each \(\top\) answer \(i \in A\) happens when \(f_i(D) + \nu_i \ge \tau + \xi\). Write \(p_\xi\) and \(p_\nu\) for the Laplace densities of the threshold noise and of a query noise. The probability of the output is the integral of these densities over the region where all the conditions hold: \[\int p_\xi(r)\,\mathbf 1\big[g(D) < \tau + r\big]\prod_{i \in A}\Big(p_\nu(v_i)\,\mathbf 1\big[f_i(D) + v_i \ge \tau + r\big]\Big)\,\mathrm dr\,\prod_{i \in A}\mathrm dv_i. \tag{J.22}\]
  3. Shift the noises. Substitute \(r = r' + g(D) - g(D')\) and, for each \(i \in A\), \(v_i = v_i' + \big(g(D) - g(D')\big) - \big(f_i(D) - f_i(D')\big)\). The first condition becomes \(g(D') < \tau + r'\), and each \(\top\) condition becomes \(f_i(D') + v_i' \ge \tau + r'\), which are exactly the conditions for \(D'\). The substitution is a translation, so it does not change the volume element. Only the densities change, because they are now evaluated at shifted points.
  4. Size of the shifts. The \(\nu_i\) for \(i \in B\) are fixed, so \(g\) is a maximum of numbers that each move by at most \(\Delta\), and \(|g(D) - g(D')| \le \Delta\) by fact (d). Each \(\top\) shift is a difference of two terms of size at most \(\Delta\), so it is at most \(2\Delta\). If there are no \(\bot\) answers, there is no \(g\), so shift \(r\) by 0 and each \(v_i\) by \(-(f_i(D) - f_i(D'))\), at most \(\Delta\).
  5. Pay for the shifts. By fact (c), the threshold noise, with scale \(\Delta/\varepsilon_1\) and shift at most \(\Delta\), costs a factor at most \(e^{\varepsilon_1}\). Each \(\top\) noise, with scale \(2c\Delta/\varepsilon_2\) and shift at most \(2\Delta\), costs at most \(e^{2\Delta\varepsilon_2/(2c\Delta)} = e^{\varepsilon_2/c}\), and there are at most \(c\) of them, so together they cost at most \(e^{\varepsilon_2}\). Pulling the factors out of the integral leaves the integral for \(D'\): \[\Pr[\text{output} \mid D, \nu_B] \le e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2}\,\Pr[\text{output} \mid D', \nu_B]. \tag{J.23}\]
  6. Collect. Fact (b) removes the conditioning on \(\nu_B\), and fact (a) turns the bound for every output into \((\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)\)-DP.

Monotone queries. If all queries move the same way, by at most \(\Delta\), then \(g\) moves the same way as each \(f_i\), also by at most \(\Delta\) (fact (d)). The \(\top\) shift in step 3 is then the difference of two numbers of the same sign, each at most \(\Delta\) in size, so it is at most \(\Delta\). With noise scale \(c\Delta/\varepsilon_2\) each shift costs \(e^{\varepsilon_2/c}\), and the rest is unchanged.

J.5.4 Checking it

The script computes the exact privacy loss of one output on one neighbouring pair, by integrating over the threshold noise on a fine grid. The pair extends the counterexample of the selection chapter, so that every shift in step 4 can approach its largest size. On \(D\) the first \(m\) queries have value 0 and the last \(c\) have value 1, while on \(D'\) the first \(m\) have value 1 and the last \(c\) have value 0, with \(\Delta = 1\), \(\tau = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon_2 = 0.5\). The output is \(m\) answers \(\bot\) followed by \(c\) answers \(\top\). Figure J.7 shows the loss against \(m\). For \(c = 1\) it is \(0.3958\) at \(m = 1\) and \(0.9940\) at \(m = 10\), the chapter’s AboveThreshold values, and for \(c = 4\) it climbs more slowly. For every \(c\) it reaches \(1.0000\), which is \(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2\), by \(m = 40\), and never goes above, so the bound is tight. With noise \(\mathrm{Lap}(2\Delta/\varepsilon_2)\) that does not grow with \(c\), each \(\top\) costs a full \(e^{\varepsilon_2}\), and for \(c = 4\) the loss reaches \(2.5000 = \varepsilon_1 + 4\varepsilon_2\).

J.6 Gumbel top-\(c\) is the exponential mechanism without replacement

J.6.1 What the chapter uses

Section 22.5 proves the Gumbel-max trick Equation 22.13: add independent Gumbel noise of scale \(\kappa\) to each score \(u_r\) and take the largest, and candidate \(i\) wins with probability \(e^{u_i/\kappa}/\sum_r e^{u_r/\kappa}\), which is the exponential mechanism Equation 22.2 when \(\kappa = 2\Delta/\varepsilon\). Write \(\lambda_r = e^{u_r/\kappa}\) for the weight of candidate \(r\). Then for any distinct candidates \(i_1, \dots, i_c\), \[\Pr\big[\text{the top } c \text{ of } u_r + G_r, \text{ in order, are } i_1, \dots, i_c\big] = \prod_{j=1}^c\frac{\lambda_{i_j}}{\sum_{r \notin \{i_1, \dots, i_{j-1}\}}\lambda_r}. \tag{J.24}\] Each factor is the probability that the exponential mechanism, run on the candidates not yet chosen, picks \(i_j\). So the \(c\) largest noisy scores have the distribution of \(c\) exponential mechanisms run in turn without replacement, and their privacy is \(c\varepsilon\), which the chapter states citing Durfee & Rogers (2019).

J.6.2 The idea

Turn each noisy score into a waiting time, \(E_r = e^{-(u_r + G_r)/\kappa}\), so that a higher score means an earlier arrival. For Gumbel noise these times are independent exponential variables with rates \(\lambda_r\), and the top \(c\) noisy scores are the first \(c\) arrivals of a race. In such a race, the chance that \(r\) arrives first is its share \(\lambda_r/\sum_s\lambda_s\) of the total rate. Exponential waits have no memory, so after one arrival the race restarts among the rest. For example, with rates 3, 2 and 1, the first candidate wins the first round with probability \(3/6\). The race then restarts between the other two, and the second candidate wins with probability \(2/3\), so the ordered pair (first, second) has probability \(\frac36\cdot\frac23 = \frac13\).

J.6.3 Derivation

  1. Arrival times are exponential. The Gumbel distribution with scale \(\kappa\) has cumulative distribution function \(\Pr[G \le g] = \exp(-e^{-g/\kappa})\). For \(s > 0\), \(E_r > s\) means \(u_r + G_r < -\kappa\ln s\), that is \(G_r < -\kappa\ln s - u_r\). So \[\Pr[E_r > s] = \exp\big(-e^{\ln s + u_r/\kappa}\big) = \exp(-s\lambda_r). \tag{J.25}\] That is the survival function of an exponential variable with rate \(\lambda_r\). The \(E_r\) are independent because the \(G_r\) are.
  2. Order reverses. The map \(x \mapsto e^{-x/\kappa}\) is decreasing, so the largest noisy score has the smallest arrival time. The top \(c\) noisy scores, in decreasing order, are the first \(c\) arrivals, in order.
  3. The first arrival. Let \(\Lambda = \sum_r\lambda_r\). Candidate \(i\) arrives first at a time near \(s\) when \(E_i\) is near \(s\) and every other \(E_r\) exceeds \(s\). Integrating over \(s\), \[\Pr[i \text{ first}] = \int_0^\infty\lambda_ie^{-\lambda_is}\prod_{r \ne i}e^{-\lambda_rs}\,\mathrm ds = \int_0^\infty\lambda_ie^{-\Lambda s}\,\mathrm ds = \frac{\lambda_i}{\Lambda}. \tag{J.26}\]
  4. No memory. Given \(E_r > s\), the extra wait \(E_r - s\) exceeds \(x\) with probability \(e^{-\lambda_r(s + x)}/e^{-\lambda_rs} = e^{-\lambda_rx}\). So it is again exponential with rate \(\lambda_r\), whatever \(s\) is. The other candidates’ times are independent of \(E_i\), so given that \(i\) arrives first at time \(s\), their extra waits are independent exponentials with their own rates, and their order does not depend on \(s\).
  5. Restart the race. By step 4, the probability that \(i\) arrives first and the rest arrive in a given order is the integral of step 3 times the probability of that order in a race among the others alone. That is \(\lambda_i/\Lambda\) times a race with \(i\) removed. Applying this \(c\) times gives Equation J.24.
  6. The same as \(c\) exponential mechanisms. Round \(j\) of the exponential mechanism without replacement picks \(i_j\) with probability \(\lambda_{i_j}\) over the sum of the remaining weights. That is the \(j\)-th factor of Equation J.24, so the two procedures have the same distribution of ordered outputs.
  7. Privacy. Each round is the exponential mechanism on a set of candidates fixed by the earlier outputs, with scores of sensitivity \(\Delta\), so each is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Basic composition, which holds even when each step depends on the earlier outputs (Section 19.3), makes the \(c\) rounds \(c\varepsilon\)-DP. Releasing the top \(c\) as an unordered set is post-processing of the ordered list, so it is \(c\varepsilon\)-DP too.

J.6.4 Checking it

Take six candidates with scores \((4, 3, 3, 1, 0, 0)\) and scale \(\kappa = 2\), the chapter’s five candidates with a sixth. Equation J.24 gives probabilities for all 30 ordered pairs, which sum to 1. For candidate 1 followed by candidate 2 it gives \(0.1313\), and in a million draws of Gumbel noise the top two came out in that order with frequency \(0.1313\). Over all 30 pairs the largest difference between frequency and formula is \(0.0003\), about the sampling error of a million draws. Figure J.8 shows the 30 pairs from a fresh run, and Figure J.9 shows the race, in which a higher score gives a steeper curve and an earlier typical arrival.

J.7 A Gumbel noisy max is private by the Laplace bar argument

J.7.1 What the chapter uses

Theorem 22.4 in Section 22.4 proves report-noisy-max with Laplace noise private by a bar argument. Fix two neighbouring datasets, a candidate \(i\), and the noise of every other candidate. Then \(i\) wins exactly when its own noise clears a bar, and moving to the other dataset moves the bar by at most \(2\Delta\), or within \([-\Delta, \Delta]\) when the scores are monotone. Lemma 22.3 pays for the move: for Laplace noise \(Z\) of scale \(b\), \(\Pr[Z > t - s] \le e^{|s|/b}\Pr[Z > t]\). The bar argument works with Gumbel noise of scale \(\kappa = 2\Delta/\varepsilon\) as well, because for \(G \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(\kappa)\) and all real \(t\) and \(s\), \[\Pr[G > t - s] \le e^{|s|/\kappa}\Pr[G > t]. \tag{J.27}\] Section 22.5 instead proves the Gumbel noisy max private as the exponential mechanism (Theorem 22.5), and this second proof does not need the softmax formula. With monotone scores it allows the smaller scale \(\kappa = \Delta/\varepsilon\).

J.7.2 The idea

The Laplace proof compares densities, and that fails for the Gumbel density, whose left tail falls like a double exponential, so that the ratio of densities a fixed distance apart grows without bound. But the bar argument needs only the tail \(\Pr[G > t]\), the chance of clearing a bar. Write the tail as \(1 - e^{-y}\), with \(y = e^{-t/\kappa}\). Lowering the bar by \(s > 0\) multiplies \(y\) by \(e^{s/\kappa}\). The function \(1 - e^{-y}\) is zero at \(y = 0\) and bends downward, so multiplying \(y\) by a factor of at least 1 multiplies the function by at most that factor. For example, at \(y = 1\), doubling \(y\) takes \(1 - e^{-y}\) from 0.6321 to 0.8647, a factor of only 1.37.

J.7.3 Derivation

  1. The tail as a function of \(y\). The Gumbel distribution of scale \(\kappa\) has \(\Pr[G \le g] = \exp(-e^{-g/\kappa})\), so with \(\phi(y) = 1 - e^{-y}\) and \(y(t) = e^{-t/\kappa}\), \[\Pr[G > t] = 1 - \exp\big(-e^{-t/\kappa}\big) = \phi\big(y(t)\big). \tag{J.28}\]
  2. Lowering the bar multiplies \(y\). Moving the bar from \(t\) to \(t - s\) gives \(y(t - s) = e^{-(t - s)/\kappa} = e^{s/\kappa}\,y(t)\).
  3. A raised bar. If \(s \le 0\), the bar \(t - s\) is at least \(t\), so the tail can only fall, and \(\Pr[G > t - s] \le \Pr[G > t]\). Since \(e^{|s|/\kappa} \ge 1\), Equation J.27 holds.
  4. A lowered bar. If \(s > 0\), write \(c = e^{s/\kappa} > 1\). The function \(\phi\) is concave, because its second derivative \(-e^{-y}\) is negative, so its graph lies above every chord. The point \(y\) lies on the chord from \(0\) to \(cy\), at the fraction \(1/c\) of the way, so, with \(\phi(0) = 0\), \[\phi(y) \ge \frac1c\,\phi(cy) + \Big(1 - \frac1c\Big)\phi(0) = \frac{\phi(cy)}{c}. \tag{J.29}\] So \(\phi(cy) \le c\,\phi(y)\), which by steps 1 and 2 is Equation J.27.
  5. The bar argument. Run the proof of Theorem 22.4 with Gumbel noise \(G_i\) in place of the Laplace noise \(Z_i\). Its steps 1 to 4 freeze the other candidates’ noise, write the event that \(i\) wins as \(G_i > t(D)\), and bound the move \(s = t(D') - t(D)\) of the bar. None of them uses the shape of the noise, only that it is drawn the same way on both datasets. Step 5 pays for the move with Equation J.27 in place of Lemma 22.3, which gives the factor \(e^{2\Delta/\kappa} = e^\varepsilon\) at \(\kappa = 2\Delta/\varepsilon\). With monotone scores the move lies in \([-\Delta, \Delta]\), and the factor is \(e^{\Delta/\kappa} = e^\varepsilon\) at \(\kappa = \Delta/\varepsilon\).

J.7.4 Checking it

Take \(\kappa = 1\) and \(s = 1\), the setting in which the chapter checks Lemma 22.3. Table J.3 gives the ratio of the two tails at four bars.

The ratio climbs towards \(e\) as the bar moves right, because far to the right \(y\) is small and \(1 - e^{-y}\) is nearly \(y\) itself, but it never reaches \(e\). On a grid of bars from \(-10\) to 20 and shifts from \(-5\) to 5, the ratio never exceeds \(e^{|s|}\). The Laplace tail, by contrast, reaches the factor \(e\) exactly once both bars are above 0, as Figure J.10 shows.

J.7.5 What it does not cover

The bar argument covers the release of one winner. Releasing the top \(c\) noisy scores needs the race argument of Section J.6. The argument also covers only the index of the winner, as for Laplace noise, because releasing the winning noisy score as well would publish \(G_i\), the noise the proof averages over.

J.8 The alarm mechanism is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, with one condition

J.8.1 What the chapter uses

Section 19.2 shows that the group-privacy bound Equation 19.5 is attained by a mechanism whose input is a count, where neighbouring counts differ by 1. The mechanism has a parameter \(K\), the largest group it is built for, and on a count \(c \le K\) it outputs “alarm” with probability \[a_c = \delta\sum_{j=0}^{K-c-1}e^{j\varepsilon} = \delta\,\frac{e^{(K-c)\varepsilon} - 1}{e^\varepsilon - 1}, \tag{J.30}\] and “quiet” otherwise, so \(a_c = 0\) for \(c \ge K\). For \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(K \ge 1\), the alarm mechanism is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP if and only if \[a_0 \le \frac{e^\varepsilon + \delta}{e^\varepsilon + 1}. \tag{J.31}\] Since \(a_0\) grows with \(K\), this caps \(K\). The chapter shows that the event “alarm” meets the definition with equality and states this condition, which this section proves.

J.8.2 The idea

There are only two outputs, so there are only four checks: “alarm” and “quiet”, each in both orders of a pair of neighbouring counts. The alarm probabilities fall as the count rises, and three of the checks hold for any \(K\). The fourth asks that “quiet” on the higher count is not too much likelier than on the lower one. If \(a_0\) is close to 1, “quiet” is rare on count 0 but much more common on count 1, whose alarm probability is smaller by about a factor \(e^\varepsilon\), and the ratio of the two “quiet” probabilities breaks the bound.

J.8.3 Derivation

Fix neighbouring counts \(c\) and \(c + 1\). The alarm probability has fewer terms at \(c + 1\), so \(a_c \ge a_{c+1}\). For \(c \ge K\) both are 0, the two output distributions are equal, and there is nothing to check. So take \(c \le K - 1\), where splitting off the first term of the sum gives \[a_c = e^\varepsilon a_{c+1} + \delta. \tag{J.32}\] The empty event has probability 0 on both sides, and the event “alarm or quiet” has probability 1 on both sides, which is at most \(e^\varepsilon + \delta\). So both pass, and four checks remain.

  1. “Alarm”, count \(c\) against \(c + 1\). The definition asks \(a_c \le e^\varepsilon a_{c+1} + \delta\). This holds with equality, by the rule above.
  2. “Alarm”, count \(c + 1\) against \(c\). It asks \(a_{c+1} \le e^\varepsilon a_c + \delta\), which holds because \(a_{c+1} \le a_c\).
  3. “Quiet”, count \(c\) against \(c + 1\). It asks \(1 - a_c \le e^\varepsilon(1 - a_{c+1}) + \delta\). This holds because \(1 - a_c \le 1 - a_{c+1}\) and \(1 - a_{c+1} \ge 0\).
  4. “Quiet”, count \(c + 1\) against \(c\). It asks \(1 - a_{c+1} \le e^\varepsilon(1 - a_c) + \delta\). Substitute the rule for \(a_c\), and the right side becomes \(e^\varepsilon - e^{2\varepsilon}a_{c+1} - (e^\varepsilon - 1)\delta\). Moving terms across, the check becomes \((e^{2\varepsilon} - 1)a_{c+1} \le (e^\varepsilon - 1)(1 - \delta)\). Since \(e^{2\varepsilon} - 1 = (e^\varepsilon - 1)(e^\varepsilon + 1)\) and \(e^\varepsilon - 1 > 0\), it is \[a_{c+1} \le \frac{1 - \delta}{e^\varepsilon + 1}. \tag{J.33}\]
  5. The worst pair. The largest \(a_{c+1}\) with \(c \le K - 1\) is \(a_1\), so all checks pass exactly when \(a_1 \le (1 - \delta)/(e^\varepsilon + 1)\). By the rule, \(a_0 = e^\varepsilon a_1 + \delta\), so this condition is the same as \(a_0 \le \big(e^\varepsilon(1 - \delta) + \delta(e^\varepsilon + 1)\big)/(e^\varepsilon + 1)\). The numerator simplifies to \(e^\varepsilon + \delta\), which gives Equation J.31. The right side of Equation J.31 is below 1, so the condition also makes \(a_0\) a valid probability.

At the cap the pair of counts 0 and 1 is exactly the four-point pair of Section J.1, with “flag \(D\)” merged into report 0 and “flag \(D'\)” merged into report 1. Then \(a_0 = \delta + (1 - \delta)t\) and \(a_1 = (1 - \delta)(1 - t)\), with \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\), as extreme as \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP allows.

J.8.4 Checking it

In the chapter’s example, \(\varepsilon = 0.5\), \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) and \(K = 10\). The cap is \((e^{0.5} + 10^{-5})/(e^{0.5} + 1) = 0.6225\), and \(a_0 = 2.272\times10^{-3}\), far below it, so the chapter’s mechanism is \((0.5, 10^{-5})\)-DP. The script’s check over every event agrees: the largest amount by which any event exceeds the bound is \(2.5\times10^{-20}\), which is rounding on the event that holds with equality. The largest allowed size at these parameters is \(K = 21\), where \(a_0 = 0.5598\). At \(K = 22\), \(a_0 = 0.9229\) is still a probability, but the event “quiet” on counts 1 against 0 exceeds the bound by \(0.3132\). So the condition is real, and it does not follow from \(a_0 \le 1\).

J.8.5 What it does not cover

The derivation divides by \(e^\varepsilon - 1\) in step 4, so it needs \(\varepsilon > 0\). At \(\varepsilon = 0\) the rule reads \(a_c = a_{c+1} + \delta\), check 4 asks \(a_c - a_{c+1} \le \delta\), which holds with equality, and the only condition left is \(a_0 \le 1\).

J.9 Code

The script apx_mechanisms.py computes every number on this page and draws its figures. It builds the four-point channel and checks it on 6,000 random pairs. It computes the stability-based histogram’s error exactly and by simulation, and the sparse vector technique’s privacy loss by numerical integration. It also evaluates the heavy-tail lower bound, compares Gumbel top-two selection with the exponential mechanism, and checks every event of the alarm mechanism. From the repository root:

uv run python code/appendix-derivations/apx_mechanisms.py

Table J.4 shows the output with seed 0.

tests/test_appendix_mechanisms.py checks that the output still matches this table.

What to take from it. The four-point construction is exact: it reproduces every pair to rounding, including pairs whose outputs only one side can produce. The stability-based histogram’s error is flat in \(N\), and its bound is within about 1.4 records of the exact worst case, so what approximate DP costs here is the constant \(\ln(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\), in place of a \(\ln N\) that grows with the universe. The sparse vector proof is tight: on a worst-case pair the loss approaches \(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2\) for every \(c\), and noise that ignores \(c\) really does cost \(c\) times more. And the alarm mechanism’s hidden condition matters at \(K = 22\) but not at the chapter’s \(K = 10\).
