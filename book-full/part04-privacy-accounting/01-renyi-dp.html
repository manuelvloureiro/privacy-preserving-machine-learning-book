29  Rényi differential privacy

You train a model with 1000 steps of full-batch noisy gradient descent. Each step releases a quantity whose sensitivity is 1, which means that one person’s record moves it by at most 1, and adds Gaussian noise with a standard deviation \(\sigma = 20\) times that sensitivity. The number \(\sigma\) is the noise multiplier. What is the total \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) differential privacy (DP) guarantee at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\)? Advanced and optimal composition turns each step into one pair \((\varepsilon_0, \delta_0)\) and composes the pairs, which gives \(\varepsilon = 68.01\). The true answer is \(\varepsilon = 7.51\), because 1000 Gaussian steps behave like one Gaussian step with noise multiplier \(\sigma/\sqrt{1000}\).

The overestimate comes from the first move, because a Gaussian step has a whole curve of valid pairs and composition needs more than one point of it. Rényi differential privacy (RDP) keeps a curve that adds under composition. For each order \(\alpha > 1\) it records the Rényi divergence between the outputs on neighbouring datasets, an exponential moment of the privacy loss. Curves add because a moment of a product of independent likelihood ratios is a product of moments. A Chernoff bound converts the summed curve to \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) once, at the end, and for the 1000 steps this gives \(\varepsilon = 8.84\), 18% above the exact 7.51.

Two running examples carry the chapter: randomized response, which reports one person’s bit truthfully with probability \(t = 0.75\), and the 1000 Gaussian steps. Neighbouring datasets \(D \sim D'\) differ by adding or removing one record, and for randomized response they are the two values of one person’s bit. Table 29.1 lists the symbols.

29.1 Rényi differential privacy

Accounting needs a summary of the whole upper tail of the privacy loss. Pure \(\varepsilon\)-DP bounds the privacy loss at every output, and \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP lets that bound fail on outputs of total probability \(\delta\), so each describes the tail by one point.

Fix a mechanism \(M\) and neighbours \(D \sim D'\), and write \(P = M(D)\) and \(Q = M(D')\) for the two output distributions, with densities or mass functions \(p\) and \(q\). The likelihood ratio \(r = p/q\) says how many times likelier an output is under \(D\) than under \(D'\). Its logarithm \(L = \ln r\) is the privacy loss of Advanced and optimal composition (Definition 21.1). The Rényi divergence \(D_\alpha\) of order \(\alpha > 1\) (Definition 3.2) is the logarithm of a moment of \(r\), divided by \(\alpha - 1\). By Proposition 3.1 you can take that moment under either distribution, and under \(P\) it is an exponential moment of the loss: \[e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P\|Q)} = \mathbb E_Q\big[r^\alpha\big] = \mathbb E_P\big[e^{(\alpha - 1)L}\big]. \tag{29.1}\] The second equality is the change of measure of Lemma 3.2, which turns an average under \(P\) into an average under \(Q\) with each output weighted by \(r\). An exponential moment gives large losses more weight than small ones, and more so at a larger order. So small orders see the typical loss and large orders the worst case, and a bound at every order describes the whole upper tail.

Definition 29.1 (Rényi differential privacy) Let \(\alpha > 1\). A mechanism \(M\) is \((\alpha, \varepsilon)\)-RDP if for all neighbouring datasets \(D \sim D'\) \[D_\alpha\big(M(D)\,\|\,M(D')\big) \le \varepsilon. \tag{29.2}\] The RDP curve of \(M\) records, at each order, the smallest \(\varepsilon\) that works, which is the divergence of the worst pair of neighbours: \[\varepsilon_M(\alpha) = \sup_{D \sim D'} D_\alpha\big(M(D)\,\|\,M(D')\big). \tag{29.3}\]

The curve takes its shape from the divergence of each pair. Divergences showed that this divergence rises with the order, from the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence at order 1 to the max-divergence \(D_\infty\), the largest privacy loss. Pure DP bounds that largest loss, so pure DP is the top point of the curve.

Proposition 29.1 (The two ends of the RDP curve) Let \(M\) be a mechanism, and write \(\varepsilon_M(\infty)\) for the supremum over neighbours of \(D_\infty\big(M(D)\|M(D')\big)\). Then:

  1. \(M\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP if and only if \(\varepsilon_M(\infty) \le \varepsilon\).
  2. \(\varepsilon_M(\alpha)\) is non-decreasing in \(\alpha\), so an \(\varepsilon\)-DP mechanism is \((\alpha, \varepsilon)\)-RDP at every order.
  3. At every order, \(\varepsilon_M(\alpha)\) is at least the largest KL divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(M(D)\|M(D')\big)\) over neighbours, and so the limit of the curve as \(\alpha \to 1\) is at least that KL divergence too.

Proof.

  1. Pure DP is the max-divergence. \(\varepsilon\)-DP says that \(\Pr_P[S] \le e^\varepsilon\Pr_Q[S]\) for every event \(S\) and every pair of neighbours. By Theorem 3.2, the largest log-ratio \(\ln(\Pr_P[S]/\Pr_Q[S])\) over events is \(D_\infty(P\|Q)\). So the condition holds for all pairs exactly when every \(D_\infty\) is at most \(\varepsilon\), which is \(\varepsilon_M(\infty) \le \varepsilon\).
  2. Monotonicity. Each pair’s \(D_\alpha\) is non-decreasing in \(\alpha\), by the monotonicity of Theorem 3.2. If every function of a family is at least as large at each \(\alpha' > \alpha\) as at \(\alpha\), so is the largest of them. With step 1, this gives \(\varepsilon_M(\alpha) \le \varepsilon_M(\infty) \le \varepsilon\) for an \(\varepsilon\)-DP mechanism.
  3. The KL end. The monotonicity of Theorem 3.2 covers all orders, and KL is the order 1, so it gives \(D_\alpha \ge D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) for each pair. The supremum over pairs keeps the inequality, and a limit of numbers that are all at least some value is at least that value.

An RDP guarantee is a curve, and every point of it is a valid guarantee. A mechanism whose loss is usually small but sometimes large has a small KL divergence but large high-order divergences, so large orders catch it. Orders below 1 exist as divergences but are not used for privacy, because the conversion of Section 29.6 needs the moment of Equation 29.1 with a positive exponent \(\alpha - 1\).

Example 29.1 (Randomized response, outcome by outcome) Randomized response (Definition 18.4) reports one person’s bit truthfully with probability \(t = 0.75\) and flips it otherwise. Table 29.2 lists its outputs when the true bit is 1 (\(P\)) and 0 (\(Q\)). The likelihood ratio of each output is 3 or \(1/3\), so the mechanism is \(\varepsilon\)-DP with \(\varepsilon = \ln\frac{t}{1-t} = \ln 3\).

At order 2, Equation 29.1 says that \(e^{D_2}\) is the sum of \(p(y)^2/q(y)\) over the outputs, which is the sum of the last column: \[e^{D_2} = 2.2500 + 0.0833 = 2.3333, \qquad \varepsilon(2) = \ln 2.3333 = 0.8473. \tag{29.4}\] The same recipe works at any order, and it gives Equation 29.7 of the next section. A swap of the two bits gives the same two terms, so both directions of the pair have the curve of Table 29.3.

The curve rises from the KL value to the pure DP level (Figure 29.1). At the bottom is the average loss under \(P\), which is \(\ln 3\) with probability 0.75 and \(-\ln 3\) with probability 0.25, so it is \(0.5\ln 3 = 0.5493\). At the top is the largest loss, \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\). For a pure DP mechanism like this one, the curve adds little to \(\ln 3\), so it matters most when \(D_\infty\) is large or infinite.

29.2 RDP curves of the basic mechanisms

To account for a mechanism you need its curve, and this section computes it for the three mechanisms that every accountant starts from. A mechanism that adds noise to a query’s answer gives, on two neighbours, the same noise distribution centred at two different answers. So its curve is the Rényi divergence between the noise and a shifted copy, which Divergences computed for the normal and the Laplace distributions. The only new step is the supremum over neighbours, and the worst pair is the one whose answers are furthest apart.

The Gaussian mechanism adds \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 I_d)\) noise to a query \(f\) whose \(\ell_2\) sensitivity \(\Delta_2 f\), the largest Euclidean distance between its answers on neighbours, is 1. The Laplace mechanism of Theorem 20.1 adds noise with density \(\frac1{2b}e^{-|z|/b}\) to a real-valued query whose sensitivity \(\Delta_1 f\), the largest \(|f(D) - f(D')|\) over neighbours, is 1. The three formulas are Corollaries 3 and 2 and Table 2 of Mironov (2017), and Section B.4 in the appendices derives the Gaussian curve from scratch.

Theorem 29.1 (RDP curves of the Gaussian, Laplace and randomized response mechanisms) Let \(\alpha > 1\). The Gaussian mechanism with noise multiplier \(\sigma\), on a query with \(\Delta_2 f = 1\), has the curve \[\varepsilon(\alpha) = \frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{29.5}\] The Laplace mechanism with scale \(b\), on a real-valued query with \(\Delta_1 f = 1\), has the curve \[\varepsilon(\alpha) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha - 1}e^{(\alpha - 1)/b} + \frac{\alpha - 1}{2\alpha - 1}e^{-\alpha/b}\right). \tag{29.6}\] Randomized response with truth probability \(t \in (\frac12, 1)\) has the curve \[\varepsilon(\alpha) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\ln\Big(t^\alpha(1-t)^{1-\alpha} + (1-t)^\alpha t^{1-\alpha}\Big). \tag{29.7}\]

Proof. The plan is the same for both noise mechanisms: reduce a pair of neighbours to the noise against a shifted copy, take its divergence from Divergences, and show that the largest shift is the worst. Randomized response needs only the sum.

  1. Gaussian, one pair. On neighbours the outputs are \(\mathcal N(f(D), \sigma^2 I_d)\) and \(\mathcal N(f(D'), \sigma^2 I_d)\), whose \(d\) coordinates are independent. Coordinate \(i\) is a pair of normals whose means are some distance \(s_i\) apart, with divergence \(\alpha s_i^2/(2\sigma^2)\) by Proposition 3.3. Rényi divergences add over independent coordinates, by the additivity of Theorem 3.2, so the pair has \[D_\alpha = \frac{\alpha\sum_i s_i^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{\alpha\,\|f(D) - f(D')\|_2^2}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{29.8}\]
  2. Gaussian, worst pair. The divergence grows with the distance between the answers, which is at most \(\Delta_2 f = 1\), so the supremum over neighbours is Equation 29.5.
  3. Laplace, one pair. A translation of the output only relabels it and changes no divergence, so the pair is \(\mathrm{Lap}(0, b)\) against \(\mathrm{Lap}(s, b)\), with \(s = |f(D) - f(D')| \le 1\). Proposition 3.4 gives its divergence, which is Equation 29.6 with \(1/b\) replaced by the shift in scale units, \(s/b\). The reverse pair has the same divergence, because the reflection \(y \mapsto s - y\) swaps the two distributions.
  4. Laplace, worst pair. You need the divergence to grow with the shift. Write \(x = s/b\) and let \(g(x)\) be the argument of the logarithm, \(\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha - 1}e^{(\alpha - 1)x} + \frac{\alpha - 1}{2\alpha - 1}e^{-\alpha x}\). The derivative of each exponential gives \[g'(x) = \frac{\alpha(\alpha - 1)}{2\alpha - 1}\big(e^{(\alpha - 1)x} - e^{-\alpha x}\big), \tag{29.9}\] which is positive for \(x > 0\), because the first exponent is positive and the second negative. The logarithm and the factor \(1/(\alpha - 1)\) keep the divergence increasing, so the worst pair has \(s = 1\), and the supremum is Equation 29.6.
  5. Randomized response. There is only one pair of inputs, the two values of the bit, so no supremum is needed. In the sum of \(p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}\), output 1 contributes \(t^\alpha(1-t)^{1-\alpha}\) and output 0 contributes \((1-t)^\alpha t^{1-\alpha}\). The logarithm of the sum, divided by \(\alpha - 1\), is Equation 29.7.

The curves have two shapes in Figure 29.2, and the shape depends on whether the privacy loss is bounded. A bounded loss makes the curve level off. For randomized response the loss is \(\pm\ln 3\). For Laplace with answers 0 and 1 it is \((|y - 1| - |y|)/b\), which the triangle inequality keeps within \(1/b\) of zero. Every \(D_\alpha\) is at most the largest loss, so the two curves rise towards \(\ln 3\) and \(1/b\) and stay below them.

An unbounded loss makes the curve grow without limit. The Gaussian privacy loss is a straight line in the output (Lemma 20.3), so it has no maximum, and the Gaussian mechanism is not \(\varepsilon\)-DP for any \(\varepsilon\). But it is RDP at every finite order, which is all that composition and conversion need. A curve that is a straight line through the origin is the mark of a normal privacy loss, and Concentrated DP builds a definition on such lines.

Example 29.2 Table 29.4 gives the three curves at four orders, from the script. The Gaussian row is \(\alpha/2\) at each order.

You can check the Laplace row by hand at order 2. The coefficients in Equation 29.6 are \(\frac23\) and \(\frac13\), the prefactor \(1/(\alpha - 1)\) is 1, and with \(b = 1\) \[\varepsilon(2) = \ln\Big(\tfrac23e + \tfrac13e^{-2}\Big) = 0.6191. \tag{29.10}\] Both noise curves agree with a numerical integral of \(p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}\) (Table 29.8).

29.3 Composition

A training run has thousands of mechanisms, each chosen after it sees the outputs of the earlier ones, and you need the curve of the whole run. For independent mechanisms the answer is the additivity of Theorem 3.2: the moment of Equation 29.1 splits into a product, and the logarithm turns the product into a sum. Adaptive steps are not independent, because the second mechanism depends on the first output. But the curves still add if each step’s curve is bounded whatever the earlier outputs were. The result is Mironov (2017), Proposition 1, and Section B.4 derives it from scratch, while Section B.5 compares it with the other accountants on one mechanism.

Theorem 29.2 (Adaptive composition of RDP curves) Let \(M_1\) have RDP curve \(\varepsilon_1\). Let \(M_2(\cdot\,; y_1)\) be a mechanism chosen using the first output \(y_1\), and let \(\varepsilon_2\) be a curve, fixed in advance, that bounds the RDP curve of \(M_2(\cdot\,; y_1)\) for every \(y_1\). The adaptive composition \(M\) releases both outputs, \(M(D) = (y_1, y_2)\), with \(y_1 \sim M_1(D)\) and \(y_2 \sim M_2(D; y_1)\). Then for every \(\alpha > 1\) \[\varepsilon_M(\alpha) \le \varepsilon_1(\alpha) + \varepsilon_2(\alpha). \tag{29.11}\] By induction, with the first \(k - 1\) steps treated as one mechanism, \(k\) adaptively chosen mechanisms have a curve of at most \(\sum_{i=1}^k \varepsilon_i(\alpha)\).

Proof. The plan is to factor the joint density, write the moment as a double integral, bound the inner integral uniformly, and recognize the outer one. Fix neighbours \(D \sim D'\) and an order \(\alpha > 1\). Write \(p_1, q_1\) for the densities of \(y_1\) under \(D\) and \(D'\), and \(p_2(\cdot \mid y_1), q_2(\cdot \mid y_1)\) for those of \(y_2\) given \(y_1\).

  1. Factor the joint densities. By the definition of the composition, \(y_1\) is drawn first and \(y_2\) is drawn given \(y_1\), so \(p(y_1, y_2) = p_1(y_1)\,p_2(y_2 \mid y_1)\), and the same holds for \(q\).
  2. Write the moment as a double integral. By Equation 29.1, the moment is the integral of \(p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}\). A power of a product is the product of the powers, so the integrand splits into a factor for \(y_1\) and a factor for \(y_2\) given \(y_1\): \[e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P\|Q)} = \int p_1^\alpha q_1^{1-\alpha}\left(\int p_2(y_2\mid y_1)^\alpha q_2(y_2\mid y_1)^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm dy_2\right)\mathrm dy_1. \tag{29.12}\]
  3. Bound the inner integral. With \(y_1\) fixed, the inner integral is \(e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha}\) for the two outputs of \(M_2(\cdot\,; y_1)\) on \(D\) and \(D'\), by Equation 29.1 again. That divergence is at most \(\varepsilon_2(\alpha)\), and the exponential is increasing because \(\alpha - 1 > 0\). So, whatever \(y_1\) is, \[\int p_2(y_2\mid y_1)^\alpha q_2(y_2\mid y_1)^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm dy_2 \le e^{(\alpha - 1)\varepsilon_2(\alpha)}. \tag{29.13}\]
  4. Recognize the outer integral. Pull that constant out. What is left is \(\int p_1^\alpha q_1^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm dy_1\), which by Equation 29.1 is \(e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P_1\|Q_1)}\), at most \(e^{(\alpha - 1)\varepsilon_1(\alpha)}\). So \[e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P\|Q)} \le e^{(\alpha - 1)\varepsilon_2(\alpha)}\,e^{(\alpha - 1)\varepsilon_1(\alpha)}. \tag{29.14}\]
  5. Take logarithms. Division by \(\alpha - 1\) gives \(D_\alpha(P\|Q) \le \varepsilon_1(\alpha) + \varepsilon_2(\alpha)\) for this pair. The right side does not depend on the pair, so the supremum over neighbours satisfies the same bound.

The bound is exact for independent mechanisms, because then the inner divergence in step 3 is the same for every \(y_1\), and its worst case loses nothing. The factorization of step 2 is why composition works for RDP but not for \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) directly. The smallest \(\delta\) at a given \(\varepsilon\) is the hockey-stick divergence \(\int(p - e^\varepsilon q)_+\), where \((x)_+ = \max(x, 0)\), and a positive part of a difference does not factorize over the steps as the moment of step 2 does.

One condition remains. The mechanisms can be chosen adaptively, but each step’s curve bound \(\varepsilon_i\) must be fixed in advance and must not depend on the data. When the budget itself is chosen during the run, you need the filters and odometers of Adaptive composition.

Example 29.3 To see the steps on numbers, take an adaptive pair of randomized responses. The first step reports the bit with truth probability 0.75. The second reports it again, with truth probability 0.75 if the first output was 1 and 0.6 if it was 0. Table 29.5 lists the four joint outcomes when the true bit is 1 (\(P\)) and 0 (\(Q\)). As in step 1, each joint probability is the first step’s probability times the second’s.

The terms sum to 5.3472, so \(D_2 = \ln 5.3472 = 1.6766\). The bound adds the first step’s curve to the worst curve that the second step can have. At order 2 the second step’s curve is 0.8473 on the branch \(y_1 = 1\) and 0.1542 on the branch \(y_1 = 0\), by Equation 29.7 with \(t = 0.75\) and \(t = 0.6\). So the bound is \(0.8473 + \max(0.8473, 0.1542) = 1.6946\). It holds, and it is loose by \(1.6946 - 1.6766 = 0.0180\), because step 3 replaced the inner divergence on the branch \(y_1 = 0\) by its worst case. If the second step does not adapt, with truth probability 0.75 on both branches, the joint distribution is a product and \(D_2 = 1.6946\) exactly.

For the opening’s Gaussian steps with noise multiplier \(\sigma = 20\), each step has the curve \(\alpha/(2 \cdot 20^2) = \alpha/800\), so \(k\) steps have \(k\alpha/800\). If you set \(k\alpha/800 = \alpha/(2\sigma'^2)\), you find that this is the curve of one Gaussian step with noise multiplier \(\sigma' = 20/\sqrt k\). At \(k = 1000\) the curve is \(1.2500\,\alpha\), one Gaussian with \(\sigma' = 0.6325\). Different mechanisms compose as easily, order by order: a Laplace step with \(b = 1\) and then a Gaussian step with \(\sigma = 1\) have \(\varepsilon(2) = 0.6191 + 1 = 1.6191\), from Table 29.4.

29.4 Post-processing and group privacy

Two properties of DP should carry over to RDP. Post-processing matters because a released model or its predictions must not leak more than the training run that made them. Group privacy matters because one user can contribute many rows. Post-processing carries over unchanged, because it is the data-processing inequality of Theorem 3.5 applied to every pair of neighbours: a map that acts on the output alone can only make the two output distributions harder to tell apart.

Theorem 29.3 (Post-processing) Let \(M\) be a mechanism and \(g\) a randomized map applied to its output that does not read the data. Then \(\varepsilon_{g\circ M}(\alpha) \le \varepsilon_M(\alpha)\) for every \(\alpha > 1\).

Proof.

  1. For each pair of neighbours, \(g \circ M(D)\) is \(M(D)\) passed through the channel \(g\), the same channel whichever dataset was used. So \(D_\alpha\) can only decrease, by Theorem 3.5.
  2. The inequality holds for every pair, so it holds for the supremum.

Step 1 is where \(g\) must ignore the data. A map that reads the data is not a channel, and it could simply print a record.

Group privacy is weaker in RDP. For \(\varepsilon\)-DP, a group of \(k\) records gets \(k\varepsilon\) (Theorem 19.2), because the likelihood ratios along a chain of \(k\) neighbours multiply. A group of two records walks from \(D\) to \(D'\) through a dataset that differs from each in one record, so it needs a bound on the divergence across two steps. The Rényi divergence has no plain triangle inequality for this, only a weak one that pays with higher orders on the two legs. Two exponents \(u\) and \(v\) choose how the cost is split between the legs, and the proof separates the legs with Hölder’s inequality.

Lemma 29.1 (Weak triangle inequality (Mironov 2017, Proposition 11)) Let \(P\), \(R\) and \(Q\) be distributions, let \(\alpha > 1\), and let \(u, v > 1\) with \(\frac1u + \frac1v = 1\). Then \[D_\alpha(P\|Q) \le \frac{\alpha - 1/u}{\alpha - 1}D_{u\alpha}(P\|R) + D_{v(\alpha - 1) + 1}(R\|Q). \tag{29.15}\] The even split \(u = v = 2\) doubles the order on the first leg and nearly doubles it on the second: \[D_\alpha(P\|Q) \le \frac{\alpha - 1/2}{\alpha - 1}D_{2\alpha}(P\|R) + D_{2\alpha - 1}(R\|Q). \tag{29.16}\]

Proof. The plan is to write the moment for \(P\) against \(Q\) as an average under \(R\) of a product of two likelihood ratios, separate the two factors with Hölder’s inequality, and recognize each factor as a Rényi moment. Write \(p\), \(p_R\) and \(q\) for the densities of \(P\), \(R\) and \(Q\).

  1. Insert the middle distribution. The exponents of \(p_R\) in \((p/p_R)^\alpha (p_R/q)^{\alpha-1} p_R\) add to 0, so the product is \(p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}\). With Equation 29.1 this gives \[e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P\|Q)} = \mathbb E_R\Big[\Big(\frac{p}{p_R}\Big)^\alpha\Big(\frac{p_R}{q}\Big)^{\alpha - 1}\Big]. \tag{29.17}\]
  2. Separate the two ratios. Hölder’s inequality bounds the average of a product of two non-negative quantities \(X\) and \(Y\) by a product of their higher moments, \(\mathbb E_R[XY] \le \big(\mathbb E_R X^u\big)^{1/u}\big(\mathbb E_R Y^v\big)^{1/v}\), and with \(u = v = 2\) it is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Apply it with \(X = (p/p_R)^\alpha\) and \(Y = (p_R/q)^{\alpha-1}\).
  3. The first factor. \(\mathbb E_R[X^u]\) is the moment of the ratio \(p/p_R\) at order \(u\alpha\) under \(R\). By Equation 29.1 at order \(u\alpha\), it equals \(e^{(u\alpha - 1)D_{u\alpha}(P\|R)}\).
  4. The second factor. \(\mathbb E_R[Y^v]\) is an average under \(R\), and the change of measure from \(R\) to \(Q\) multiplies the integrand by \(p_R/q\). So it equals \(\mathbb E_Q\big[(p_R/q)^{v(\alpha - 1) + 1}\big]\), which by Equation 29.1 is \(e^{v(\alpha - 1)D_{v(\alpha-1)+1}(R\|Q)}\).
  5. Combine. Raise the factors to the powers \(1/u\) and \(1/v\) and take logarithms: \[(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P\|Q) \le \frac{u\alpha - 1}{u}D_{u\alpha}(P\|R) + (\alpha - 1)D_{v(\alpha-1)+1}(R\|Q). \tag{29.18}\] Division by \(\alpha - 1\) gives Equation 29.15, since \(\frac{u\alpha - 1}{u} = \alpha - \frac1u\). With \(u = v = 2\) the second order is \(2\alpha - 1\), which gives Equation 29.16.

For a group of two records, the lemma halves the order and costs a constant factor in \(\varepsilon\). If you repeat it, you get groups of \(2^c\) records, where \(c\) is the number of doublings.

Theorem 29.4 (Group privacy in RDP (Mironov 2017, Proposition 2)) Let \(M\) be \((\alpha, \varepsilon)\)-RDP, let \(c \ge 1\) be an integer, and suppose \(\alpha \ge 2^{c+1}\). Then for any two datasets that differ in \(2^c\) records, \(M\) satisfies the RDP bound of order \(\alpha/2^c\) with \(\varepsilon\) replaced by \(3^c\varepsilon\).

Proof. The plan is to prove the case of two records by a walk through an intermediate dataset, and then to repeat it for larger groups.

  1. Two records, the walk. Let \(D\) and \(D'\) differ in two records, and let \(D_1\) be the dataset in between, a neighbour of both. Put \(P = M(D)\), \(R = M(D_1)\) and \(Q = M(D')\), and let \(\beta = \alpha/2 \ge 2\) be the order of the group guarantee. Equation 29.16 at order \(\beta\) turns its orders into \(2\beta = \alpha\) and \(2\beta - 1 = \alpha - 1\): \[D_\beta(P\|Q) \le \frac{\beta - 1/2}{\beta - 1}D_{\alpha}(P\|R) + D_{\alpha - 1}(R\|Q). \tag{29.19}\]
  2. Two records, the legs. Both legs are pairs of neighbours, so \(D_\alpha(P\|R) \le \varepsilon\). By the monotonicity of Proposition 29.1, \(D_{\alpha-1}(R\|Q) \le D_\alpha(R\|Q) \le \varepsilon\).
  3. Two records, the factor. The factor \(\frac{\beta - 1/2}{\beta - 1} = 1 + \frac{1/2}{\beta - 1}\) decreases in \(\beta\) and equals \(\frac32\) at \(\beta = 2\), so it is at most \(\frac32\). Hence \[D_\beta(P\|Q) \le \tfrac32\varepsilon + \varepsilon = \tfrac52\varepsilon \le 3\varepsilon. \tag{29.20}\] Mironov states the bound with the constant 3, while the argument gives \(\frac52\).
  4. Larger groups. A group of \(2^c\) records is two groups of \(2^{c-1}\) records, so each round of steps 1 to 3 halves the order and multiplies \(\varepsilon\) by 3. After \(c\) rounds the order is \(\alpha/2^c\) and the bound is \(3^c\varepsilon\), and the condition \(\alpha \ge 2^{c+1}\) keeps the order of the last round at least 2.

Each doubling of the group costs half the order as well as a factor in \(\varepsilon\). The reason is step 2 of the lemma’s proof, where the average of a product of two likelihood ratios needs higher moments of each factor. For the Gaussian you know the exact answer. A group of \(2^c\) records shifts a query of sensitivity 1 by up to \(2^c\), so with noise multiplier \(\sigma\) the exact group curve is \(\alpha(2^c)^2/(2\sigma^2)\). Table 29.6 compares it with the bounds at \(\sigma = 1\). For two records the exact value at order \(\beta\) is \(4\beta/(2\sigma^2) = 2\varepsilon(2\beta)\) and the theorem gives \(3\varepsilon(2\beta)\), a factor 1.5, and the factor grows to \(1.5^c\) for \(2^c\) records.

So use the mechanism’s own curve when you can, which for the Gaussian is the curve with sensitivity \(2^c\). For a mechanism that is already \(\varepsilon\)-DP, the pure DP group bound \(k\varepsilon\) holds at every order by Proposition 29.1, and it can beat the RDP route.

Example 29.4 Post-processing first: pass the output of randomized response with \(t = 0.75\) through a channel that flips it with probability 0.2. When the bit is 1, the final output is 1 if the first output was 1 and kept, or 0 and flipped, which has probability \(0.75 \times 0.8 + 0.25 \times 0.2 = 0.65\). When the bit is 0, the same event has probability \(0.25 \times 0.8 + 0.75 \times 0.2 = 0.35\). So the two output distributions are now closer. \(D_2\) drops from 0.8473 to \(\ln\big(0.65^2/0.35 + 0.35^2/0.65\big) = 0.3333\), and \(D_\infty\) drops from 1.0986 to \(\ln(0.65/0.35) = 0.6190\).

For group privacy, take two records at \(\beta = 2\) with \(\sigma = 1\), the first row of Table 29.6. The weak triangle inequality gives \(\frac{1.5}{1}\varepsilon(4) + \varepsilon(3) = 1.5 \times 2 + 1.5 = 4.5\) against the exact 4. In Figure 29.3 the weak-triangle bound stays close to the exact line \(2\beta\) for \(\beta \ge 3\), and it grows without limit near \(\beta = 1\), as its factor \(\frac{\beta - 1/2}{\beta - 1}\) does.

29.5 The moments accountant

Many libraries describe their accounting in terms of the moments accountant of Abadi et al. (2016), which came before RDP, so you need to know that it tracks the same object. For each \(\lambda > 0\), in practice the integers up to 32, it tracks the logarithm of the moment generating function of the privacy loss, adds these numbers over the steps, and converts the total to \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) with a tail bound.

Definition 29.2 (Log-moment of a mechanism) For \(\lambda > 0\), the log-moment of a mechanism \(M\) is the cumulant generating function of the privacy loss of the worst pair of neighbours, \[K_M(\lambda) = \sup_{D \sim D'}\ln\mathbb E_{Y \sim M(D)}\big[e^{\lambda L(Y)}\big]. \tag{29.21}\] Abadi et al. write it \(\alpha_M(\lambda)\), which clashes with the Rényi order.

The moment inside the logarithm is the one in Equation 29.1 with \(\alpha - 1 = \lambda\). So the log-moment is the RDP curve one order up, multiplied by \(\lambda\).

Proposition 29.2 (The moments accountant is RDP) For every mechanism \(M\) and every \(\lambda > 0\), \[K_M(\lambda) = \lambda\,\varepsilon_M(\lambda + 1), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \varepsilon_M(\alpha) = \frac{K_M(\alpha - 1)}{\alpha - 1}. \tag{29.22}\]

Proof.

  1. The moment is a Rényi moment. Fix neighbours. By Equation 29.1 at the order \(\alpha = \lambda + 1\), \(\mathbb E_P[e^{\lambda L}] = e^{\lambda D_\alpha(P\|Q)}\), and its logarithm is \(\lambda D_\alpha(P\|Q)\).
  2. Take the supremum. The factor \(\lambda > 0\) does not change which pair of neighbours is worst, so the supremum of the left side is \(K_M(\lambda)\) and that of the right side is \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_M(\alpha) = \lambda\,\varepsilon_M(\lambda + 1)\).

So both parts of Abadi et al.’s Theorem 2 are results of this chapter. Composability, \(K_M(\lambda) \le \sum_i K_{M_i}(\lambda)\), is Equation 29.11 at order \(\lambda + 1\) multiplied by \(\lambda\). The tail bound says that \(M\) is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP with \(\delta = \min_\lambda\exp\big(K_M(\lambda) - \lambda\varepsilon\big)\). This is the conversion of the next section, Section 29.6, solved for \(\delta\) at a fixed \(\varepsilon\). So the two accountants give the same numbers with different bookkeeping. What was new in 2016 was a bound on \(K_M(\lambda)\) for the subsampled Gaussian step of private stochastic gradient descent (SGD), called DP-SGD, in which each step uses a random fraction of the data. Amplification by subsampling derives it in RDP form.

Example 29.5 For randomized response with \(t = 0.75\) at \(\lambda = 1\), the moment \(\mathbb E_P[e^L] = 0.75 \times 3 + 0.25 \times \frac13\) has the same two terms, 2.2500 and 0.0833, as the last column of Table 29.2. So its logarithm is \(1 \cdot \varepsilon(2) = 0.8473\).

For the Gaussian pair \(P = \mathcal N(0, 1)\) and \(Q = \mathcal N(\mu, 1)\), the privacy loss under \(P\) is normal with mean \(\mu^2/2\) and variance \(\mu^2\) (Lemma 20.3). A normal variable with mean \(m\) and variance \(s^2\) has the cumulant generating function \(m\lambda + s^2\lambda^2/2\), so \[K(\lambda) = \lambda\frac{\mu^2}{2} + \frac{\lambda^2\mu^2}{2} = \lambda\cdot\frac{(\lambda + 1)\mu^2}{2} = \lambda\,\varepsilon(\lambda + 1), \tag{29.23}\] with \(\varepsilon(\alpha) = \alpha\mu^2/2\), as Equation 29.22 says, and a Monte Carlo average agrees (Section 29.7).

29.6 From RDP to (ε, δ)

After composition you hold a curve, but you report an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) pair, because that guarantee has a direct meaning for every event. Outputs whose privacy loss is at most some threshold \(c\) already satisfy the \(e^c\) bound, so only outputs with a larger loss can break it, and you can charge their total probability to \(\delta\). That probability is a tail of the privacy loss. The Chernoff bound of Theorem 2.3 in Concentration inequalities controls a tail with an exponential moment, which is what each point of the RDP curve gives: for any random variable \(X\), any level \(c\) and any \(\lambda > 0\), \(\Pr[X \ge c]\) is at most \(e^{-\lambda c}\,\mathbb E[e^{\lambda X}]\). The result is Mironov (2017), Proposition 3, and Section D.3 derives it from scratch.

Theorem 29.5 (From RDP to (ε, δ)-DP) Let \(M\) be \((\alpha, \varepsilon(\alpha))\)-RDP for some order \(\alpha > 1\). Then for every \(0 < \delta < 1\), \(M\) is \((\varepsilon_\delta, \delta)\)-DP with \[\varepsilon_\delta = \varepsilon(\alpha) + \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}. \tag{29.24}\] If you hold the whole curve, the best order gives \(\varepsilon_\delta = \min_\alpha\big[\varepsilon(\alpha) + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha - 1)\big]\).

Proof. The plan is to split every event into outputs with small loss, which satisfy the \(e^c\) bound, and outputs with large loss, whose probability is a tail, and then to bound the tail with Chernoff. Fix neighbours with privacy loss \(L\), and a threshold \(c\) to be chosen.

  1. Split the event. For any event \(S\), \(\Pr_P[S] = \Pr_P[S \cap \{L \le c\}] + \Pr_P[S \cap \{L > c\}]\).
  2. Small losses. On \(\{L \le c\}\) the definition of \(L\) gives \(p \le e^c q\). The integral over \(S \cap \{L \le c\}\) shows that the first term is at most \(e^c\Pr_Q[S]\).
  3. Large losses. The set \(S \cap \{L > c\}\) lies inside \(\{L > c\}\), so the second term is at most \(\Pr_P[L > c]\).
  4. Chernoff on the loss. Apply Theorem 2.3 to \(L\) under \(P\) with \(\lambda = \alpha - 1\): \[\Pr_P[L > c] \le e^{-(\alpha-1)c}\,\mathbb E_P\big[e^{(\alpha - 1)L}\big]. \tag{29.25}\] By Equation 29.1 the average is \(e^{(\alpha - 1)D_\alpha(P\|Q)}\), at most \(e^{(\alpha - 1)\varepsilon(\alpha)}\), so \[\Pr_P[L > c] \le e^{(\alpha - 1)(\varepsilon(\alpha) - c)}. \tag{29.26}\]
  5. Choose the threshold. Set \((\alpha - 1)(\varepsilon(\alpha) - c) = \ln\delta\), which gives \(c = \varepsilon(\alpha) + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha - 1)\). Then \(\Pr_P[S] \le e^c\Pr_Q[S] + \delta\) for every event and every pair of neighbours, which is \((c, \delta)\)-DP.

For the Gaussian curve the best order has a closed form. The curve grows with \(\alpha\) while the tail term falls, so the minimum is where the two balance.

Corollary 29.1 (The best order for a Gaussian curve) For the Gaussian curve \(\alpha/(2\sigma^2)\) and \(0 < \delta < 1\), the right side of Equation 29.24, over all real orders \(\alpha > 1\), is smallest at \[\alpha^* = 1 + \sigma\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta)}, \qquad \varepsilon_\delta = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta)}}{\sigma}. \tag{29.27}\]

Proof.

  1. Substitute. Write \(a = \alpha - 1 > 0\) and \(\ell = \ln(1/\delta)\). The Gaussian curve is \((a + 1)/(2\sigma^2)\), so Equation 29.24 of Theorem 29.5 becomes \(\varepsilon_\delta(a) = \frac{a + 1}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\ell}{a}\).
  2. Set the derivative to zero. The derivative \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{\ell}{a^2}\) is zero at \(a = \sigma\sqrt{2\ell}\). The function is a line plus \(\ell/a\), whose second derivative \(2\ell/a^3\) is positive, so this is the minimum.
  3. Evaluate. At that \(a\), both \(\frac{a}{2\sigma^2}\) and \(\frac{\ell}{a}\) equal \(\frac{\sqrt{2\ell}}{2\sigma}\), so they add to \(\sqrt{2\ell}/\sigma\). With the constant \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\), this is Equation 29.27.

The conversion loses in two places. Step 3 of the conversion’s proof charges the whole tail \(\Pr_P[L > c]\) to \(\delta\). But the exact smallest \(\delta\) at level \(c\) is the average \(\mathbb E_P\big[(1 - e^{c - L})_+\big]\) (Lemma 21.1), which charges an output with loss above \(c\) only the fraction \(1 - e^{c - L}\) of its probability. Step 4 then bounds the tail with a Chernoff bound, which overestimates it. From RDP to (ε, δ) and to tests removes much of both losses with sharper conversions.

The order trades the two terms against each other. Small orders make the curve small but the tail term \(\ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha - 1)\) large, and large orders do the opposite (Figure 29.4, left). A steeper composed curve makes large orders more expensive. So at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) one Gaussian step with \(\sigma = 20\) wants \(\alpha \approx 97\), while 1000 such steps want \(\alpha \approx 4\). So this part optimizes over one fixed grid that spans the range: 99 orders from 1.1 to 10.9 in steps of 0.1, the 52 integers from 12 to 63, and 128, 256 and 512, which is 154 orders:

ORDERS = [1 + x / 10 for x in range(1, 100)] + list(range(12, 64)) + [128, 256, 512]

Example 29.6 Take one Gaussian step with \(\sigma = 1\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). Here \(\ln(1/\delta) = 11.5129\) and \(\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta)} = 4.7985\). The curve is \(\alpha/2\), and Equation 29.24 adds \(11.5129/(\alpha - 1)\). At \(\alpha = 2\) the sum is \(1.0000 + 11.5129 = 12.5129\), mostly the tail term, and at \(\alpha = 20\) it is \(10.0000 + 0.6059 = 10.6059\), mostly the curve. The grid’s best order is 5.8, next to the continuous optimum \(\alpha^* = 1 + 4.7985 = 5.7985\), and both give \(\varepsilon = 5.2985\). The exact \(\varepsilon\) comes from the privacy profile of Theorem 20.3 with \(\mu = 1/\sigma = 1\). Bisection on \(\delta_\mu(\varepsilon) = 10^{-5}\) gives 4.3772, so the conversion is 21% too large (\(5.2985/4.3772 = 1.21\)).

Now the opening’s problem, \(k\) Gaussian steps with \(\sigma = 20\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\), which Table 29.7 accounts for in four ways. The exact value is known because \(k\) steps are one Gaussian with \(\mu = \sqrt k/\sigma\). The two routes of Part 3 turn each step into one point \((\varepsilon_0, \delta_0)\) of its exact curve and compose the points. Basic composition (Theorem 19.3) spends \(\delta/k\) per step and reports \(k\varepsilon_0\). Advanced composition in its sharp form (Equation 21.17) spends a fraction \(s\) of \(\delta\) on the per-step slacks and the rest on the tail of the summed loss, with the best \(s\) on Part 3’s grid.

Advanced composition is 5 to 9 times the exact value, and it gets looser as \(k\) grows, because each step’s curve became one point before composition. RDP is 18% to 57% above exact, and it gets tighter as \(k\) grows. The grid costs something only at \(k = 1\), 0.251 against 0.241 at the continuous optimum 97.0, because the grid jumps from 63 to 128. The rest of the gap is the conversion’s slack, which depends on the composed \(\mu = \sqrt k/\sigma\). At the continuous optimum, Mironov’s conversion is 1.07 times exact at \(\mu = 10\), 1.21 at \(\mu = 1\), 1.42 at \(\mu = 0.1\) and 1.76 at \(\mu = 0.01\). So the most private composed mechanisms, with small \(\mu\), pay the most.

The running example of the later chapters runs 10,000 steps of DP-SGD with \(\sigma = 1\). If each step used the whole dataset, even the exact guarantee at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) would be \(\varepsilon = 5425.5\), which is no guarantee. DP-SGD is private only because each step touches a random 1% of the data, and Amplification by subsampling computes the curve of such a step.
