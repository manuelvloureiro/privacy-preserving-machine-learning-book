23  Beyond global sensitivity

23.1 Why this matters

The Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms answer a numeric query \(f\) by adding random noise to the true answer, and they set the size of that noise by the global sensitivity: the most that changing one record can move \(f\), looking at every pair of neighbouring datasets that could exist, not just at yours. In symbols, \(\Delta f = \max_{D \simeq D'}|f(D) - f(D')|\), where \(D \simeq D'\) means the two datasets are neighbours. For counts and bounded sums this is the right scale: one person changes a count by at most 1, whatever the data. For many other statistics it is hopeless, for three different reasons.

The first is that the worst case can be absurd. The median of ages in \([0, 100]\) has global sensitivity 100. To see it, take a dataset in which half the people are 0 and half are 100, with one more person at 0 so that the median is 0. Replacing that one person by a 100-year-old moves the median from one end to the other. So the Laplace mechanism must add noise of scale \(100/\varepsilon\), which swamps any real answer, even though no realistic dataset of ages looks like this. The second is that the worst case can be infinite. The mean of unbounded data (incomes, session lengths, gradient norms) can be moved arbitrarily far by one record, because that record can be arbitrarily large, so no amount of Laplace noise makes it private. The third is that the worst case can be unknown. For a statistic you can only compute, such as an estimator fitted by an algorithm, nobody knows how to bound how far one record moves it.

Yet on the dataset you actually hold, the median of 1,001 ages typically moves by a tenth of a year when one person changes, because with that many people the ages near the middle are packed tightly together. That is the local sensitivity, and it is hundreds of times smaller than the global one. This chapter asks: can you add noise on the scale of the data you have, rather than the worst dataset there could be, and still satisfy differential privacy (DP)?

Recall what DP asks for. A randomized mechanism \(M\) is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP if, for every pair of neighbouring datasets \(D \simeq D'\) and every set \(S\) of outputs, \(\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^\varepsilon\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta\): one record can change the probability of any outcome by at most a factor \(e^\varepsilon\), up to a small slack \(\delta\). With \(\delta = 0\) this is pure \(\varepsilon\)-DP. The answer to the question is yes, with one idea that organizes the whole chapter:

The noise scale is itself a function of the data, so it must be private too.

If you calibrate noise to the local sensitivity of your dataset, the size of the noise reveals something about the dataset: an observer who sees a release with very wide noise learns that the data were of the kind that has a large local sensitivity. That is enough to break the guarantee. Each technique of this chapter repairs this in a different way:

  • Smooth sensitivity replaces the local sensitivity by an upper bound that changes slowly between neighbours, so that the noise scale leaks little.
  • Propose-test-release proposes a noise scale, and privately tests whether the dataset is far from any dataset where that scale would be too small.
  • Subsample-and-aggregate makes the sensitivity small by construction: run the statistic on disjoint blocks and privately combine the block answers, each of which one record can affect only once.
  • Clipping forces bounded sensitivity on unbounded data, at the price of a bias that you trade against the noise.

Throughout, datasets have a fixed size \(n\) and \(D \simeq D'\) means that \(D'\) is obtained from \(D\) by replacing one record (the bounded relation of The definition of differential privacy). The Hamming distance \(d(D, D')\) counts the records in which two datasets differ, so neighbours are datasets at distance 1, and \(d(D, D'') = k\) means you can walk from \(D\) to \(D''\) by changing one record at a time in \(k\) steps. Every proof below compares the output densities \(p_{M(D)}\) and \(p_{M(D')}\) of a mechanism on two neighbours, point by point. The natural quantity for that is the log of their ratio at an output \(y\), the privacy loss: it says how much more likely \(y\) is under \(D\) than under \(D'\), and pure \(\varepsilon\)-DP holds when it never exceeds \(\varepsilon\). In symbols, \(L_{D,D'}(y) = \ln\frac{p_{M(D)}(y)}{p_{M(D')}(y)}\).

23.2 Toolkit: four facts every derivation uses

23.2.1 Fact 1 — The Laplace mechanism with global sensitivity

If one record never moves \(f\) by more than \(\Delta\), then adding Laplace noise of scale \(\Delta/\varepsilon\) is enough. Formally, if \(|f(D) - f(D')| \le \Delta\) for all \(D \simeq D'\), then \(f(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon)\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. This is the Laplace mechanism, and the reason is short. The Laplace density with centre \(\mu\) and scale \(b\) is \(\frac1{2b}e^{-|y - \mu|/b}\), so with \(b = \Delta/\varepsilon\) the normalizing constants cancel in the ratio of the two densities, and the privacy loss is \(\big(|y - f(D')| - |y - f(D)|\big)\varepsilon/\Delta\). The triangle inequality says \(|y - f(D')| \le |y - f(D)| + |f(D) - f(D')|\), so the bracket is at most \(|f(D) - f(D')| \le \Delta\), and the loss is at most \(\varepsilon\) at every \(y\). A density that is at most \(e^\varepsilon\) times another at every point has at most \(e^\varepsilon\) times its probability on every set (integrate over the set), which is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Every technique in this chapter ends by applying this fact, or a cousin of it, to something whose sensitivity is bounded.

23.2.2 Fact 2 — A tail bound on the privacy loss gives \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)

Some mechanisms in this chapter have a privacy loss that is at most \(\varepsilon\) for almost every output, but not for all of them. This fact says that is enough for \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, with \(\delta\) the probability of the rare outputs where the loss is too large. For every set \(S\) of outputs, \[\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^\varepsilon \Pr[M(D') \in S] + \Pr_{y \sim M(D)}\big[L_{D,D'}(y) > \varepsilon\big]. \tag{23.1}\]

The proof splits \(S\) into the outputs where the loss is small and those where it is large: \(S_1 = S \cap \{L \le \varepsilon\}\) and \(S_2 = S \cap \{L > \varepsilon\}\). On \(S_1\) the loss is at most \(\varepsilon\), which says exactly that the densities satisfy \(p_{M(D)} \le e^\varepsilon p_{M(D')}\) there, so integrating over \(S_1\) gives \(\Pr[M(D) \in S_1] \le e^\varepsilon\Pr[M(D') \in S_1] \le e^\varepsilon\Pr[M(D') \in S]\). On \(S_2\) you give up comparing and just bound the probability: \(\Pr[M(D) \in S_2]\) is at most the probability that the loss exceeds \(\varepsilon\) at all, \(\Pr_{M(D)}[L > \varepsilon]\). Adding the two pieces gives Equation 23.1.

So if the privacy loss exceeds \(\varepsilon\) only with probability \(\delta\), the mechanism is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP. This “bad event” argument is behind every \(\delta\) in this chapter.

23.2.3 Fact 3 — Distances to a set have sensitivity 1

A question of the form “how many records would have to change before something bad happens?” has an answer that one record can change by at most 1. Formally, for any set \(\mathcal B\) of datasets (the “bad” ones), let \(\operatorname{dist}(D, \mathcal B) = \min_{D'' \in \mathcal B} d(D, D'')\) be the number of changes needed to reach the nearest of them. Then \(|\operatorname{dist}(D, \mathcal B) - \operatorname{dist}(D', \mathcal B)| \le 1\) for \(D \simeq D'\).

The reason is that a path from \(D'\) to a bad dataset can always go through \(D\) first, at the cost of one extra step. In terms of the Hamming distance, which satisfies the triangle inequality, \(d(D', D'') \le d(D', D) + d(D, D'') = 1 + d(D, D'')\) for every \(D''\). Minimizing both sides over \(D'' \in \mathcal B\) gives \(\operatorname{dist}(D', \mathcal B) \le 1 + \operatorname{dist}(D, \mathcal B)\), and the same argument with \(D\) and \(D'\) exchanged gives the other direction. So such a count can be released with \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) noise by Fact 1. Propose-test-release is built on it.

23.2.4 Fact 4 — Changing \(k\) records moves each order statistic by at most \(k\) places

If you change \(k\) records, the \(j\)-th smallest value can move, but only as far as the \((j-k)\)-th or \((j+k)\)-th smallest value of the original data. To state it, sort the values of \(D\) as \(x_1 \le \dots \le x_n\) (the order statistics), all in \([0, \Lambda]\). Ranks \(j - k\) or \(j + k\) can fall outside \(1, \dots, n\), so pad with \(x_i = 0\) for \(i \le 0\) and \(x_i = \Lambda\) for \(i > n\): “below the smallest” means “possibly as low as 0”, and “above the largest” means “possibly as high as \(\Lambda\)”. If \(D''\) differs from \(D\) in at most \(k\) records and has sorted values \(y_1 \le \dots \le y_n\), then \[x_{j-k} \le y_j \le x_{j+k} \quad\text{for every } j. \tag{23.2}\]

For the upper bound, count the values below \(x_{j+k}\). At least \(j + k\) values of \(D\) are \(\le x_{j+k}\) (when \(j + k > n\) the padding sets \(x_{j+k} = \Lambda\), which bounds every value, so the claim is trivially true). Changing \(k\) records removes at most \(k\) of them, so at least \(j\) values of \(D''\) are still \(\le x_{j+k}\), and that says the \(j\)-th smallest value of \(D''\) is at most \(x_{j+k}\): \(y_j \le x_{j+k}\). The lower bound is the same argument counting values above \(x_{j-k}\). This fact pins down how far the median of a modified dataset can travel.

23.3 Local sensitivity, and why it leaks

23.3.1 Context

Global sensitivity takes a maximum over all pairs of neighbours, including pathological ones that look nothing like your data. The natural fix is to take the maximum only over the neighbours of the dataset you hold. That gives a much smaller number. But adding noise proportional to it is not private, and seeing exactly why is the key to everything else in this chapter.

23.3.2 Statement

The local sensitivity of \(f\) at \(D\) is the most that one record can move \(f\) starting from \(D\) itself, rather than from the worst dataset: \[\mathrm{LS}_f(D) = \max_{D':\ d(D, D') = 1}|f(D) - f(D')|, \tag{23.3}\] and the global sensitivity is its worst case over all datasets, \(\Delta f = \max_D \mathrm{LS}_f(D)\).

For the median, one change can push the median at most one place up or down the sorted list, so its local sensitivity is the larger of the two gaps next to it. Take \(n\) values in \([0, \Lambda]\), with \(n\) odd and \(m = (n+1)/2\) the rank of the median, so that the median is \(x_m\). Then \[\mathrm{LS}_{\mathrm{med}}(D) = \max\big(x_m - x_{m-1},\ x_{m+1} - x_m\big), \qquad \Delta\,\mathrm{med} = \Lambda. \tag{23.4}\]

23.3.3 Derivation

  1. The median of a neighbour lies between \(x_{m-1}\) and \(x_{m+1}\). This is Fact 4 with \(k = 1\) change and rank \(j = m\). So the median moves from \(x_m\) by at most the larger of the gaps to its two sorted neighbours: \(|\mathrm{med}(D') - x_m| \le \max(x_m - x_{m-1}, x_{m+1} - x_m)\).
  2. Both ends are reached. To show the bound is attained, move the median value itself to an extreme. Replace \(x_m\) by \(\Lambda\): the sorted values become \(x_1, \dots, x_{m-1}, x_{m+1}, \dots, x_n, \Lambda\), and the \(m\)-th of them is \(x_{m+1}\). Replace \(x_m\) by 0 instead, and everything above it keeps its rank while the new 0 goes first, so the \(m\)-th value becomes \(x_{m-1}\). So the bound of step 1 is attained, which is Equation 23.4.
  3. The global sensitivity is \(\Lambda\). Take \(m\) values equal to 0 and \(m - 1\) equal to \(\Lambda\). Then \(x_m = 0\) and \(x_{m+1} = \Lambda\), so Equation 23.4 gives \(\Lambda\), and no dataset can do worse because every value lies in \([0, \Lambda]\), so no gap exceeds \(\Lambda\).

23.3.4 Interpretation: the noise scale is a statistic

Consider the mechanism that adds Laplace noise scaled to the local sensitivity of the data it is given, \(M_{\mathrm{LS}}(D) = f(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(\mathrm{LS}_f(D)/\varepsilon)\). Its noise scale is the function \(\mathrm{LS}_f\) of the data, and \(\mathrm{LS}_f\) itself can have large sensitivity: one record can change the local sensitivity of the median from 0 to \(\Lambda\). The mechanism then releases \(f(D)\) with no noise on one dataset and with enormous noise on its neighbour, and the noise level alone tells the two apart, even when \(f\) is the same on both.

The degenerate case makes this stark (Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith 2007, Example 1). With \(n = 5\) and \(\Lambda = 100\), the dataset \(D = (0, 0, 0, 0, 100)\) has median 0, and both gaps next to the median are 0, so \(\mathrm{LS} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{LS}}(D) = 0\) exactly. Its neighbour \(D' = (0, 0, 0, 100, 100)\) also has median 0, but the gap above the median is now 100, so \(\mathrm{LS} = 100\) and \(M_{\mathrm{LS}}(D')\) has a continuous distribution, which equals 0 with probability 0. The event “the output is exactly 0” has probability 1 under \(D\) and 0 under \(D'\). No finite \(\varepsilon\) and no \(\delta < 1\) cover that, because the DP inequality would need \(1 \le e^\varepsilon \cdot 0 + \delta\).

23.3.5 Example: two neighbours with the same median

The leak does not need a degenerate dataset. Take \(\Lambda = 100\), \(\varepsilon = 1\), and the two datasets of Table 23.2, which differ in one record and have the same median, but whose local sensitivities differ by a factor of six.

The two releases are \(30 + \mathrm{Lap}(10)\) and \(30 + \mathrm{Lap}(60)\). They have the same centre, so the privacy loss depends only on the distance \(u = |y - 30|\) from it. Far from the centre the wide density is much larger than the narrow one, because the narrow one decays six times faster, so the loss should grow with \(u\). Writing the two Laplace densities \(\frac1{2b}e^{-u/b}\) with \(b = 60\) and \(b = 10\) and taking the log of their ratio: \[L_{D',D}(y) = \ln\frac{e^{-u/60}/120}{e^{-u/10}/20} = -\ln 6 + u\Big(\frac1{10} - \frac1{60}\Big) = -1.792 + \frac{u}{12}. \tag{23.5}\] The constant is \(\ln(20/120) = -\ln 6 = -1.792\), and the slope is \(\frac{6 - 1}{60} = \frac1{12}\). The loss grows without bound in \(u\) (Figure 23.1), so no pure \(\varepsilon\) can hold. It exceeds \(\varepsilon = 1\) once \(-\ln 6 + u/12 > 1\), that is once \(u > 12(1 + \ln 6) = 33.50\). A Laplace variable of scale \(b\) lands more than \(u\) from its centre with probability \(e^{-u/b}\), so this happens with probability \(e^{-33.5/60} = 0.572\) under \(D'\) and \(e^{-33.5/10} = 0.0351\) under \(D\).

How large must \(\delta\) be for this pair? The smallest \(\delta\) for which the pair satisfies the \((1, \delta)\) inequality is the hockey-stick divergence of Divergences: the largest gap \(\Pr[M(D') \in S] - e^{1}\Pr[M(D) \in S]\) over all sets \(S\), which is attained by taking \(S\) to be exactly the outputs where the loss exceeds 1. The script computes it as \(0.477\) in the direction \(D'\) against \(D\) (and \(0.215\) in the other direction). An adversary who sees a value more than 34 away from 30 can bet on \(D'\) and be right far more often than any reasonable \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) allows. This is the distinguishing game of Threat models and privacy games, in which an adversary who knows both candidate datasets sees one release and guesses which dataset produced it. Here it wins by looking at the spread of the noise, not its centre.

23.3.6 Check yourself

  • Is \(\mathrm{LS}_f(D) \le \Delta f\) always, and when are they equal? (Always, since the global sensitivity is the maximum of the local one over all datasets. They are equal at the datasets where that maximum is attained, such as half zeros and half \(\Lambda\) for the median.)
  • Why doesn’t the Laplace proof of Fact 1 go through for \(M_{\mathrm{LS}}\)? (It needs the same scale \(\Delta\) on both sides. With scales \(b \ne b'\) the loss contains \(|y - f(D')|/b' - |y - f(D)|/b\), whose slopes no longer cancel, so it is unbounded in \(y\).)

23.4 Smooth sensitivity

23.4.1 Context

The leak came from the noise scale jumping between neighbours. So use a noise scale that cannot jump: an upper bound on the local sensitivity whose logarithm changes by at most \(\beta\) between neighbours, so that the scale itself changes by at most a factor \(e^\beta\). A change by a bounded factor is the right kind of control, because a privacy loss is a log-ratio: rescaling the noise by \(e^\beta\) shifts log-densities by about \(\beta\). Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith (2007) call such a bound smooth, find the smallest one, and show which noise distributions can be scaled by it. Two questions need answers: how to compute the bound, and which noise makes the result private.

23.4.2 Statement

A function \(S\) is a \(\beta\)-smooth upper bound on \(\mathrm{LS}_f\) if it is never below the local sensitivity and changes by at most a factor \(e^\beta\) between neighbours: \[S(D) \ge \mathrm{LS}_f(D) \quad\text{and}\quad S(D) \le e^\beta S(D') \quad\text{for all } D \simeq D'. \tag{23.6}\] The smallest such bound has an explicit form. If some dataset \(D''\) at distance \(k\) has a large local sensitivity, a smooth bound cannot be small at \(D\), because it can only decrease by a factor \(e^{-\beta}\) per step on the way from \(D''\) to \(D\). So the bound at \(D\) must be at least \(\mathrm{LS}_f(D'')e^{-\beta k}\) for every \(D''\), and the \(\beta\)-smooth sensitivity takes exactly the largest of these: \[S^*_{f,\beta}(D) = \max_{D''}\ \mathrm{LS}_f(D'')\,e^{-\beta\,d(D, D'')} \tag{23.7}\] It is a \(\beta\)-smooth upper bound, and it is the smallest one: \(S^*_{f,\beta}(D) \le S(D)\) for every \(\beta\)-smooth upper bound \(S\).

The mechanism adds noise from a fixed shape, stretched by \(S(D)\): \[M(D) = f(D) + \frac{S(D)}{\alpha}\,Z, \tag{23.8}\] where \(Z\) is drawn from a fixed density \(h\) and \(\alpha > 0\) is a constant that sets how many noise scales one record’s shift is worth. Which \(h\) work is the content of the next two results. They differ in how heavy the tail of \(h\) is, and the derivation will show why that matters: the noise scale differs between neighbours, and a light-tailed density compared with a stretched copy of itself has a log-ratio that grows without bound in the tail, as in the example of Table 23.2.

Pure DP with Cauchy-like noise. Let \(h_\gamma(z) \propto 1/(1 + |z|^\gamma)\) with \(\gamma > 1\), a density whose tails decay like the power \(|z|^{-\gamma}\) (\(\gamma = 2\) is the standard Cauchy). If \(S\) is a \(\beta\)-smooth upper bound and the sources of privacy loss together fit within the budget (the shift of the centre is worth \(\gamma\alpha\), and the change of scale is worth \(\beta\) through the density’s normalizing factor plus \(\gamma\beta\) through the stretch of its shape), \[\gamma\alpha + (\gamma + 1)\beta \le \varepsilon, \tag{23.9}\] then Equation 23.8 is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith (2007, Lemma 2.7) use \(\alpha = \beta = \varepsilon/(2(\gamma + 1))\), which satisfies Equation 23.9 with room to spare. For the Cauchy, \(\gamma = 2\) gives \(\alpha = \beta = \varepsilon/6\), the often-quoted release \(f(D) + \frac{6}{\varepsilon}S^*_{f,\varepsilon/6}(D)\cdot Z\).

Approximate DP with Laplace noise. Let \(Z \sim \mathrm{Lap}(1)\), the Laplace density \(\frac12e^{-|z|}\). Its tail is too light for pure DP, so the condition gains a term that charges for the rare draws of size above \(\ln(1/\delta)\). If \(S\) is a \(\beta\)-smooth upper bound and \[\beta + e^\beta\alpha + (e^\beta - 1)\ln(1/\delta) \le \varepsilon, \tag{23.10}\] then Equation 23.8 is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP. Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith (2007, Lemma 2.9) prove a version with a slightly different split of the budget, \(\alpha = \varepsilon/2\) and \(\beta = \varepsilon/(2\ln(2/\delta))\). Because the third term is roughly \(\beta\ln(1/\delta)\) for small \(\beta\), here \(\beta\) must shrink like \(1/\ln(1/\delta)\), and no \(\beta > 0\) gives pure DP with Laplace noise.

23.4.3 Derivation

\(S^*\) is a smooth upper bound, and the smallest one.

  1. Upper bound. One of the datasets in the maximum of Equation 23.7 is \(D'' = D\) itself, at distance 0, which contributes \(\mathrm{LS}_f(D)e^0 = \mathrm{LS}_f(D)\). A maximum is at least each of its terms, so \(S^*_{f,\beta}(D) \ge \mathrm{LS}_f(D)\).
  2. Smooth. Moving from \(D\) to a neighbour \(D'\) makes every dataset at most one step further away: \(d(D', D'') \le d(D, D'') + 1\), by the triangle inequality of Fact 3. So each term \(\mathrm{LS}_f(D'')e^{-\beta d(D', D'')}\) of \(S^*(D')\) is at least \(e^{-\beta}\) times the term for the same \(D''\) in \(S^*(D)\). Taking the maximum over \(D''\) on both sides, \(S^*(D') \ge e^{-\beta}S^*(D)\). Nothing in this argument depended on which of the two neighbours was called \(D\), so running it with \(D\) and \(D'\) exchanged gives \(S^*(D) \ge e^{-\beta}S^*(D')\), the smoothness condition.
  3. Smallest. This is the argument that motivated the definition. Let \(S\) be any \(\beta\)-smooth upper bound and \(D''\) any dataset at distance \(k\) from \(D\). Walk from \(D''\) to \(D\) through \(k\) neighbours; smoothness lets \(S\) drop by at most a factor \(e^{-\beta}\) per step, so \(S(D) \ge e^{-\beta k}S(D'')\), and \(S(D'') \ge \mathrm{LS}_f(D'')\) because \(S\) is an upper bound. So \(S(D) \ge e^{-\beta k}\mathrm{LS}_f(D'')\) for every \(D''\); maximize over \(D''\).

Pure DP for Cauchy-like noise. The plan is to write the privacy loss at an output \(y\) as a sum of three pieces, one for each way the two output distributions differ (their scales differ, their centres differ, and the noise draw that explains \(y\) is stretched), and to bound each piece by smoothness or by the upper-bound property.

  1. Write out the privacy loss. Stretching \(Z\) by \(S(D)/\alpha\) divides its density by that factor, so the density of \(M(D)\) at \(y\) is \(\frac{\alpha}{S(D)}h\big(z\big)\), where \(z = \alpha\frac{y - f(D)}{S(D)}\) is the noise draw that would produce \(y\) from \(D\). Likewise that of \(M(D')\) is \(\frac{\alpha}{S(D')}h(z')\) with \(z' = \alpha\frac{y - f(D')}{S(D')}\). To compare the two draws, substitute \(y = f(D) + S(D)z/\alpha\) into \(z'\): \(z' = \frac{S(D)}{S(D')}\big(z + \alpha\frac{f(D) - f(D')}{S(D)}\big)\). That is, \(z'\) is \(z\) shifted and then stretched: \[z' = e^{\lambda}(z + \Delta), \qquad e^\lambda = \frac{S(D)}{S(D')},\quad \Delta = \alpha\,\frac{f(D) - f(D')}{S(D)}. \tag{23.11}\] (Here \(\Delta\) is this shift, not the global sensitivity.) The log of the ratio of the two densities splits into the ratio of the prefactors and the ratio of \(h\) at \(z\) and at \(z'\), and inserting \(h(z + \Delta)\) in the middle splits the second part into the shift and the stretch: \[L_{D,D'}(y) = \underbrace{\ln\frac{S(D')}{S(D)}}_{\text{scale}} + \underbrace{\ln\frac{h(z)}{h(z + \Delta)}}_{\text{sliding}} + \underbrace{\ln\frac{h(z + \Delta)}{h(e^\lambda(z + \Delta))}}_{\text{dilation}}. \tag{23.12}\]
  2. Size of the shift and the stretch. Smoothness says \(S(D)/S(D')\) lies between \(e^{-\beta}\) and \(e^\beta\), so \(|\lambda| \le \beta\). And one record moves \(f\) by at most the local sensitivity, which is at most \(S(D)\): \(|f(D) - f(D')| \le \mathrm{LS}_f(D) \le S(D)\), so \(|\Delta| \le \alpha\). This is the only place where “\(S\) is an upper bound on the local sensitivity” enters.
  3. Scale term: at most \(\beta\), by smoothness again.
  4. Sliding term. The question is how fast \(-\ln h\) can change when its argument shifts by \(|\Delta|\). Write \(\varphi(u) = \ln(1 + u^\gamma)\) for \(u \ge 0\), so \(-\ln h_\gamma(z) = \varphi(|z|) + \text{const}\). Its slope is \(\varphi'(u) = \gamma u^{\gamma-1}/(1 + u^\gamma) \le \gamma\), because \(u^{\gamma-1} \le 1 + u^\gamma\) (if \(u \le 1\) the left side is at most 1; if \(u > 1\) it is below \(u^\gamma\)). The mean value theorem says a function whose slope is at most \(\gamma\) in size changes by at most \(\gamma\) times the change in its argument. The argument changes from \(|z|\) to \(|z + \Delta|\), and these differ by at most \(|\Delta|\) (the reverse triangle inequality). So \(|\varphi(|z + \Delta|) - \varphi(|z|)| \le \gamma\big||z + \Delta| - |z|\big| \le \gamma|\Delta| \le \gamma\alpha\).
  5. Dilation term. Here the question is how much \(h\) changes when its argument is stretched by \(e^\lambda\). With \(w = |z + \Delta|\) and \(\lambda \ge 0\), \(\frac{h(w)}{h(e^\lambda w)} = \frac{1 + e^{\lambda\gamma}w^\gamma}{1 + w^\gamma} \le e^{\lambda\gamma}\), since \((1 + ca)/(1 + a) \le c\) for \(c \ge 1\) (multiply out: \(1 + ca \le c + ca\)). This bound holds for every \(w\), however far in the tail, and that is what the power-law tail buys. For \(\lambda < 0\) the stretch moves the argument towards 0, where \(h\) is larger, so the ratio is at most 1. Either way the term is at most \(\gamma|\lambda| \le \gamma\beta\).
  6. Add up: \(L_{D,D'}(y) \le \beta + \gamma\alpha + \gamma\beta\) for every \(y\). That is \(\gamma\alpha + (\gamma + 1)\beta\), so under Equation 23.9 it is at most \(\varepsilon\), which is pure \(\varepsilon\)-DP. With \(\alpha = \beta = \varepsilon/6\) and \(\gamma = 2\) the bound is \(\gamma\alpha + (\gamma + 1)\beta = \frac{2}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = 0.8333\,\varepsilon\).

Approximate DP for Laplace noise. Steps 1–3 are unchanged. For \(h(z) = \frac12e^{-|z|}\) the log of a ratio of \(h\) values is just a difference of absolute values, so the sliding and dilation terms combine into one. Using \(|e^\lambda(z + \Delta)| \le e^\lambda(|z| + |\Delta|)\), then \(e^\lambda \le e^\beta\) and \(|\Delta| \le \alpha\) from step 2: \[\ln\frac{h(z)}{h(z')} = |e^\lambda(z + \Delta)| - |z| \le e^\lambda(|z| + |\Delta|) - |z| \le (e^\beta - 1)|z| + e^\beta\alpha. \tag{23.13}\] Adding the scale term, \(L_{D,D'}(y) \le \beta + e^\beta\alpha + (e^\beta - 1)|z|\), where \(z\) is the noise actually drawn, \(Z \sim \mathrm{Lap}(1)\). This is not bounded: the stretch makes the Laplace log-density change by an amount proportional to \(|z|\), so far in the tail the dilation term wins. But large draws are rare. A \(\mathrm{Lap}(1)\) variable exceeds \(t\) in absolute value with probability \(e^{-t}\) (integrate the two tails of \(\frac12e^{-|z|}\); see Concentration inequalities), so \(\Pr[|Z| > \ln(1/\delta)] = \delta\). Outside that event of probability \(\delta\), \(|z| \le \ln(1/\delta)\) and the loss is at most the left side of Equation 23.10, hence at most \(\varepsilon\). Fact 2 turns that into \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP.

23.4.4 Interpretation: why heavy tails, and what admissibility means

Pure DP forces polynomial tails. The dilation step needed \(h(w)/h(e^\lambda w)\) to be bounded for all \(w\), however large. Write \(h(w) \approx e^{-g(w)}\); the condition says \(g(e^\beta w) - g(w)\) stays below some constant \(c\). Applying it \(k\) times from \(w = 1\) gives \(g(e^{k\beta}) \le g(1) + kc\), so \(g\) can grow at most like \(\frac{c}{\beta}\ln w\), and \(h\) can decay at most like a power of \(w\): Cauchy-like. Laplace noise has \(g(w) = w\), for which \(g(e^\beta w) - g(w) = (e^\beta - 1)w\) grows without bound, and the Gaussian is worse. That is why both fail for pure DP and pay with \(\delta\).

The two conditions are what Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith call admissibility. They call \(h\) \((\alpha, \beta)\)-admissible when shifting it by at most \(\alpha\) (sliding) and rescaling it by at most a factor \(e^{\pm\beta}\) (dilation) each change probabilities by at most a factor \(e^{\varepsilon/2}\) plus \(\delta/2\). The derivation above bounds the two together, which gives slightly different constants.

The price of heavy tails shows up in the error. The Cauchy has no mean, so its error is reported through the median. For a standard Cauchy, \(\Pr[|Z| \le t] = \frac2\pi\arctan t\), which is \(\frac12\) at \(t = 1\) because \(\arctan 1 = \pi/4\). So \(|Z|\) has median 1, and the typical error is the noise scale \(S/\alpha\). But the tail is heavy: \(\Pr[|Z| > 10] = 1 - \frac2\pi\arctan 10 = 0.0635\), so about one release in 16 is off by more than ten times the typical error. With Laplace noise the same probability is \(e^{-10} \approx 4.5\times10^{-5}\).

Finally, \(\beta\) is a lever. A small \(\beta\) makes \(S^*\) larger, because the factor \(e^{-\beta k}\) discounts distant datasets less and so lets more of them raise the maximum. In exchange it costs less privacy per unit of noise. Laplace noise needs \(\beta \approx \varepsilon/(2\ln(1/\delta))\), much smaller than the Cauchy’s \(\varepsilon/6\), because in Equation 23.10 the change of scale is charged once for every unit of \(\ln(1/\delta)\).

23.4.5 Computing \(S^*\) for the median

Equation 23.7 is a maximum over all datasets, which is not something you can enumerate. The way out is to group the datasets by their distance \(k\) from \(D\): all datasets at distance \(k\) get the same discount \(e^{-k\beta}\), so within a group you only need the largest local sensitivity, \(A^{(k)}(D)\), and then the best discounted group: \[S^*_{\beta}(D) = \max_{k = 0, \dots, n} e^{-k\beta}A^{(k)}(D), \qquad A^{(k)}(D) = \max_{D'':\ d(D, D'') \le k}\mathrm{LS}(D''). \tag{23.14}\] (Using “\(\le k\)” instead of “\(= k\)” changes nothing: a dataset at distance \(j < k\) is counted again with the smaller weight \(e^{-k\beta}\), which never beats its own weight \(e^{-j\beta}\).)

For the median, \(A^{(k)}\) asks: by changing \(k\) records, how wide a gap can you open next to the median? A gap is opened by moving the values that lie inside an interval of the sorted data out of it, to 0 or to \(\Lambda\). With \(k\) changes you can empty out the \(k\) values between two order statistics \(k + 1\) ranks apart, and you can choose how many to move down and how many up so that the emptied interval ends up next to the median. So the answer is the widest such window that sits close enough to rank \(m\). With the padding of Fact 4, Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith (2007, Proposition 3.4) show \[A^{(k)}(D) = \max_{0 \le t \le k+1}\big(x_{m+t} - x_{m+t-k-1}\big). \tag{23.15}\] Each window runs from rank \(m + t - k - 1\) to rank \(m + t\), and \(t\) slides it across the median. Each \(A^{(k)}\) costs \(O(k)\) to evaluate, one subtraction per window, and \(k\) never needs to exceed \(n\): at \(k = n\) the window with \(t = m\) spans \(x_0 = 0\) to \(x_{n+1} = \Lambda\), so \(A^{(n)} = \Lambda\), the largest possible value, and larger \(k\) only lowers the weight. So the exact \(S^*_\beta\) takes \(O(n^2)\) time. (An \(O(n\log n)\) algorithm exists; the script uses the plain formula.)

Derivation of Equation 23.15. Steps 1–4 show that no dataset within distance \(k\) has a gap next to its median wider than the widest window, and step 5 shows that every window is achieved.

  1. What the gap looks like. Let \(D''\) be within distance \(k\) of \(D\), with sorted values \(y_i\). By Equation 23.4 its local sensitivity is one of its two gaps next to the median. Take the upper one, \((y_m, y_{m+1})\). The lower one needs no separate argument: reflecting every value, \(x \mapsto \Lambda - x\), turns lower gaps into upper gaps and maps the set of windows in Equation 23.15 to itself.
  2. The gap contains at most \(k\) original values. Assume the gap is positive (otherwise there is nothing to bound), so exactly \(m\) values of \(D''\) are \(\le y_m\). No value of \(D''\) lies strictly inside \((y_m, y_{m+1})\), so every value of \(D\) strictly inside it must have been one of the \(k\) changed records. Call the values of \(D\) inside the gap \(x_{a+1}, \dots, x_{a+j}\) with \(j \le k\). Then \(x_a \le y_m\) and \(x_{a+j+1} \ge y_{m+1}\), so the gap is at most \(x_{a+j+1} - x_a\), a window of the original data.
  3. The gap sits near rank \(m\). Now pin down \(a\) by counting values of \(D''\) at or below \(y_m\), of which there are exactly \(m\). Each is either an unchanged value of \(D\), and those are among \(x_1, \dots, x_a\) (everything above \(x_a\) is inside the gap or beyond it), or one of at most \(k\) new values. So \(m \le a + k\), that is \(a \ge m - k\). In the other direction, at least \(a - (k - j)\) of \(x_1, \dots, x_a\) are unchanged (the \(j\) values inside the gap used up \(j\) of the \(k\) changes), and all of them are \(\le y_m\), so \(a - (k - j) \le m\), that is \(a \le m + k - j\).
  4. Fit it in a window. Let \(i = \min(a, m)\), the lower end of the window to use. If \(a \le m\), the window \([x_a, x_{a+k+1}]\) has \(i = a \ge m - k\), so it is one of the windows of Equation 23.15 (\(t = a + k + 1 - m \in [0, k+1]\)), and it contains \([x_a, x_{a+j+1}]\) because \(j \le k\). If \(a > m\), the window \([x_m, x_{m+k+1}]\) (\(t = k + 1\)) starts below \(x_a\) and contains \([x_a, x_{a+j+1}]\) because \(a + j + 1 \le m + k + 1\) by step 3. Either way the gap is at most the right side of Equation 23.15.
  5. Every window is reached. For a window \([x_\ell, x_u]\) with \(\ell = m + t - k - 1\) and \(u = m + t\), there are exactly \(u - \ell - 1 = k\) values strictly between its ends. Change them: move \(k + 1 - t\) of them to 0 and the rest to \(\Lambda\) (for \(t = 0\), move all \(k\) to 0). Each value moved to 0 now sits below \(x_\ell\) and raises its rank by one. So \(x_\ell\) has rank \(\ell + (k + 1 - t) = m\) and \(x_u\) rank \(m + 1\) for \(t \ge 1\) (for \(t = 0\), ranks \(m - 1\) and \(m\)). Either way the median sits at one end of an empty interval of length \(x_u - x_\ell\), which is then one of its two gaps.

A walkthrough. Take \(n = 5\) values on \([0, 10]\), \(D = (1, 3, 4, 6, 9)\), so \(m = 3\) and the median is 4, and pad with \(x_i = 0\) for \(i \le 0\) and \(x_i = 10\) for \(i \ge 6\). Table 23.3 evaluates Equation 23.15 window by window, with \(\beta = 0.5\), and checks it against brute force: the script enumerates all \(11^5 = 161{,}051\) datasets with values in \(\{0, \dots, 10\}\), computes the local sensitivity of each and its distance from \(D\), and takes the maximum for each \(k\).

The local sensitivity (the row \(k = 0\)) is 2, the gap from 4 up to 6. But one change, moving the 6 up to 10, opens a gap of \(9 - 4 = 5\) next to the median, and even after the discount \(e^{-0.5} = 0.6065\) for one step, \(e^{-0.5}\times 5 = 3.03\) beats 2. Further rows open wider gaps but pay a larger discount. So \(S^*_{0.5}(D) = 3.03\), attained at \(k = 1\) (Figure 23.2, left).

23.4.6 Example: the median age

The script draws \(n = 1001\) ages from a normal distribution with mean 38 and standard deviation 14, clipped to \([0, 100]\), and releases the median (36.96) with \(\varepsilon = 1\). Each option is compared by its median absolute error. For noise \(bZ\) that is \(b\) times the median of \(|Z|\): 1 for the Cauchy, as shown above, and \(\ln 2\) for the Laplace, because \(\Pr[|Z| > t] = e^{-t}\) equals \(\frac12\) at \(t = \ln 2\).

With global sensitivity, Laplace noise has scale \(\Lambda/\varepsilon = 100\) and median absolute value \(100\ln 2 = 69.31\) years: useless. The local sensitivity is 0.1386 years, but releasing with it is not private, as Section 23.3 showed.

Smooth sensitivity with Cauchy noise uses \(\alpha = \beta = 1/6\). Then \(S^*_{1/6} = 0.1632\), attained at \(k = 7\): only slightly above the local sensitivity, because with this many people even seven changes cannot open a wide gap near the middle. The noise scale \(6S^*/\varepsilon = 6 \times 0.1632 = 0.979\) is also the median absolute error (simulation: 0.980).

Smooth sensitivity with Laplace noise uses \(\delta = 10^{-6}\) and \(\alpha = 1/2\). The largest \(\beta\) allowed by Equation 23.10 is then \(0.0322\) (close to the \(0.0345\) of Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith). The smaller \(\beta\) lets more distant datasets count, so \(S^*_{0.0322} = 0.5144\), attained at \(k = 21\). The noise scale is \(S^*/\alpha = 2S^*/\varepsilon = 1.029\) and the median absolute error \(1.029\ln 2 = 0.713\).

Both smooth releases are 70 to 100 times more accurate than the global one (\(69.31/0.979\) and \(69.31/0.713\)). Figure 23.3 shows the gain grows with \(n\): the smooth noise falls roughly like \(1/n\), the spacing of the data near the median, while the global noise stays put. For \(n \lesssim 20\) the smooth noise is worse than the global one, because with few points a handful of changes can open a gap of nearly the whole range, so \(S^*\) can be close to \(\Lambda\), and then the factor \(1/\alpha\) multiplies it.

For the median, \(A^{(n)} = \Lambda\) gives \(S^* \ge e^{-n\beta}\Lambda > 0\), which also means the scale in Equation 23.8 is never zero.)

23.5 Propose-test-release

23.5.1 Context

Smooth sensitivity needs a formula like Equation 23.15, and those are known for only a few functions. Dwork & Lei (2009) take a cruder route that needs much less. Guess a bound \(b\) on the local sensitivity, check privately that the data are not near any dataset where the guess fails, and only then release with noise of scale \(b/\varepsilon\). If the check fails, refuse to answer. The check is private because it is a “how many changes until something bad” count, which has sensitivity 1 by Fact 3.

23.5.2 Statement

Fix a proposed bound \(b > 0\), a privacy parameter \(\varepsilon > 0\) and a failure probability \(\delta \in (0, 1)\). The quantity to test is the number of records you must change before the local sensitivity can exceed \(b\), that is, how far \(D\) is from the nearest dataset where the proposal would be too small: \[\operatorname{dist}_b(D) = \min\big\{d(D, D''):\ \mathrm{LS}_f(D'') > b\big\}. \tag{23.16}\] Propose-test-release adds Laplace noise to this distance and compares it with a threshold \(\ln(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\), chosen so that the noise alone pushes a distance of 0 over it only with probability \(\delta/2\). It outputs one of two things:

  • \(\bot\) (“no answer”) if \(\operatorname{dist}_b(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon) \le \ln(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\);
  • \(f(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(b/\varepsilon)\) otherwise.

It is \((2\varepsilon, \delta/2)\)-DP, so in particular \((2\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP. For the median, \(\operatorname{dist}_b(D) = \min\{k : A^{(k)}(D) > b\}\), directly from Equation 23.15: \(A^{(k)}\) is the largest local sensitivity within \(k\) changes, so the first \(k\) where it exceeds \(b\) is the distance to the first dataset where \(b\) fails.

23.5.3 Derivation

The idea is to split neighbouring pairs into two cases. If the proposal \(b\) is right for this pair, the release is an ordinary Laplace mechanism. If it is wrong, both datasets sit right at an unstable point, the distance is 0, and the test almost always refuses.

  1. The test is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. \(\operatorname{dist}_b\) is a distance to the set of datasets whose local sensitivity exceeds \(b\), so it has sensitivity 1 (Fact 3), and adding \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) is the Laplace mechanism (Fact 1). Whether the test passes is a post-processing of that noisy value: a function of it that does not look at the data again, which cannot increase the privacy loss.
  2. Case 1: \(|f(D) - f(D')| \le b\). Then the release step is also \(\varepsilon\)-DP (Fact 1 with \(\Delta = b\)). The output is a function of two independent \(\varepsilon\)-DP outputs, the noisy distance and the noisy value. Basic composition says that running two mechanisms with independent noise costs the sum of their \(\varepsilon\)s, because the privacy loss of the pair is the sum of the two losses; post-processing says the function of them costs nothing more (Post-processing, group privacy and composition). So the mechanism is \(2\varepsilon\)-DP on this pair.
  3. Case 2: \(|f(D) - f(D')| > b\). Then both \(\mathrm{LS}_f(D) > b\) and \(\mathrm{LS}_f(D') > b\), because each local sensitivity is the largest change over that dataset’s neighbours, and each of \(D\), \(D'\) is a neighbour of the other. So \(\operatorname{dist}_b(D) = 0\), and the test passes only if \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon) > \ln(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\). A \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)\) variable exceeds \(t\) with probability \(\frac12e^{-\varepsilon t}\) (one tail of the density), so this has probability \(\frac12e^{-\ln(1/\delta)} = \delta/2\).
  4. Bound case 2. For any set \(S\) of outputs, \(M(D)\) lands in \(S\) either by refusing (if \(\bot \in S\)) or by releasing, and the release happens with probability at most \(\delta/2\) by step 3. So \(\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le \Pr[M(D) = \bot]\,\mathbf 1[\bot \in S] + \delta/2\), where \(\mathbf 1[\bot \in S]\) is 1 if \(\bot\) is in \(S\) and 0 otherwise. The refusal is a post-processing of the \(\varepsilon\)-DP test of step 1, so the first term is at most \(e^\varepsilon\Pr[M(D') = \bot]\,\mathbf 1[\bot\in S] \le e^{2\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D') \in S]\).
  5. Combine: in both cases \(\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^{2\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta/2\).

The “release the median for free” failure of the previous section is now confined to step 3: a dataset whose local sensitivity exceeds the proposal leaks only when a Laplace draw lands \(\ln(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\) above zero.

23.5.4 Interpretation

Propose-test-release is all or nothing. When the data are stable, you get noise of scale \(b/\varepsilon\), set by your proposal rather than by the data, so the noise scale reveals nothing. When they are not, you get \(\bot\), and \(\bot\) itself is a private answer: it says only that the data were probably near an unstable dataset.

The threshold the distance must clear is large. The test passes with probability \(\frac12\) when the distance equals the threshold, because the Laplace noise is positive half the time. With \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\delta = 10^{-6}\) that threshold is \(\ln(10^6) = 13.82\), so the test needs \(\operatorname{dist}_b(D) \gtrsim 13.82\) to pass with probability \(\frac12\). The data must be stable against more than a dozen changes, not just one.

The proposal \(b\) must not depend on the data. It is a proposal made before looking. Choosing \(b\) as, say, twice the observed local sensitivity makes the noise scale of the release a function of the data again, and reintroduces the leak of the previous section through the back door.

Compared with smooth sensitivity, propose-test-release needs only the distance to instability, which is often easier to compute or bound than \(S^*\). It pays \(2\varepsilon\) instead of \(\varepsilon\), and it can refuse.

23.5.5 Example: the median age

On the 1,001 ages, propose \(b = 1\) year. The first \(k\) with \(A^{(k)} > 1\) is \(k = 21\), so \(\operatorname{dist}_1(D) = 21\), comfortably above the threshold \(13.82\). The test fails only if the noise drops the distance by more than \(21 - 13.82\), which a \(\mathrm{Lap}(1)\) draw does with probability \(\frac12e^{-(21 - 13.82)}\). So the pass probability is \(1 - \frac12e^{-(21 - 13.82)} = 0.999621\), and the release has noise \(\mathrm{Lap}(1)\), with median absolute error \(\ln 2 = 0.693\) years at a total budget of \(2\varepsilon = 2\). On a sample of 101 ages, \(\operatorname{dist}_1 = 1\), and the test passes only if the noise adds more than \(13.82 - 1\), which has probability \(\frac12e^{-(13.82 - 1)} = 1.36\times10^{-6}\): there, one year is far too optimistic a proposal. Figure 23.3 (right) shows the pass probability switching from 0 to 1 between \(n = 500\) and \(n = 1000\).

23.5.6 Check yourself

  • Why does step 3 need both local sensitivities to exceed \(b\), and why is that automatic? (If either were at most \(b\), the pair would fall into case 1. It is automatic because \(|f(D) - f(D')|\) is one of the terms in the maximum defining each local sensitivity.)
  • Why does propose-test-release cost \(2\varepsilon\) while smooth sensitivity costs \(\varepsilon\)? (It runs two Laplace mechanisms, one for the test and one for the release, and composes them. Smooth sensitivity folds the cost of a data-dependent scale into the single noise draw.)

23.6 Subsample-and-aggregate

23.6.1 Context

Sometimes you cannot analyse \(f\) at all: it is an estimator fitted by an optimizer, a trained model, or a pipeline you did not write. Subsample-and-aggregate (Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith 2007; Smith 2011) treats \(f\) as a black box. It runs \(f\) on disjoint pieces of the data and privately combines the answers. One record lives in one piece, so it can change only one answer, and the aggregator needs to be private only with respect to changing one of its inputs.

23.6.2 Statement

Split the \(n\) records into \(B\) disjoint blocks of \(n/B\) records, by a partition fixed in advance (for example by position, or by a hash of a record identifier). Compute the block answers \(z_j = f(\text{block } j)\), clip each to a range \([0, R]\) (replace values below 0 by 0 and above \(R\) by \(R\)), and release their average plus Laplace noise. One record can move one clipped answer by at most \(R\), so it moves the average by at most \(R/B\), and that sets the noise: \[M(D) = \frac1B\sum_{j=1}^B \operatorname{clip}_{[0,R]}(z_j) + \mathrm{Lap}\Big(\frac{R}{B\varepsilon}\Big). \tag{23.17}\] This is \(\varepsilon\)-DP for any \(f\).

How accurate is it, as an estimate of a target \(\theta\)? Three things go wrong, and they add up in the mean squared error. Each block sees only \(n/B\) records, so \(f\) on a block can be biased in a way that \(f\) on the whole dataset is not; averaging the blocks keeps some random sampling error; and the Laplace noise has variance \(2(R/(B\varepsilon))^2\). Writing \(z\) for the output of \(f\) on one block, \[\underbrace{\big(\mathbb E[z] - \theta\big)^2}_{\text{bias of } f \text{ on } n/B \text{ records}} + \underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(z)}{B}}_{\text{sampling}} + \underbrace{2\Big(\frac{R}{B\varepsilon}\Big)^2}_{\text{noise}}, \tag{23.18}\] when the clipping is inactive and the blocks are independent samples.

23.6.3 Derivation

  1. One record touches one block. Replacing record \(i\) changes only the block that contains it, because the partition does not depend on the values. So the vector \((z_1, \dots, z_B)\) changes in at most one coordinate.
  2. The aggregate has sensitivity \(R/B\). After clipping, that coordinate moves by at most \(R\), the width of the clip range, and dividing by \(B\) in the average divides that by \(B\).
  3. Privacy: Fact 1 with \(\Delta = R/B\).
  4. Error. The released value minus \(\theta\) is the sum of three pieces: \(\mathbb E[z] - \theta\) (a constant, the bias), \(\frac1B\sum_j(z_j - \mathbb E z)\) (mean zero; it is an average of \(B\) independent terms, each with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(z)\), so its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(z)/B\)) and the Laplace noise (mean zero, variance \(2(R/(B\varepsilon))^2\), since a \(\mathrm{Lap}(s)\) variable has variance \(2s^2\)). Squaring the sum gives the three squares plus cross terms. Each cross term is a product involving at least one mean-zero piece that is independent of the other factor, so it averages to zero. That leaves Equation 23.18.

23.6.4 Interpretation

The number of blocks sets the trade-off: more blocks means less noise but more bias. The noise falls like \(1/B\), because one record’s influence on the average is diluted over more blocks. The sampling term hardly moves: for estimators whose variance scales like one over the sample size, a block estimate has variance about \(B\) times that of \(f\) on all \(n\) records, and averaging \(B\) of them divides it by \(B\) again, so the term stays near \(\operatorname{Var}(f \text{ on } n \text{ records})\). The bias of \(f\) on small blocks grows with \(B\), because the blocks get smaller. The best \(B\) balances the first and the last.

The privacy proof never looks inside \(f\). This is what makes the method useful for trained models: an ensemble of “teacher” models, each trained on a disjoint shard and combined by a noisy vote (private aggregation of teacher ensembles, Papernot et al. 2017), is subsample-and-aggregate with a vote as the aggregator.

The range \(R\) is still a global bound, on the output of \(f\) rather than on the data, and a loose \(R\) means large noise. Nissim, Raskhodnikova & Smith replace the clipped average by a “center of attention” aggregator with smooth sensitivity, which adapts to how tightly the \(z_j\) cluster. Smith (2011) shows that for asymptotically normal estimators a version of this method loses nothing in the leading-order error.

23.6.5 Example: estimating a rate

You observe \(n = 10{,}000\) inter-arrival times, independent \(\mathrm{Exponential}\) with rate \(\theta = 1\), and want to estimate \(\theta\) privately with \(\varepsilon = 1\). The maximum-likelihood estimate is one over the sample mean, \(1/\bar x\), whose global sensitivity is infinite: a dataset of tiny times has a huge \(1/\bar x\), and one record changes it by an arbitrary amount.

To fill in Equation 23.18 you need the mean and variance of \(1/\bar x\) on a block of \(n_b = n/B\) records. The block sum \(n_b\bar x\) is a sum of \(n_b\) independent exponentials with rate \(\theta\), which has the gamma distribution with shape \(n_b\) and rate \(\theta\): \(n_b\bar x \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(n_b, \theta)\). The inverse moments of a gamma variable \(G\) with shape \(k\) are \(\mathbb E[1/G] = \theta/(k - 1)\) and \(\mathbb E[1/G^2] = \theta^2/((k - 1)(k - 2))\): integrate \(g^{-1}\) or \(g^{-2}\) against the gamma density \(\theta^kg^{k-1}e^{-\theta g}/\Gamma(k)\), recognize what is left as a gamma density with shape \(k - 1\) or \(k - 2\), and use \(\Gamma(k) = (k - 1)\Gamma(k - 1)\). Since \(z = 1/\bar x = n_b/G\), multiplying by \(n_b\) and \(n_b^2\) gives \[\mathbb E[z] = \frac{n_b\theta}{n_b - 1}, \qquad \text{bias } \frac{\theta}{n_b - 1}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(z) = \frac{n_b^2\theta^2}{(n_b - 1)^2(n_b - 2)}. \tag{23.19}\] The variance is \(\mathbb E[z^2] - \mathbb E[z]^2 = n_b^2\theta^2\big(\frac1{(n_b-1)(n_b-2)} - \frac1{(n_b-1)^2}\big)\), which simplifies to the form shown. With clip range \(R = 5\), Table 23.4 and Figure 23.4 show the trade-off. For example, with \(B = 10\) blocks the noise standard deviation is \(\sqrt2\,R/(B\varepsilon) = \sqrt2 \times 0.5 = 0.7071\), and the bias on blocks of 1,000 is \(1/999 = 0.0010\).

The best \(B\) on the script’s grid is 250, with error \(0.0396\), where the bias and the noise are of similar size. A non-private estimate has standard deviation about \(\theta/\sqrt n = 0.0100\), so privacy at \(\varepsilon = 1\) costs a factor of four here, for an estimator whose sensitivity you never had to bound.

23.6.6 Check yourself

  • Why must the partition into blocks not depend on the data values? (Otherwise replacing one record could reshuffle many records between blocks and change many \(z_j\), breaking step 1.)
  • Why does the sampling term in Table 23.4 barely move with \(B\)? (The average of \(B\) block estimates, each with variance about \(\theta^2 B/n\), has variance about \(\theta^2/n\) whatever \(B\) is. Only the higher-order terms of the inverse-gamma variance depend on the block size.)

23.7 Clipping

23.7.1 Context

The mean is the most common statistic of all, and on unbounded data its global sensitivity is infinite. Clipping forces a bound: replace each value \(x\) by \(\min(x, C)\) before averaging. The sensitivity becomes \(C/n\), and Laplace noise of scale \(C/(n\varepsilon)\) makes the mean private. Nothing is free, though. Clipping changes the quantity you estimate, and the choice of \(C\) trades that bias against the noise. The same move, applied to per-example gradients rather than to values, is the heart of private model training in later parts.

23.7.2 Statement

Take non-negative data \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) drawn independently from a distribution with mean \(\mu\). The clipped mean averages the clipped values and adds noise for sensitivity \(C/n\): \[M(D) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\min(x_i, C) + \mathrm{Lap}\Big(\frac{C}{n\varepsilon}\Big) \tag{23.20}\] It is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Its error has the same three parts as in subsample-and-aggregate. Clipping cuts off the part of each value above \(C\), written \((x - C)_+ = \max(x - C, 0)\), and its average is the bias. The clipped values still vary from sample to sample, and the noise adds its own variance. So the mean squared error is \[\operatorname{err}(C)^2 = \underbrace{\mathbb E[(X - C)_+]^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\min(X, C))}{n}}_{\text{sampling}} + \underbrace{\frac{2C^2}{n^2\varepsilon^2}}_{\text{noise}}. \tag{23.21}\] Raising \(C\) shrinks the bias and grows the noise, so the best \(C\) is where the two rates of change balance, which is where the derivative of Equation 23.21 in \(C\) is zero. Here \(\Pr[X > C]\) is the fraction of values that clipping touches. The best \(C\) solves \[\Pr[X > C]\Big(\mathbb E[(X - C)_+] - \frac{C - \mathbb E[\min(X, C)]}{n}\Big) = \frac{2C}{n^2\varepsilon^2}. \tag{23.22}\]

23.7.3 Derivation

  1. Sensitivity. Each clipped value lies in \([0, C]\), so replacing one record changes the sum by at most \(C\) and the mean by at most \(C/n\). Fact 1 gives \(\varepsilon\)-DP.
  2. The bias. Clipping removes exactly the excess above \(C\): \(x - \min(x, C) = (x - C)_+\). Taking expectations, \(\mu - \mathbb E[\min(X, C)] = \mathbb E[(X - C)_+]\): the mass that clipping cuts off.
  3. Error decomposition. As in the subsample-and-aggregate derivation, the error is a constant (minus the bias), a mean-zero sampling term with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\min(X, C))/n\), and independent Laplace noise with variance \(2(C/(n\varepsilon))^2\). Squaring and averaging kills the cross terms, since each contains a mean-zero factor independent of the other: Equation 23.21.
  4. Derivatives in \(C\). Raising \(C\) by a small amount \(h\) raises \(\min(X, C)\) by \(h\) exactly when \(X > C\) and leaves it alone otherwise, so \(\frac{d}{dC}\mathbb E[\min(X, C)] = \Pr[X > C]\). By the same reasoning \(\frac{d}{dC}\mathbb E[\min(X, C)^2] = 2C\Pr[X > C]\). The bias is \(\mathbb E[(X - C)_+] = \mu - \mathbb E[\min(X, C)]\), whose derivative is \(-\Pr[X > C]\), so by the chain rule the bias term has derivative \(-2\,\mathbb E[(X - C)_+]\Pr[X > C]\). The variance is \(\mathbb E[\min(X, C)^2] - \mathbb E[\min(X, C)]^2\), so the sampling term has derivative \(\frac1n\big(2C\Pr[X > C] - 2\,\mathbb E[\min(X, C)]\Pr[X > C]\big) = \frac2n\Pr[X > C]\big(C - \mathbb E[\min(X, C)]\big)\). The noise term has derivative \(\frac{4C}{n^2\varepsilon^2}\).
  5. Set the sum to zero and divide by 2: Equation 23.22.

23.7.4 Interpretation

The bias does not shrink with \(n\), but the noise does. So the best \(C\) grows with \(n\): with more data you can afford to clip less. For large \(n\) the sampling part of Equation 23.22, which has an extra \(1/n\), drops out, and the condition reads \(\Pr[X > C]\,\mathbb E[(X - C)_+] \approx 2C/(n\varepsilon)^2\). The right side shrinks as \(n\varepsilon\) grows, so the left side must shrink too, which pushes \(C\) out into the tail.

The bias has a sign. Clipping only from above always lowers values, so it always underestimates. For data on both sides of zero, clipping to \([-C, C]\) cuts off mass on both sides, and the two cuts cancel only for distributions symmetric about 0, so only those are estimated without bias.

Choosing \(C\) is itself a data-dependent decision. Setting \(C\) to the observed maximum, or to the empirical 99th percentile, makes the noise scale a function of the data, and the leak of Section 23.3 returns. Choose \(C\) from domain knowledge, or spend part of the budget on a private quantile: the exponential mechanism, or the smooth-sensitivity and propose-test-release methods above applied to an order statistic.

Clipping is the honest version of “bounded data”. Every “assume \(x \in [0, \Lambda]\)” in earlier chapters is a clip in disguise, and its bias is the price of that assumption.

23.7.5 Example: mean income

Incomes (in thousands) follow a log-normal distribution with \(\ln X \sim \mathcal N(\ln 40, 0.8^2)\): the log of an income is normal. The median is \(e^{\ln 40} = 40\), because the exponential preserves the median. The mean is larger, \(e^{\ln 40 + 0.32} = 55.085\), because a log-normal with \(\ln X \sim \mathcal N(\eta, s^2)\) has mean \(e^{\eta + s^2/2}\) and here \(s^2/2 = 0.64/2 = 0.32\); the gap reflects a heavy right tail.

Every term of Equation 23.21 needs averages of \(X\) and \(X^2\) over the values below \(C\). For a log-normal these partial moments have a closed form, \(\mathbb E[X^j\mathbf 1\{X \le c\}] = e^{j\eta + j^2s^2/2}\,\Phi\big((\ln c - \eta - js^2)/s\big)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and \(\mathbf 1\{X \le c\}\) is 1 when \(X \le c\) and 0 otherwise. To get it, write \(X = e^Y\) with \(Y\) normal, so the integral is \(\int_{-\infty}^{\ln c}e^{jy}\varphi(y)\,dy\) with \(\varphi\) the normal density of \(Y\); completing the square in the exponent turns \(e^{jy}\varphi(y)\) into \(e^{j\eta + j^2s^2/2}\) times a normal density with mean shifted to \(\eta + js^2\), whose integral up to \(\ln c\) is the \(\Phi\) term (as in Probability distributions). Table 23.5 uses these with \(n = 1000\) and \(\varepsilon = 1\).

Clipping at the median is a disaster: half the incomes are cut back, and the bias of 23 dwarfs everything. The optimum, \(C^* = 331.34\), cuts off only 0.4% of the population and has total error \(1.666\), only 1% above the standard deviation \(1.649\) of the unclipped, non-private mean (the ratio is \(1.666/1.649 \approx 1.01\)). Above the optimum, the noise takes over: its standard deviation \(\sqrt2\,C/(n\varepsilon)\) grows linearly in \(C\), reaching \(1.414\) at \(C = 1000\) (Figure 23.5, left). The optimum grows with \(n\), as the interpretation predicted: \(177.5\) at \(n = 100\), \(557.3\) at \(n = 10{,}000\) and \(876.3\) at \(n = 100{,}000\) (Figure 23.5, right).

  • Under the add/remove relation, why is a clipped sum easier to release than a clipped mean? (Adding or removing one record changes the sum by at most \(C\), but it also changes \(n\), so the mean’s denominator is not public. One releases a noisy sum and a noisy count and divides.)
  • If you double \(n\), roughly how does the noise term change, and which way does \(C^*\) move? (The noise standard deviation halves at fixed \(C\), so the balance in Equation 23.22 shifts toward a larger \(C^*\) with less bias.)

Rules of thumb

  • If the noise scale depends on the data, ask what the noise scale reveals. If you cannot answer, use one of the four repairs.
  • Pure DP with a data-dependent scale needs polynomial tails; light tails need \(\delta\) and a much smaller \(\beta \approx \varepsilon/(2\ln(1/\delta))\).
  • Smooth sensitivity pays off once the data are dense around the statistic: for the median, once \(n\) is in the hundreds.
  • Choose clip bounds and block counts before looking at the data, by the error formula, or with a separate slice of the privacy budget.
