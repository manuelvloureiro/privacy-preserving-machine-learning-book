21  Advanced and optimal composition

Author

Manuel V. Loureiro

Published

Version 2026-10-30

A differentially private system rarely answers one query: a dashboard refreshes a hundred counts, and a training run takes thousands of noisy gradient steps. Each step satisfies \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-differential privacy (DP) on its own, and you need a guarantee for the whole run. Post-processing, group privacy and composition gave the first answer, basic composition: \(k\) mechanisms that are each \(\varepsilon\)-DP are together \(k\varepsilon\)-DP. For large \(k\) that is far too pessimistic. A hundred queries at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) give \(k\varepsilon = 10\), which lets an observer multiply its odds between two neighbouring datasets by up to \(e^{10} \approx 22{,}000\). Yet at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) the same hundred queries are \((4.31, 10^{-5})\)-DP, and no analysis that knows only each query’s \(\varepsilon\) can say less.

The one idea behind the gap is that the privacy loss of a composition is a sum. Each output is evidence for one dataset or the other, measured by the logarithm of how much likelier it is under \(D\) than under its neighbour \(D'\), and the evidence of \(k\) outputs adds up. Basic composition charges the largest value of that sum, as if every step produced its worst output. But a sum of \(k\) bounded random terms rarely strays from its mean by more than a multiple of \(\sqrt k\). A small \(\delta\) lets you ignore the rare extreme sums and charge for the typical one, which is advanced composition. The best bound of this kind, optimal composition, has an exact formula, because randomized response is the mechanism whose composition is hardest to protect. Both hold when an analyst chooses each query after the earlier answers, which is adaptive composition.

The running example is the dashboard: \(k = 100\) counts, each released by a \(0.1\)-DP mechanism, under add/remove neighbours. Basic composition charges \(\varepsilon = 10\), advanced composition 5.85 at \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\), and optimal composition 4.31. The chapter derives all three from the distribution of the total privacy loss.

21.1 The privacy loss of a composition

Every bound in this chapter is a statement about one random variable, the total privacy loss of the run. This section defines it, shows that it is a sum over the steps, and finds its distribution for composed randomized response, which Section 21.3 proves is the worst case.

Definition 21.1 (The privacy loss random variable) Let \(M\) be a mechanism and \(D \sim D'\) two neighbouring datasets. Write \(P = M(D)\) and \(Q = M(D')\) for the two output distributions, with mass functions or densities \(p\) and \(q\). The privacy loss of an output \(y\) is the log-ratio \(L_{D,D'}(y) = \ln\big(p(y)/q(y)\big)\), which is \(+\infty\) where \(q(y) = 0 < p(y)\), and the privacy loss random variable is the loss of a random output drawn under \(D\), \[L = L_{D,D'}(Y) = \ln\frac{p(Y)}{q(Y)}, \qquad Y \sim P. \tag{21.1}\] Its distribution is the privacy loss distribution of the pair. Once the pair is fixed, \(L(y)\) is short for \(L_{D,D'}(y)\).

\((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP asks that \(\Pr_P[S] \le e^\varepsilon\Pr_Q[S] + \delta\) for every set of outputs \(S\). With \(\delta = 0\) this says \(|L| \le \varepsilon\) for every output (The definition of differential privacy). For one ordered pair of datasets, the smallest \(\delta\) at a given \(\varepsilon\) is the largest gap \(\Pr_P[S] - e^\varepsilon\Pr_Q[S]\), reached on the outputs with \(p(y) > e^\varepsilon q(y)\) by Lemma 3.4. That largest gap is the hockey-stick divergence, \[\delta(\varepsilon) = E_{e^\varepsilon}(P\|Q) = \sum_y\big(p(y) - e^\varepsilon q(y)\big)_+, \qquad (x)_+ = \max\{x, 0\}, \tag{21.2}\] and as a function of \(\varepsilon\) it is the privacy profile of the pair in one direction. DP asks for it in both directions, for every pair of neighbours.

The smallest case is randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\) (The definition of differential privacy), which reports a person’s bit truthfully with probability \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\) and flips it otherwise. For a person whose bit is 0 in \(D\) and 1 in \(D'\), at \(\varepsilon = \ln 3\), a report of 0 has probability \(\frac34\) under \(D\) and \(\frac14\) under \(D'\), so its loss is \(\ln 3\), and a report of 1 has loss \(-\ln 3\). The first lemma says that the profile is an average of a function of the loss. So a composition theorem only has to control the distribution of the total loss.

Lemma 21.1 (The privacy profile is an average of the privacy loss) For every pair \((P, Q)\) and every \(\varepsilon \ge 0\), \[\delta(\varepsilon) = E_{e^\varepsilon}(P\|Q) = \sum_y\big(p(y) - e^\varepsilon q(y)\big)_+ = \mathbb E\big[(1 - e^{\varepsilon - L})_+\big] \le \Pr[L > \varepsilon], \tag{21.3}\] where the expectation and the probability are over \(Y \sim P\). In particular, \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) depends on the pair only through the distribution of \(L\) under \(P\).

Proof. This is Lemma 20.2, which rests on Proposition 3.5. Each term of the sum is \((p - e^\varepsilon q)_+ = p\,(1 - e^{\varepsilon - L(y)})_+\), so the sum is the average of the bracket under \(P\). An output with \(q = 0\) has \(L = +\infty\) and bracket 1. The tail form of Proposition 3.5 writes the divergence as \(\Pr[L > \varepsilon] - e^\varepsilon\Pr_Q[L > \varepsilon]\), which is at most \(\Pr[L > \varepsilon]\).

An output whose loss is at most \(\varepsilon\) adds nothing to \(\delta(\varepsilon)\), and an output that is impossible under \(D'\) adds its whole probability. For randomized response at \(\ln 3\), only the report 0 has a positive loss, \(\ln 3\). So the lemma at \(\varepsilon = 0\) gives \(\delta(0) = \frac34(1 - \frac13) = \frac12\), the total variation distance \(\frac34 - \frac14\), as it should. Advanced composition uses the tail bound of the lemma, and optimal composition uses the equality.

Now run \(k\) mechanisms. The probability of a whole sequence of outputs is a product of one factor per step, and the logarithm turns that product into a sum.

Lemma 21.2 (The privacy loss of a composition is a sum) Run \(M_1, \dots, M_k\) in sequence, where \(M_i\) may depend on the earlier outputs \(y_{<i} = (y_1, \dots, y_{i-1})\). Write \(p_i(y_i \mid y_{<i})\) for the probability that step \(i\) outputs \(y_i\) on \(D\) after the history \(y_{<i}\), and \(q_i(y_i \mid y_{<i})\) for the same on \(D'\). Then the privacy loss of the whole sequence is the sum of the per-step losses, \[L(y_1, \dots, y_k) = \sum_{i=1}^k L_i(y_i \mid y_{<i}), \qquad L_i(y_i \mid y_{<i}) = \ln\frac{p_i(y_i \mid y_{<i})}{q_i(y_i \mid y_{<i})}, \tag{21.4}\] where \(L_i\) is the privacy loss of step \(i\) alone, for the mechanism that was run after that history. If step \(i\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP for every history, then \(|L_i| \le \varepsilon\), and so \(|L| \le k\varepsilon\).

Proof. Lemma 19.2, in its form with dependent outputs and applied \(k - 1\) times, gives Equation 21.4. If step \(i\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP whatever the history, each term lies in \([-\varepsilon, \varepsilon]\), and the sum of \(k\) such bounds gives \(|L| \le k\varepsilon\).

Table 21.1 shows the sum for two independent rounds of randomized response at \(\varepsilon = \ln 3\). The output \((0, 1)\) has loss 0, because one report favours \(D\) as much as the other favours \(D'\).

For randomized response the sum is a random walk with steps \(\pm\varepsilon\), and its whole distribution has a closed form. A report of 0 is the truth under \(D\), so it points to \(D\). Under \(D\) a report points to \(D'\) only when it is a lie.

Proposition 21.1 (The loss of composed randomized response) Compose \(k\) independent rounds of randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\), on a person whose bit is 0 in \(D\) and 1 in \(D'\), and let \(J\) be the number of rounds whose report points to \(D'\). Under \(D\), \[J \sim \mathrm{Bin}(k, 1 - t), \qquad L = (k - 2J)\varepsilon, \tag{21.5}\] and the loss has mean, variance and worst-case probability \[\mathbb E[L] = k\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(L) = k\varepsilon^2\big(1 - \tanh^2(\varepsilon/2)\big), \qquad \Pr[L = k\varepsilon] = t^k. \tag{21.6}\]

Proof. The plan is to find the loss of one round, add \(k\) of them, and then compute the mean and variance of one round and multiply by \(k\).

  1. One round. A truthful report has probability \(t\) under \(D\) and \(1 - t\) under \(D'\), so its loss is \(\ln\frac{t}{1 - t} = \ln e^\varepsilon = \varepsilon\), because \(t/(1 - t) = e^\varepsilon\) by the definition of \(t\). A lie has the two probabilities swapped and loss \(-\varepsilon\).
  2. \(k\) rounds. By Lemma 21.2 the total is the sum of the \(k\) per-round losses. The \(J\) rounds that point to \(D'\) contribute \(-\varepsilon\) each and the other \(k - J\) contribute \(+\varepsilon\), so \(L = (k - J)\varepsilon - J\varepsilon = (k - 2J)\varepsilon\). Each round points to \(D'\) exactly when it lies, independently with probability \(1 - t\), so \(J\) counts the successes in \(k\) independent trials and is binomial.
  3. Mean. One round has mean \(\varepsilon t - \varepsilon(1 - t) = \varepsilon(2t - 1)\). With the value of \(t\), \(2t - 1 = \frac{e^\varepsilon - 1}{e^\varepsilon + 1}\), which is \(\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\) because \(\tanh x = \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{e^{2x} + 1}\). The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, so multiply by \(k\).
  4. Variance. One round takes the two values \(\pm\varepsilon\), so its square is always \(\varepsilon^2\) and its variance is \(\varepsilon^2 - (\text{mean})^2 = \varepsilon^2(1 - \tanh^2(\varepsilon/2))\). Variances of independent terms add, so the total variance is \(k\) times this.
  5. The worst case. The value \(L = k\varepsilon\) needs \(J = 0\), every report truthful, which has probability \(t^k\).

For small \(\varepsilon\), \(\tanh(\varepsilon/2) \approx \varepsilon/2\), so the mean is about \(k\varepsilon^2/2\) and the standard deviation about \(\sqrt k\,\varepsilon\). Both are far below the worst case \(k\varepsilon\), which needs all \(k\) reports to be truthful, so basic composition guards against an event that will almost never happen. By the central limit theorem the total loss is nearly normal (Figure 21.1), the starting point of the Gaussian view of privacy in Privacy accounting. Advanced composition bounds its tail, and optimal composition evaluates Equation 21.3 on the binomial exactly.

Example 21.1 Release each of the dashboard’s \(k = 100\) counts by randomized response at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the worst \(0.1\)-DP mechanism by Section 21.3. Each round is truthful with probability \(t = e^{0.1}/(1 + e^{0.1}) = 0.5250\). By Proposition 21.1 the total loss has mean \(100 \times 0.1\tanh(0.05) = 0.4996\) and standard deviation 0.9988. Basic composition’s \(k\varepsilon = 10\) sits \((10 - 0.50)/1.00 = 9.5\) standard deviations above the mean, and it needs all 100 reports to be truthful, which has probability \(t^{100} = 1.0\times10^{-28}\). The loss \((100 - 2J) \times 0.1\) exceeds 4 only when \(J < 30\), about 3.5 standard deviations of \(J\) (which are 5) below its mean \(100(1 - t) = 47.5\). The optimal \(\varepsilon'\) at \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\) that Section 21.4 computes, 4.3068, sits \((4.3068 - 0.4996)/0.9988 = 3.8\) standard deviations above the mean of the loss.

21.2 Advanced composition

Advanced composition, due to Dwork, Rothblum & Vadhan (2010), charges for the typical value of the sum rather than its largest. It needs only each step’s \(\varepsilon\), holds for mechanisms chosen in any adaptive way, and grows like \(\sqrt k\). Its tool is Azuma’s inequality, a version of Hoeffding’s inequality for sums whose terms may depend on the earlier terms. Azuma controls how far the sum strays from its mean, so the theorem also needs a bound on the mean of each term, the drift of the sum, which comes first.

The loss can be as large as \(\varepsilon\), but on average it is much smaller. When \(\varepsilon\) is small, positive and negative losses nearly cancel, and what is left is of order \(\varepsilon^2\). The average loss under \(P\) is \(\sum_y p(y)\ln(p(y)/q(y))\), the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\) of Divergences.

Lemma 21.3 (The mean privacy loss of an ε-DP pair is second order in ε) If \(|L| \le \varepsilon\) for every output, then \[\mathbb E[L] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) \le \varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1). \tag{21.7}\]

Proof.

  1. Symmetrize. The KL divergence is never negative (Divergences). So the addition of \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P) \ge 0\) to \(\mathbb E[L]\) can only increase it, and the two sums combine term by term: \[\mathbb E[L] \le \sum_y p\ln\frac pq + \sum_y q\ln\frac qp = \sum_y (p - q)\ln\frac pq. \tag{21.8}\]
  2. Use the bound on the loss. Each \(|\ln(p/q)|\) is at most \(\varepsilon\), so the sum is at most \(\varepsilon\sum_y|p - q|\), and the total difference \(\sum_y|p - q|\) is itself small when \(P\) and \(Q\) are close.
  3. Bound the total difference. Write \(\sum_y|p - q| = \sum_y q\,|p/q - 1|\). The ratio \(p/q\) lies in \([e^{-\varepsilon}, e^\varepsilon]\), and on that interval \(|p/q - 1|\) is largest at the upper end, where it is \(e^\varepsilon - 1\) (at the lower end it is \(1 - e^{-\varepsilon}\), which is smaller). Since the \(q(y)\) sum to 1, \(\sum_y|p - q| \le e^\varepsilon - 1\), and the bound follows.

So the mean is of order \(\varepsilon^2\), and at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) the bound is \(0.1(e^{0.1} - 1) = 0.0105\). It is not tight. Theorem 21.3 in Section 21.3 gives the best bound, \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\), which is 0.0050 at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and 0.4621 at \(\varepsilon = 1\), where the lemma gives 1.7183. Section B.2 in the appendices derives the bound from scratch for a single count.

Theorem 21.1 (Advanced composition (Dwork, Rothblum & Vadhan 2010)) Fix \(k\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\) in advance, and let \(M_1, \dots, M_k\) be mechanisms, each chosen after seeing the earlier outputs, such that every \(M_i\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP for every history. Then for every \(\delta' > 0\) the composition is \((\varepsilon', \delta')\)-DP with \[\varepsilon' = \varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1). \tag{21.9}\]

Proof. The plan is to write the loss as a sum, centre each term by its conditional mean to get a martingale, bound the fluctuation of the martingale by Azuma’s inequality and the drift by Lemma 21.3, and convert the tail bound into a \(\delta\) by Lemma 21.1.

  1. The loss is a sum. By Lemma 21.2, \(L = \sum_i L_i\), where \(L_i\) is the loss of step \(i\) given the history \(y_{<i}\).
  2. Each term is bounded, given the past. For every fixed history, step \(i\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP, so \(L_i \in [-\varepsilon, \varepsilon]\).
  3. Each term has a small conditional mean. Given the history, the average of \(L_i\) over step \(i\)’s output is \(m_i = \mathbb E[L_i \mid y_{<i}] = \sum_{y_i}p_i\ln(p_i/q_i)\), the KL divergence of step \(i\)’s pair. So \(m_i \le \varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)\) by Lemma 21.3.
  4. Centre the terms to get a martingale. A martingale is a running sum whose next increment has mean zero given everything that happened before. Each term minus its conditional mean makes one: \(W_k = \sum_i(L_i - m_i)\) has increments with conditional mean zero. Given the past, \(L_i - m_i\) lies in \([-\varepsilon - m_i, \varepsilon - m_i]\), an interval of length \(c_i = 2\varepsilon\), whose position depends on the past but whose length does not.
  5. Azuma. The Azuma–Hoeffding inequality of Concentration inequalities says this. If each increment of a martingale has conditional mean zero and lies, given the past, in an interval of length \(c_i\) fixed in advance, then the sum exceeds \(s\) with probability at most \(\exp(-2s^2/\sum_i c_i^2)\). With \(c_i = 2\varepsilon\), so that \(\sum_i c_i^2 = 4k\varepsilon^2\), \[\Pr[W_k \ge s] \le \exp\left(-\frac{2s^2}{4k\varepsilon^2}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{s^2}{2k\varepsilon^2}\right). \tag{21.10}\]
  6. Choose the level. The right side equals \(\delta'\) when \(s^2 = 2k\varepsilon^2\ln(1/\delta')\), that is \(s = \varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\). The loss is the fluctuation plus the drift, \(L = W_k + \sum_i m_i \le W_k + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)\). So if \(L\) exceeds \(\varepsilon' = s + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)\), then \(W_k\) must exceed \(s\), and \(\Pr[L > \varepsilon'] \le \delta'\).
  7. Convert. By Lemma 21.1, \(\delta(\varepsilon') \le \Pr[L > \varepsilon'] \le \delta'\). The same argument with \(D\) and \(D'\) swapped gives the other direction, so the composition is \((\varepsilon', \delta')\)-DP.

The gain is \(\sqrt k\) instead of \(k\). The price is \(\delta'\) and the factor \(\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta')}\), which is \(\sqrt{2 \times 11.51} = 4.80\) at \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\), so the theorem beats basic composition only once \(k\) is large. It wins when \(\varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1) < k\varepsilon\). Divide by \(k\varepsilon\) to get \(\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta')/k} < 2 - e^\varepsilon\). The square of both sides, which needs \(\varepsilon < \ln 2\) so that \(2 - e^\varepsilon > 0\), gives the crossover: \[k > \frac{2\ln(1/\delta')}{(2 - e^\varepsilon)^2}. \tag{21.11}\] With the leading term set to a target \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}\), each step gets \(\varepsilon \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}/\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\), where basic composition would allow only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}/k\). Corollary 21.1 in Section 21.4 extends the theorem to \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP steps at a cost of \(k\delta\), with the sharper drift \(k\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\).

The bound is loose in three places. Azuma assumes each increment has the largest variance its range allows, the mean bound of Lemma 21.3 is a factor 2 too large for small \(\varepsilon\), and the tail bound of Lemma 21.1 counts every output above \(\varepsilon'\) in full. At \(k = 100\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the exact \(\delta\) at the advanced \(\varepsilon' = 5.85\) is \(1.96\times10^{-9}\), not \(10^{-5}\).

Example 21.2 Take the dashboard’s \(k = 100\) counts at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\). The Azuma term is \(0.1\sqrt{200\ln10^5} = 0.1\sqrt{2302.6} = 4.7985\), and the mean term is \(100 \times 0.1(e^{0.1} - 1) = 1.0517\), so \(\varepsilon' = 5.8502\), against 10 for basic composition. The crossover is \(23.03/0.8948^2 = 28.8\), so advanced composition wins from \(k = 29\) on (Figure 21.2, and Table 21.4 in Section 21.4). At \(k = 10{,}000\) the mean term dominates: it costs 105 of the total 153, against 48 for the Azuma term, and the sharper drift of Corollary 21.1 costs 50 instead.

Laplace noise in many dimensions. The same arithmetic explains where the Gaussian mechanism’s advantage in high dimension comes from. Take \(d\) dense counts, where one record can move every count by 1, at \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). Laplace noise of scale \(b\) has root-mean-square error \(\sqrt2\,b\), and on the whole vector it must use the \(\ell_1\) sensitivity \(d\), so \(b = d/\varepsilon\). Instead, compose \(d\) separate Laplace mechanisms at the largest \(\varepsilon_0\) whose composition stays at 1, for an error of \(\sqrt2/\varepsilon_0\). For \(d = 1000\), \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.00645\) by Equation 21.9, or \(0.00849\) by the optimal composition of Section 21.4. Table 21.2 adds the Gaussian mechanism of the Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms. Composed Laplace noise grows like \(\sqrt d\) and stays within a factor of 1.9 of the Gaussian, while pure-DP Laplace noise falls behind by a factor of 38 at \(d = 10{,}000\). So the \(\sqrt d\) gain comes from \(\delta > 0\), not from the Gaussian density.

21.3 The worst mechanism

Which composition bound is best? Kairouz, Oh & Viswanath (2015) answered with post-processing: every \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP mechanism is a post-processing of one version of randomized response, so no composition of such mechanisms can be worse than composed randomized response. This section builds that mechanism and proves the reduction, and Section 21.4 composes it.

A channel \(K\) is a table of probabilities \(K(y \mid z)\): given an input \(z\), it draws an output \(y\) without looking at the data. A distribution \(P^\star\) fed through it gives \(P^\star K(y) = \sum_z P^\star(z)K(y \mid z)\). By the data-processing inequality Theorem 3.5, a channel never increases the hockey-stick divergence or the KL divergence.

The worst mechanism must spend both parameters. It spends \(\delta\) by announcing, with probability \(\delta\), which dataset it ran on, and \(\varepsilon\) by otherwise running randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\).

Definition 21.2 (The four-point randomized response) The four-point pair \((P^\star, Q^\star)\) at level \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) is the pair of output distributions in Table 21.3: with probability \(\delta\) the mechanism announces its dataset outright, and otherwise it reports the person’s bit through randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\), with \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\).

Every \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP pair can be produced from the four-point pair by one channel, and for \(\delta = 0\) the channel has a closed form.

Theorem 21.2 (Randomized response is the worst (ε, δ)-DP mechanism) Let \((P, Q)\) be a pair of distributions on a common set of outputs that satisfies \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP in both directions. Then there is a channel \(K\) with \(P = P^\star K\) and \(Q = Q^\star K\), where \((P^\star, Q^\star)\) is the four-point pair of Definition 21.2. For \(\delta = 0\) the channel is \[K(y \mid 0) = \frac{e^\varepsilon p(y) - q(y)}{e^\varepsilon - 1}, \qquad K(y \mid 1) = \frac{e^\varepsilon q(y) - p(y)}{e^\varepsilon - 1}. \tag{21.12}\]

Proof. For \(\delta = 0\), the plan is to solve for the channel and check that it reproduces the pair. For \(\delta > 0\), the last step says why the channel exists and where it is built.

  1. Solve for the channel. Plain randomized response outputs 0, the report that points to \(D\), with probability \(t\) on \(D\) and \(1 - t\) on \(D'\). So for every output \(y\) of the target pair, the channel must satisfy \(p(y) = tK(y \mid 0) + (1 - t)K(y \mid 1)\) and \(q(y) = (1 - t)K(y \mid 0) + tK(y \mid 1)\). These are two linear equations in the two unknowns \(K(y \mid 0)\) and \(K(y \mid 1)\), and their solution with \(t/(1 - t) = e^\varepsilon\) is Equation 21.12.
  2. The rows are distributions. A channel’s rows must be non-negative and sum to 1. \(K(y \mid 0) \ge 0\) exactly when \(e^\varepsilon p(y) \ge q(y)\), and \(K(y \mid 1) \ge 0\) exactly when \(e^\varepsilon q(y) \ge p(y)\). Both hold for every \(y\) because the pair is \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Since \(p\) and \(q\) each sum to 1, each row sums to \((e^\varepsilon - 1)/(e^\varepsilon - 1) = 1\).
  3. They reproduce \(P\). The rows, mixed with randomized response’s probabilities on \(D\), give \[t\,K(y \mid 0) + (1 - t)K(y \mid 1) = \frac{\big(te^\varepsilon - (1 - t)\big)p(y) + \big((1 - t)e^\varepsilon - t\big)q(y)}{e^\varepsilon - 1}. \tag{21.13}\] With \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\), \((1 - t)e^\varepsilon = t\), so the coefficient of \(q\) is 0. The coefficient of \(p\) is \(\frac{te^\varepsilon - (1 - t)}{e^\varepsilon - 1} = \frac{(e^{2\varepsilon} - 1)/(1 + e^\varepsilon)}{e^\varepsilon - 1} = 1\), since \(e^{2\varepsilon} - 1 = (e^\varepsilon - 1)(e^\varepsilon + 1)\).
  4. They reproduce \(Q\). The same computation with \(t\) and \(1 - t\) swapped.
  5. The case \(\delta > 0\), cited. The trade-off function of a pair gives, for each false positive rate, the smallest false negative rate any test between \(P\) and \(Q\) can reach. A pair is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP exactly when its trade-off function lies above a boundary \(f_{\varepsilon,\delta}\) made of two straight lines (The definition of differential privacy), and the four-point pair sits on it (Figure 21.3, left). So every \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP pair is at least as hard to test as \((P^\star, Q^\star)\), at every level. By Blackwell’s theorem (1953), that holds exactly when it is a post-processing of \((P^\star, Q^\star)\). Kairouz, Oh & Viswanath (2015) construct the channel, and Section J.1 in the appendices derives it step by step, by a split of each distribution into an \(\varepsilon\)-DP part of mass \(1 - \delta\) and a leftover of mass \(\delta\).

The same reduction sharpens the drift term of advanced composition, because the KL divergence, like the hockey-stick, can only shrink under a channel.

Theorem 21.3 (The sharp mean loss) If the pair \((P, Q)\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP in both directions, then \[\mathbb E[L] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) \le \varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2), \tag{21.14}\] with equality for randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\).

Proof.

  1. Reduce to randomized response. By Theorem 21.2 with \(\delta = 0\), the pair is a post-processing of randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\) through the channel Equation 21.12. So by the data-processing inequality for the KL divergence, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\) is at most the KL divergence of randomized response.
  2. The KL divergence of randomized response. Its mean loss is \(\varepsilon t - \varepsilon(1 - t) = \varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\), by step 3 of the proof of Proposition 21.1 with \(k = 1\), and randomized response itself reaches it.

Example 21.3 The dashboard’s randomized-response steps need no channel, so take another pair: \(P = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)\) and \(Q = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)\) have largest loss \(\ln(0.5/0.2) = \ln 2.5 = 0.9163\), below \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\), so the pair is \(\ln 3\)-DP. With \(e^\varepsilon = 3\), Equation 21.12 gives \(K(\cdot \mid 0) = (3P - Q)/2 = (0.65, 0.30, 0.05)\) and \(K(\cdot \mid 1) = (3Q - P)/2 = (0.05, 0.30, 0.65)\) (Figure 21.3, right). Randomized response at \(\ln 3\) has \(t = \frac34\), and the first output comes back as \(\frac34 \times 0.65 + \frac14 \times 0.05 = 0.5\). As Theorem 21.3 requires, the pair’s KL divergence, \(0.5\ln 2.5 + 0.3\ln 1 + 0.2\ln 0.4 = 0.2749\), is below randomized response’s \(\ln 3 \times \frac12 = 0.5493\), and its \(\delta(0)\), the total variation \(0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3\), is below 0.5.

21.4 Optimal composition

With the worst mechanism in hand, the best bound for \(k\) mechanisms known only by their \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) is the exact profile of \(k\) copies of the four-point pair. Either some step raises a flag, and the output counts in full, or none does, and the loss is binomial by Equation 21.5. Lemma 21.1 turns that binomial loss into a \(\delta\), written \(\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon')\).

Theorem 21.4 (Optimal composition (Kairouz, Oh & Viswanath 2015)) For every composition of \(k\) mechanisms that are each \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, adaptive or not, and every \(\varepsilon' \ge 0\), \[\delta(\varepsilon') \le 1 - (1 - \delta)^k\big(1 - \delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon')\big), \qquad \delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon') = \sum_{j=0}^k\binom kj t^{k-j}(1 - t)^j\big(1 - e^{\varepsilon' - (k - 2j)\varepsilon}\big)_+, \tag{21.15}\] with equality for \(k\) independent copies of the four-point pair. The binomial weight \(\binom kj t^{k-j}(1 - t)^j\) is the probability that exactly \(j\) of the \(k\) reports point to \(D'\). At the points where \(\varepsilon'\) equals one of the values the loss can take, \(\varepsilon' = (k - 2i)\varepsilon\) for \(i = 0, \dots, \lfloor k/2\rfloor\), the positive part can be dropped and the sum becomes a ratio of exponentials, \[\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}\big((k - 2i)\varepsilon\big) = \frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{i-1}\binom k\ell\big(e^{(k-\ell)\varepsilon} - e^{(k - 2i + \ell)\varepsilon}\big)}{(1 + e^\varepsilon)^k}, \tag{21.16}\] which is the formula of Kairouz, Oh & Viswanath’s main theorem.

Proof. The plan is to show that a composition, adaptive or not, is a post-processing of \(k\) independent four-point pairs, so that the data-processing inequality reduces the bound to the four-point product, and then to evaluate Lemma 21.1 on that product.

  1. Non-adaptive. If step \(i\) is \(P_i = P^\star K_i\) and \(Q_i = Q^\star K_i\) by Theorem 21.2, then the product \(P_1 \times \dots \times P_k\) is \((P^\star)^k\) followed by the channel that applies \(K_i\) to coordinate \(i\), independently. So \(\delta(\varepsilon')\) of the composition is at most that of \((P^\star)^k\) against \((Q^\star)^k\), by the data-processing inequality.
  2. Adaptive. Now the mechanism of step \(i\), and so its channel \(K_i^{y_{<i}}\), may depend on the earlier outputs. The trick is to draw all the four-point outputs \(z_i\) first and apply the channels after. Draw \(z_1, \dots, z_k\) from \((P^\star)^k\), then \(y_1 \sim K_1(\cdot \mid z_1)\), then \(y_2 \sim K_2^{y_1}(\cdot \mid z_2)\), and so on. Given \(y_{<i}\), \(z_i\) is still a fresh draw from \(P^\star\), because it was drawn independently of \(z_{<i}\) and \(y_{<i}\) depends only on those. So \(y_i\) has law \(P^\star K_i^{y_{<i}} = P_i(\cdot \mid y_{<i})\), and the sequence has exactly the law of the adaptive run on \(D\). The same sampler fed with \((Q^\star)^k\) produces the run on \(D'\), so the adaptive run is still one post-processing of \((P^\star)^k\) against \((Q^\star)^k\).
  3. The four-point product. With probability \(1 - (1 - \delta)^k\) some coordinate of \((P^\star)^k\) shows “flag \(D\)”, which \((Q^\star)^k\) never produces, so there \(L = +\infty\), the bracket \((1 - e^{\varepsilon' - L})_+\) is 1, and the output contributes its full probability to Equation 21.3. Outputs with “flag \(D'\)” have probability 0 under \((P^\star)^k\). Otherwise, with probability \((1 - \delta)^k\), all coordinates are plain randomized response; the factor \((1 - \delta)\) per coordinate cancels in the ratio, so \(L = (k - 2J)\varepsilon\) by Equation 21.5, and Lemma 21.1 on this part gives \((1 - \delta)^k\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon')\). Adding the two parts gives \(1 - (1 - \delta)^k + (1 - \delta)^k\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon')\), which is Equation 21.15.
  4. Equality. The four-point pair is itself a mechanism, on a dataset of one bit, and it is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP. Composing \(k\) independent copies of it gives equality, so no better bound can hold for the whole class.
  5. The grid formula. At \(\varepsilon' = (k - 2i)\varepsilon\), the terms of \(\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}\) with \(j \ge i\) vanish, because their loss \((k - 2j)\varepsilon\) is at most \(\varepsilon'\) and the bracket is 0. For \(j = \ell < i\) the bracket is positive, so the positive part can go. Since \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\) and \(1 - t = 1/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\), the weight is \(\binom k\ell t^{k-\ell}(1-t)^\ell = \binom k\ell e^{(k-\ell)\varepsilon}/(1 + e^\varepsilon)^k\). Its product with \(e^{\varepsilon' - (k - 2\ell)\varepsilon} = e^{(2\ell - 2i)\varepsilon}\) turns \(e^{(k-\ell)\varepsilon}\) into \(e^{(k - 2i + \ell)\varepsilon}\), which is Equation 21.16.

“Optimal” means optimal for the class: Equation 21.15 is the best you can say about \(k\) mechanisms if all you know about each is its \((\varepsilon, \delta)\). A Laplace mechanism with the same \(\varepsilon\) does a little better, because its loss often lies strictly inside \((-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)\): its mean loss at \(\varepsilon = 1\) is 0.3679, against 0.4621 for randomized response.

A single \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) also throws information away. The privacy loss of a Gaussian step with noise \(\sigma\) on a sensitivity-1 query is \(\mathcal N(\mu^2/2, \mu^2)\) with \(\mu = 1/\sigma\) (Lemma 20.3). By Lemma 21.2 the loss of \(k\) independent steps is a sum of \(k\) such normals, which is \(\mathcal N(k\mu^2/2, k\mu^2)\). That is the loss of one step with \(\mu\sqrt k\), that is with noise \(\sigma/\sqrt k\), so its exact profile is Equation 20.16 at that \(\mu\). Figure 21.4 shows what a point summary costs: with \(\sigma = 20\), \(k = 1{,}000\) steps are exactly \((7.51, 10^{-5})\)-DP, but the best per-step split of \(\delta\) followed by optimal composition gives 62.7. Composition of the curves themselves is the subject of Privacy accounting.

The four-point product also extends advanced composition to steps with \(\delta > 0\), and with the sharper drift: the flags cost their probability, and the rest is randomized response.

Corollary 21.1 (Advanced composition for (ε, δ)-DP steps, with the sharp drift) Fix \(k\), \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(\delta \ge 0\) in advance, and let \(M_1, \dots, M_k\) be mechanisms, each chosen after seeing the earlier outputs, such that every \(M_i\) is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP for every history. Then for every \(\delta' > 0\) the composition is \((\varepsilon', k\delta + \delta')\)-DP with \(\varepsilon'\) from Equation 21.9, and also with \[\varepsilon' = \varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2). \tag{21.17}\]

Proof.

  1. Reduce to the four-point product. By step 2 of the proof of Theorem 21.4, the adaptive run is a post-processing of \(k\) independent copies of the four-point pair, and a channel never increases \(\delta(\varepsilon')\), so it suffices to bound the \(k\)-fold four-point product.
  2. The flags. Some coordinate shows “flag \(D\)” with probability \(1 - (1 - \delta)^k\), which is at most \(k\delta\) by the union bound. Those outputs are impossible under \(D'\) and add their whole probability to \(\delta(\varepsilon')\), so the flags cost at most \(k\delta\).
  3. The rest. Otherwise all \(k\) coordinates are plain randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\), which are \(k\) independent \(\varepsilon\)-DP steps, so Theorem 21.1 bounds their contribution at the \(\varepsilon'\) of Equation 21.9 by \(\delta'\). Their drift is exactly \(k\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\) by Proposition 21.1, so step 6 of that proof with this drift in place of \(k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)\) gives the same \(\delta'\) at the \(\varepsilon'\) of Equation 21.17. Adding the two parts gives \(k\delta + \delta'\).

Example 21.4 For the dashboard’s \(k = 100\) steps at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the optimal \(\varepsilon'\) at \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\) is 4.3068, and the sharper advanced bound of Equation 21.17 is \(4.7985 + 0.4996 = 5.2981\). The optimal value is 19% below it, 26% below the original advanced bound 5.8502, and 57% below basic composition’s 10. Table 21.4 compares the four bounds over \(k\). At \(k = 10\), below the crossover, both advanced bounds are worse than basic composition, while the optimal bound is slightly better, 0.994 against 1. If each step also has \(\delta = 5\times10^{-8}\), the flags use up \(1 - (1 - \delta)^{100} = 5.0\times10^{-6}\) of a total \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). The optimal \(\varepsilon'\) then rises to 4.4469, and advanced composition with the remaining \(\delta' = 5\times10^{-6}\) gives 5.9926.

Computing the bound. Equation 21.16 as printed divides two numbers that overflow: at \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(k = 1{,}000\), \((1 + e)^{1000} \approx 10^{570}\), beyond the largest double. The chapter’s script (Section 21.6) adds the non-negative terms of Equation 21.15 in log space with a log-sum-exp, so nothing overflows and nothing cancels. Between two grid points \(\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon') = A - e^{\varepsilon'}B\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are the masses under \(P\) and \(Q\) of the outputs with loss above \(\varepsilon'\). Both are constant between grid points, so the optimal \(\varepsilon'\) for a target \(\delta\) is exactly \(\ln((A - \delta)/B)\) on the right interval. With a different \(\varepsilon_i\) per step, the loss depends on which steps lied, not just how many, and the exact bound is #P-complete to compute (Murtagh & Vadhan, 2016).

21.5 Adaptive composition

In practice the next query depends on the last answer. An analyst zooms into the largest bin of a noisy histogram, and a training loop computes each gradient at parameters that came from earlier noisy gradients. The proofs above cover this. This section makes the model explicit, collects the guarantees in one statement, and says where adaptivity does break things.

Definition 21.3 (The k-fold adaptive composition experiment) An adversary plays \(k\) rounds against a hidden bit \(b \in \{0, 1\}\), fixed for the whole run (Dwork, Rothblum & Vadhan 2010). In round \(i\) it looks at the outputs \(y_1, \dots, y_{i-1}\) so far and, as any function of them, chooses a pair of neighbouring datasets \(D_i^0 \sim D_i^1\) and a mechanism \(M_i\) from a class of \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP mechanisms. It then receives \(y_i \sim M_i(D_i^b)\). Everything the adversary sees, its view, is the sequence \(V = (y_1, \dots, y_k)\). A composition theorem holds for the experiment when, for every adversary, the distributions of the view under \(b = 0\) and under \(b = 1\) satisfy its guarantee in both directions.

The claim is that every bound of this chapter holds in the experiment.

Theorem 21.5 (Every composition bound holds under adaptive choice) In the experiment of Definition 21.3, with \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\) fixed before the interaction starts, the views under \(b = 0\) and \(b = 1\) satisfy basic composition, \((k\varepsilon, k\delta)\)-DP; advanced composition, \((\varepsilon', k\delta + \delta')\)-DP with \(\varepsilon'\) from Equation 21.9 or Equation 21.17; and optimal composition, Equation 21.15.

Proof. Every argument above looked at one step at a time, given the history so far, and once the history is fixed, so is the adversary’s choice.

  1. The loss of the view is a sum. Lemma 21.2 applies with each term \(L_i(y_i \mid y_{<i})\) computed for the mechanism and the pair of datasets chosen after \(y_{<i}\). Nothing in Lemma 21.1, Lemma 21.2 or Lemma 21.3 needs the pair to be the same in every round. Each step only needs its own pair to be neighbours.
  2. Basic composition. For \(\delta = 0\), \(|L_i| \le \varepsilon\) for every history, so \(|L| \le k\varepsilon\) by Lemma 21.2. For \(\delta > 0\), step 1 of the proof of Corollary 21.1 reduces the run to \(k\) independent four-point pairs: a flag shows with probability at most \(k\delta\), and otherwise \(|L| \le k\varepsilon\), so \(\delta(k\varepsilon) \le k\delta\).
  3. Advanced composition. Azuma’s inequality needs only conditional statements, given the past: a conditional mean of zero and a range of fixed length. That is the setting of steps 2 to 5 of the proof of Theorem 21.1, and the \(\delta > 0\) extension goes through the same reduction as step 2.
  4. Optimal composition. Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 21.4 builds the adaptive run from the four-point product with history-dependent channels.

Adaptivity does not cover privacy parameters chosen on the fly. If the analyst picks \(\varepsilon_i\) after earlier outputs, the range length \(c_i = 2\varepsilon_i\) in the proof of Theorem 21.1 depends on the history, and Azuma’s inequality needs it fixed in advance. A rule such as “keep querying until the budget is spent” needs its own theorem, a privacy filter (Rogers, Roth, Ullman & Vadhan, 2016). A report of the privacy spent so far, at every moment, needs a privacy odometer. Adaptive composition treats both. For pure DP with basic composition the naive rule is fine, because \(|L| \le \sum_i\varepsilon_i\) holds pointwise whatever order the \(\varepsilon_i\) came in.

Nor does adaptivity cover choices that depend on the data rather than the outputs. A system that answers private counts until the true count of some group crosses a threshold reveals that count through the number of answers it gives. So every answer can be \(\varepsilon\)-DP and the whole run still not be DP for any finite \(\varepsilon\). A rule that stops early within a fixed \(k\), and looks only at the noisy answers, is covered, because the stopping time is then a post-processing of the outputs.

Example 21.5 The script simulates an analyst who, at each of the dashboard’s \(k = 100\) steps, chooses the next mechanism from the last output. If the previous output pointed to \(D\), that is its privacy loss was positive, the analyst asks a Laplace query at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), and otherwise a randomized-response query at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\). Over 100,000 simulated runs on \(D\), Table 21.5 estimates \(\delta(2)\) through Equation 21.3, as the average of \((1 - e^{2 - L})_+\) over the simulated total losses \(L\). The exact Laplace mean is 100 times the per-step KL divergence of two Laplace densities whose centres are \(\varepsilon\) scale units apart, \(e^{-\varepsilon} + \varepsilon - 1\).

The adaptive analyst’s \(\delta(2)\) lies between the two non-adaptive compositions, and below the optimal bound, as Theorem 21.5 requires. Figure 21.5 shows the whole curves: the three compositions nearly coincide, and the advanced-composition points lie far to the right of them.
