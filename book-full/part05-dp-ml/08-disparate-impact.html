45  Disparate impact

45.1 Why this matters

The chapter on private gradient descent showed that training the part’s running example with differential privacy (DP) at \(\varepsilon = 4\) costs less than one point of test accuracy: 0.9548 against 0.9610 without privacy. That number is an average over the test set. It says nothing about who loses the point. Bagdasaryan, Poursaeed & Shmatikov (2019) asked that question and found an uncomfortable answer: when a network is trained with stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with per-example clipping and Gaussian noise, DP-SGD, the accuracy it loses is concentrated on the classes and subgroups that are under-represented in the training data. In their gender classifier, for example, the drop in accuracy was much larger on Black faces than on white faces, and the gap between groups that the non-private model already had grew under DP. They called this the disparate impact of DP on model accuracy.

This matters in two ways. A model that is “less than a point worse” can be ten points worse on a group of people, and an average hides it. And the groups that pay are often the ones a privacy guarantee is meant to protect: small populations, rare conditions, unusual records. This chapter explains why it happens, from the two ingredients of DP-SGD, measures it on the running example, and asks what can be done about it.

45.1.1 The one idea: every record gets the same budget of influence, and every group gets the same noise

DP-SGD caps each record’s pull on the update at \(C\), then adds noise whose size depends only on \(C\) and \(\sigma\), not on the data. Both rules are blind to groups, and that is exactly why they are not neutral. A group’s pull on the expected update is at most its share of the records times \(C\), however badly the model fits it, so a group whose gradients are large is cut back more than a group whose gradients are small: that is the clipping mechanism. And the noise is the same for everyone, while a group’s signal is proportional to its size, so a small group’s signal-to-noise ratio is smaller by its share: that is the noise mechanism. Small groups and groups the model finds hard suffer from both, and the two compound, because clipping shrinks a group’s effective share and the noise then acts on the shrunk share.

Table 45.1 lists the results and where each is used.

Notation. As in DP-SGD, \(n\) records \(z_i = (x_i, y_i)\) train a model with weights \(\theta\), \(g_i\) is record \(i\)’s gradient, \(C\) the clipping norm, \(\sigma\) the noise multiplier, \(q\) the sampling rate, \(B = qn\) the expected batch size, \(T\) the number of steps and \(\eta\) the learning rate. The records are split into groups \(g = 1, \dots, K\); group \(g\) has \(n_g\) records and share \(s_g = n_g/n\). A record’s clipping weight is \(\min(1, C/\|g_i\|)\), the factor clipping multiplies its gradient by, and a group’s clipping weight \(w_g\) is its average over the group’s records. \(\mathrm{acc}_g\) is a model’s accuracy on test records of group \(g\). A subscript \(i\) always names a record and a subscript \(g\) or \(h\) a group, so \(g_i\) is a record’s gradient and \(\bar g_g\), defined below, a group’s average gradient.

The running example. The part’s running example classifies 50-dimensional inputs with a two-layer network (DP-SGD). This chapter adds a minority group. Of the \(n = 10{,}000\) training records, 9,500 are the first records of the running example’s fixed training set: standard normal inputs, labelled by the random teacher network. The other 500, a share of 5%, come from a different region and follow a different rule. Their inputs are \(x = 8u + z\), where \(u\) is a fixed unit vector and \(z\) is standard normal in a fixed 15-dimensional subspace orthogonal to \(u\), and their label is the sign of one coordinate of \(z\). So the minority’s records lie far from the majority’s, have larger inputs (average length 8.89 against 7.04), and need a rule that the majority does not teach. The non-private model learns both: it reaches 0.9529 on the majority and 0.9411 on the minority. Each group is tested on 20,000 fresh records of its own, so that the test error is small compared with the effects being measured. DP-SGD runs with the running example’s settings: \(q = 0.01\), \(C = 0.5\), \(\eta = 0.5\), \(T = 10{,}000\) steps and \(\delta = 10^{-5}\).

How to read this chapter. Section 45.3 defines what to measure and shows the effect. Section 45.4 and Section 45.5 explain it, one mechanism each; they are the core of the chapter. Section 45.6 is about measuring per-group accuracy honestly, and applies to any model. Section 45.7 discusses what can be done and what each fix costs.

45.2 Toolkit: four facts every derivation uses

45.2.1 Fact 1 — The expected update is a reweighted average gradient

Each DP-SGD step adds up the clipped gradients of a Poisson batch, adds Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(\sigma C\) to each coordinate, and divides by the public expected batch size \(B = qn\). Each record is in the batch with probability \(q\), so the expected sum is \(q\) times the sum over all records, and dividing by \(qn\) gives \[\mathbb E\Big[\frac{G_t}{B}\Big] = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\min\Big(1, \frac C{\|g_i\|}\Big)g_i, \tag{45.1}\] the average gradient with each record weighted by its clipping weight (DP-SGD). The noise on the averaged update has standard deviation \(\tau = \sigma C/B\) per coordinate, whatever the data. In the running example at \(\varepsilon = 4\), \(\sigma = 1.36\) and \(\tau = 1.36 \times 0.5/100 = 0.0068\).

45.2.2 Fact 2 — A drift beats a random walk after (noise/signal)² steps

Suppose each step moves the weights by a signal of length \(\ell_{\text{sig}}\) that keeps pointing the same way, plus independent noise of length \(\ell_{\text{noise}}\). After a number \(t\) of steps the signals add up to length \(t\ell_{\text{sig}}\). The noise vectors are independent with mean zero, so their squared lengths add, and their sum has length about \(\ell_{\text{noise}}\sqrt t\). The signal is longer once \(t\ell_{\text{sig}} > \ell_{\text{noise}}\sqrt t\), that is after \[t > \Big(\frac{\ell_{\text{noise}}}{\ell_{\text{sig}}}\Big)^2 \tag{45.2}\] steps. DP-SGD uses this to explain why training works when every step’s noise is ten times longer than its signal: at \(\varepsilon = 4\) the noise on the averaged gradient has length \(\tau\sqrt{3{,}329}\) for the network’s 3,329 weights, and the signal emerges after a few hundred steps.

45.2.3 Fact 3 — A shrinking recursion with noise settles at a fixed variance

Let a number evolve as \(x_{t+1} = (1 - a)x_t + e_t\), where \(0 < a < 2\) and the \(e_t\) are independent with mean zero and variance \(v\). Each step shrinks \(x\) toward zero by the factor \(1 - a\) and adds fresh noise. Because the noise is independent of the current value, variances add: \(\operatorname{Var}x_{t+1} = (1 - a)^2\operatorname{Var}x_t + v\). Since \((1 - a)^2 < 1\), this converges, and the limit \(V\) solves \(V = (1 - a)^2V + v\): \[V = \frac{v}{1 - (1 - a)^2} = \frac{v}{a(2 - a)}. \tag{45.3}\] For example, with \(a = 0.1\) and \(v = 1\) the variance settles at \(1/(0.1 \times 1.9) = 5.26\): a weak pull toward zero lets the noise accumulate to more than five times one step’s variance. Halving the pull roughly doubles the stationary variance. The number of steps to forget the starting point is about \(1/a\), since \((1 - a)^t \approx e^{-at}\).

45.2.4 Fact 4 — An accuracy measured on \(m\) records is a binomial proportion

If a model classifies each test record of a group correctly with probability \(p\), independently, then the number correct out of \(m\) is binomial with mean \(mp\) and variance \(mp(1 - p)\), and the measured accuracy \(k/m\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)/m}\). The Clopper–Pearson interval (Hypothesis testing) inverts the binomial distribution exactly: at the 95% level its upper end \(u\) is the accuracy at which \(k\) or fewer correct answers would have probability 0.025, and its lower end \(l\) the accuracy at which \(k\) or more would have probability 0.025. It covers the true accuracy with probability at least 0.95. The smaller the group, the wider the interval, in proportion to \(1/\sqrt m\).

45.3 Disparate impact, measured

45.3.1 Context

What exactly should you measure to say that DP hurts one group more than another, and does it happen on the running example? An accuracy gap between groups is not enough, because the non-private model may already have one. The question is how much privacy adds to each group’s error.

45.3.2 Statement

The privacy cost of group \(g\) is the accuracy the group loses when the same training recipe is made private: \[\pi_g = \mathrm{acc}_g(\text{non-private}) - \mathbb E\big[\mathrm{acc}_g(\text{private})\big], \tag{45.4}\] where the expectation is over the randomness of private training (Esipova et al. 2023 use this definition; Bagdasaryan et al. 2019 compare the same differences). DP has disparate impact when the privacy costs of groups differ, \(\pi_a \ne \pi_b\), and the privacy cost gap \(|\pi_a - \pi_b|\) measures how much.

On the running example with a 5% minority, Table 45.2 gives each group’s accuracy over three training runs per row.

45.3.3 Derivation

This statement is a definition and a measurement, so its “derivation” is the experiment’s design, and each choice in it is there to make the comparison fair.

  1. Same data, same recipe. Every row trains on the same 10,000 records with the same sampling rate, learning rate and number of steps. The only differences are the clipping (absent in the non-private row) and the noise multiplier, which the accountant of DP-SGD sets for each \(\varepsilon\).
  2. Large test sets. Each group is tested on 20,000 records, so by Fact 4 a measured accuracy near 0.9 has a standard deviation of \(\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1/20{,}000} = 0.0021\). The differences in the table are much larger than that.
  3. Several runs. Private training is random, and its spread differs between groups. Each row averages three runs and reports their standard deviation; the privacy cost subtracts the average private accuracy from the average non-private accuracy.
  4. A non-private model that serves both groups. The minority’s non-private accuracy, 0.9411, is within 1.2 points of the majority’s, 0.9529. Any large gap in the private rows is therefore caused by privacy, not inherited from the data.

45.3.4 Interpretation

The minority pays almost all of it. At \(\varepsilon = 4\) the majority loses 0.59 points of accuracy and the minority 9.34, 15.7 times as much. At \(\varepsilon = 8\), a budget at which DP-SGD found the running example nearly as accurate as non-private training, the majority loses nothing (its accuracy is 0.20 points higher) and the minority loses 8.58 points. The ratio is smaller at \(\varepsilon = 1\), 4.6, only because there the majority starts to lose too: 7.07 points against the minority’s 32.78. An average over a test set drawn like the training set, 95% majority, would report a loss of \(0.95 \times 0.0059 + 0.05 \times 0.0934 = 0.0103\) at \(\varepsilon = 4\), about one point.

Both mechanisms are visible. The clipping-only row has no noise at all, yet the minority loses 4.04 points while the majority gains 0.51: clipping alone is disparate. Adding noise then widens the gap steadily as \(\varepsilon\) falls. The next two sections explain each part.

The minority’s runs also vary more. At \(\varepsilon = 4\) the standard deviation over runs is 0.0211 for the minority and 0.0037 for the majority. A single private run says less about a small group than about a large one, a point Section 45.6 returns to.

45.3.5 Example: what the published experiments found

Bagdasaryan, Poursaeed & Shmatikov (2019) trained classifiers with DP-SGD on images and text and found the same pattern: the under-represented classes and subgroups, and the more complex ones, lost the most accuracy, and an existing gap between groups grew. Esipova et al. (2023) report, for MNIST digits where class 8 was undersampled to about 1% of the training set, a privacy cost of 8.9 points for the typical class 2 and 57.9 points for class 8 under DP-SGD (their Table 2, \(\varepsilon = 5.90\)). On the running example, Figure 45.1 shows the same shape: the majority’s curve is the curve of DP-SGD, the minority’s lies far below it.

45.3.6 Check yourself

  • Why is the privacy cost a better measure than the gap between the private model’s group accuracies? (Because part of the gap may be inherited from non-private training, which DP did not cause. The privacy cost subtracts each group’s own non-private accuracy.)
  • At \(\varepsilon = 4\), a test set with the training proportions shows an overall loss of under one point. What would a test set with equal numbers of both groups show? (The average of the two privacy costs, \((0.0059 + 0.0934)/2 = 0.0497\): about five points. The overall number depends on the test set’s mix as much as on the model.)

45.4 Clipping reweights the groups

45.4.1 Context

Clipping without noise changes nothing about privacy (without noise there is none), yet in Table 45.2 it costs the minority 4.04 points. How does a rule that treats every record the same way end up treating groups differently?

45.4.2 Statement

Split the expected update of Fact 1 by group. Write \(\bar g_g = \frac1{n_g}\sum_{i\in g}g_i\) for group \(g\)’s average gradient and \(m_g = \frac1{n_g}\sum_{i\in g}\min(1, C/\|g_i\|)\,g_i\) for its average clipped gradient. Then \[\mathbb E\Big[\frac{G_t}{B}\Big] = \sum_gs_g\,m_g, \qquad \|s_g\,m_g\| \le s_g\,C: \tag{45.5}\] each group pulls with its share times its average clipped gradient, and that pull is never longer than its share times \(C\), however large the group’s gradients are.

When the records within each group have parallel gradients, each group’s average clipped gradient is its average gradient scaled by a number, and the expected update becomes the gradient of a reweighted mixture of the groups: \[\mathbb E\Big[\frac{G_t}{B}\Big] = \Big(\sum_hs_h\,c_h\Big)\sum_g\tilde s_g\,\bar g_g, \qquad \tilde s_g = \frac{s_g\,c_g}{\sum_hs_h\,c_h}, \tag{45.6}\] where \(c_g = \|m_g\|/\|\bar g_g\|\) is the fraction of the group’s average gradient that survives clipping and \(\tilde s_g\) is its effective share. When all of a group’s records have the same norm, \(c_g\) is the group’s clipping weight \(w_g\).

45.4.3 Derivation

  1. Split the sum. Fact 1’s expected update is \(\frac1n\sum_i\min(1, C/\|g_i\|)g_i\). Group the records: \(\frac1n\sum_g\sum_{i\in g}(\cdots) = \sum_g\frac{n_g}n\cdot\frac1{n_g}\sum_{i\in g}(\cdots) = \sum_gs_gm_g\).
  2. Bound the pull. Each clipped gradient has length at most \(C\), and the length of an average is at most the average of the lengths (the triangle inequality), so \(\|m_g\| \le C\) and \(\|s_gm_g\| \le s_gC\).
  3. Parallel gradients. If every \(g_i\) in group \(g\) points along the same unit vector \(e_g\), then \(g_i = \|g_i\|e_g\) and \(\min(1, C/\|g_i\|)g_i = \min(\|g_i\|, C)e_g\). Averaging, \(m_g\) and \(\bar g_g\) both point along \(e_g\), so \(m_g = c_g\bar g_g\) with \(c_g = \|m_g\|/\|\bar g_g\|\).
  4. Factor out the total. \(\sum_gs_gc_g\bar g_g = (\sum_hs_hc_h)\sum_g\frac{s_gc_g}{\sum_hs_hc_h}\bar g_g\), which is Equation 45.6. The effective shares are non-negative and add up to 1.
  5. Equal norms. If all of group \(g\)’s records also have the same norm \(\nu_g\), then \(c_g = \min(\nu_g, C)/\nu_g = \min(1, C/\nu_g) = w_g\).

45.4.4 Interpretation

Two errors: length and direction. Equation 45.6 separates what clipping does into two parts. The factor \(\sum_hs_hc_h\) shortens the update, which a larger learning rate can undo. The effective shares change which mixture of the groups is being descended, and no learning rate undoes that. Esipova et al. (2023) make this distinction for a whole batch: they compare training with the clipped batch gradient’s length but the true direction against the true length but the clipped direction, and find the direction error far more harmful, especially to the under-represented class (their Table 1). In Figure 45.2, clipping shortens the update and also turns it toward the majority.

Which groups are cut back. A group’s effective share falls when its clipping weight falls, that is when its gradients are long compared with \(C\). Gradients are long for two reasons. The model fits the group badly: for the logistic loss, the derivative with respect to the output is \(-y\,\mathrm{sigmoid}(-yf)\), close to 1 in size for a misclassified record and close to 0 for a record classified with a wide margin. And the group’s inputs are large: a dense layer’s per-example gradient is the outer product of the back-propagated error and the layer’s input, whose norm is the product of the two norms (DP-SGD), so at the same error a record with a longer input has a longer gradient. Tran, Dinh & Fioretto (2021) identify both factors, gradient norms and the input norms behind them, as the source of the clipping part of disparate impact. A small group the model has not yet learned has both.

A feedback loop. Early in training every record is clipped and every group has a similar weight. As the majority is learned, its gradients shrink below \(C\) and its weight rises toward 1, while the minority, not yet learned, keeps long gradients and a low weight. Its effective share falls below its true share, so it is learned more slowly, so its gradients stay long. The loop ends only when the minority’s gradients fall below \(C\) too.

Where training stops. Clipped gradient descent stops where the expected update is zero, \(\sum_gs_gm_g = 0\), not where the true average gradient \(\sum_gs_g\bar g_g\) is zero. If the groups’ gradients conflict, the stopping point is that of the reweighted objective, which has moved toward the majority.

45.4.5 Example: two dimensions, then the running example

Take 95 majority records whose gradient is \((0.2, 0)\), already short, and 5 minority records whose gradient is \((0, 4)\), long because the model fits them badly. Without clipping the average gradient is \((0.95 \times 0.2, 0.05 \times 4) = (0.19, 0.2)\), of length 0.2759: the five minority records pull as hard as the ninety-five others, and the minority’s component is a fraction \(0.2/0.39 = 0.513\) of the sum of the two components. Clip at \(C = 0.5\). The majority’s gradients are untouched; the minority’s shrink to length 0.5. The average becomes \((0.19, 0.05 \times 0.5) = (0.19, 0.025)\), of length 0.1916, and the minority’s fraction falls to 0.116. The update has turned by 39.0 degrees toward the majority. Here \(c_{\text{maj}} = 1\) and \(c_{\text{min}} = 0.5/4 = 0.125\), and Equation 45.6 gives the minority an effective share of \(0.05 \times 0.125/(0.05 \times 0.125 + 0.95) = 0.0065\) instead of 0.05. Scaling every gradient by \(C/C_{\max}\) with \(C_{\max} = 4\), the largest norm, instead gives \((0.0238, 0.0250)\): eight times shorter, but at an angle of 0.0 degrees to the true average.

In the running example, Table 45.3 follows each group’s gradients during training with clipping and no noise, on 1,000 majority and the 500 minority training records.

At step 0 both groups are fully clipped, with similar weights, and the minority’s effective share, 0.0495, is its true share. By step 2,500 the majority’s median gradient norm has fallen to \(3.8 \times 10^{-4}\), though 18% of its records, those still near the decision boundary, are clipped. The minority’s median norm is still 2.7 and 70% of its records are clipped. Its effective share has fallen to 0.0251, half its true share, exactly while it most needs to be learned: its test accuracy is 0.6724 against the majority’s 0.9569. It recovers only as the minority is learned, to 0.0387 at the end. The same happens with noise (Figure 45.3): at \(\varepsilon = 4\) the minority’s effective share is 0.0261 at step 2,500 and 0.0358 at the end, where its weight is 0.637 against the majority’s 0.902. The noise keeps the minority’s gradients long, so clipping keeps cutting them back.

45.4.6 Check yourself

  • If you multiply the minority’s loss by 10, does its pull on the update grow tenfold? (Not for clipped records. Their gradients grow tenfold and are then rescaled back to length \(C\); only records whose upweighted gradient stays below \(C\) gain. Exercise 1 works it out.)
  • Why does a larger clipping norm reduce the clipping part of disparate impact? (It raises the clipping weights of the groups with long gradients more than those of groups whose gradients are already below \(C\), whose weight is already 1, so the effective shares move back toward the true shares.)

45.5 The noise floor of a small group

45.5.1 Context

Noise is added to the sum, the same for everyone. Why should it hurt a small group more than a large one? Because what the noise competes with is each group’s signal, and a group’s signal in the sum is proportional to its size.

45.5.2 Statement

A drift that is a fraction of the population’s. If the part of the expected update that serves group \(g\) is a fraction \(s_g\) of what the whole population’s signal would be, and the noise is unchanged, Fact 2 says that group’s signal emerges from the noise only after \[t_g = \frac1{s_g^2}\Big(\frac{\ell_{\text{noise}}}{\ell_{\text{sig}}}\Big)^2 \tag{45.7}\] steps, \(1/s_g^2\) times as many as for a signal the whole population shares.

A quadratic model of the noise floor. Suppose group \(g\)’s loss depends on its own block \(\theta_g\) of \(d_g\) weights, which the other groups’ losses do not touch, as \(L_g = \frac\lambda2\|\theta_g - \theta_g^*\|^2\), and the training objective is the share-weighted average \(\sum_gs_gL_g\). Run gradient descent with noise of standard deviation \(\tau\) per coordinate on the averaged gradient, as DP-SGD adds. Then group \(g\)’s excess loss settles at \[\mathbb E[L_g] = \frac{d_g\,\eta\,\tau^2}{2\,s_g\,(2 - \eta s_g\lambda)} \approx \frac{d_g\,\eta\,\tau^2}{4\,s_g}, \tag{45.8}\] and it takes about \(1/(\eta s_g\lambda)\) steps to get there. With clipping, \(s_g\) is replaced by \(s_gc_g\), the group’s share times the fraction of its gradient that survives clipping; when the majority is hardly clipped, this is close to the effective share \(\tilde s_g\) of Equation 45.6.

45.5.3 Derivation

  1. The group’s gradient. The objective’s gradient with respect to \(\theta_g\) is \(s_g\lambda(\theta_g - \theta_g^*)\): only group \(g\)’s loss depends on these weights, and it enters with weight \(s_g\).
  2. One step. With \(x = \theta_g - \theta_g^*\) and noise \(\tau\xi\) on the averaged gradient, \(\xi\) standard normal, the update \(\theta_g \leftarrow \theta_g - \eta(s_g\lambda x + \tau\xi)\) gives \(x \leftarrow (1 - \eta s_g\lambda)x - \eta\tau\xi\), coordinate by coordinate.
  3. Stationary variance. This is Fact 3 with \(a = \eta s_g\lambda\) and \(v = \eta^2\tau^2\): each coordinate settles at variance \(\eta^2\tau^2/(a(2 - a)) = \eta\tau^2/(s_g\lambda(2 - \eta s_g\lambda))\).
  4. Excess loss. \(L_g = \frac\lambda2\sum_jx_j^2\) over the \(d_g\) coordinates, so its expectation is \(\frac\lambda2d_g\) times the variance, which is Equation 45.8. For a small group \(\eta s_g\lambda\) is small and \(2 - \eta s_g\lambda \approx 2\).
  5. Time scale. By Fact 3 the starting point is forgotten after about \(1/a = 1/(\eta s_g\lambda)\) steps.
  6. The break-even law. If the population’s signal has length \(\ell_{\text{sig}}\) per step, the part that serves group \(g\) has length \(s_g\ell_{\text{sig}}\) and the noise length is still \(\ell_{\text{noise}}\); Fact 2 with \(s_g\ell_{\text{sig}}\) in place of \(\ell_{\text{sig}}\) gives Equation 45.7.
  7. With clipping. By Equation 45.6, with parallel gradients within the group, group \(g\)’s term in the expected update is \(s_gc_g\bar g_g\) instead of \(s_g\bar g_g\), while the noise is unchanged. Treating \(c_g\) as constant over the steps considered, steps 1 to 6 apply with \(s_gc_g\) in place of \(s_g\). If the majority’s \(c\) is close to 1, then \(\sum_hs_hc_h\) is close to 1 and \(s_gc_g\) is close to \(\tilde s_g\).

45.5.4 Interpretation

The floor scales like one over the share. A group with a twentieth of the records sits at about twenty times the excess loss of a group with all of them. The curvature \(\lambda\) cancels: a group whose loss is sharper is pulled back harder, but each unit of noise also costs it more, and the two effects balance. What does not cancel is the share, because the noise is not scaled by it. It also takes the small group \(1/s_g\) times longer to reach even that floor.

Why waiting does not help. At a fixed budget, more steps require more noise per step. Under the small-\(q\) law of DP-SGD, multiplying the number of steps by \(m\) multiplies the noise variance per step by about \(m\), so the break-even count of Equation 45.7 grows as fast as the run (Exercise 3).

The two mechanisms compound. Clipping lowers a group’s pull from \(s_g\) to \(s_gc_g\); the noise floor scales like one over the pull. A group that is 5% of the data and whose gradients clipping halves pays the floor of a group of 2.5%.

How good is the model. The quadratic model is exact for what it assumes, as the simulation below confirms, but a network is not quadratic and its groups do not use disjoint weights. Where the minority’s task overlaps the majority’s, the majority’s gradient does part of the minority’s work, and the penalty is smaller. Tran, Dinh & Fioretto (2021) derive a related per-step decomposition for DP-SGD without assuming disjoint weights: the expected change in a group’s loss has a clipping term, driven by the group’s gradient norms, and a noise term proportional to \(\eta^2\sigma^2C^2\) times the trace of the group’s loss Hessian, which they relate to the group’s distance to the decision boundary and its input norms. Section M.5 in the appendices derives this decomposition, which is exact for quadratic losses, and shows that it contains Equation 45.8. The quadratic model makes the role of the share explicit, which their per-step terms leave implicit.

45.5.5 Example: the model, then the running example

At \(\varepsilon = 4\) the noise on the averaged gradient has \(\tau = 0.0068\) per coordinate. Take \(d_g = 51\) weights per group, \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\eta = 0.5\). Table 45.4 compares Equation 45.8 with a simulation of 40,000 noisy steps, averaged over the second half.

The formula and the simulation agree to within 1%, and the floor grows from \(4.07 \times 10^{-4}\) for a group with 95% of the data to \(2.96 \times 10^{-2}\) for a group with 1%, a factor of 73. The approximate form of Equation 45.8 would say 95, the ratio of the shares; the exact form is lower because the factor \(2 - \eta s_g\lambda\) is 1.525 for the large group rather than 2. The break-even law is harsher. In DP-SGD the averaged clipped gradient of the running example has length about 0.026 during training, and at \(\varepsilon = 4\) the noise has length \(\tau\sqrt{3{,}329} = 0.392\). A signal shared by the whole population emerges after \((0.392/0.026)^2 = 228\) steps. A signal that is 20% of it needs 5,693 steps, 5% needs 91,085, nine times the entire run, and 1% needs 2,277,115. A group whose rule the rest of the data does not teach is in this position.

In the network, Table 45.5 varies the minority’s share with everything else fixed.

The minority’s privacy cost falls steeply with its share: 34.88 points at 1%, 9.34 at 5%, 2.80 at 20%. Multiplying the share by 5, from 1% to 5%, divides the cost by \(0.3488/0.0934 = 3.7\), and multiplying it by 4, from 5% to 20%, divides it by \(0.0934/0.0280 = 3.3\): a little less than the ratio of the shares, which is what Equation 45.8 predicts for the loss. Accuracy is not loss, and it saturates: at 1% the private model is at chance on the minority, 0.5009, and cannot lose more. The majority’s cost stays below one point throughout. With 20% of the data, the minority is no longer a minority in any sense that matters to the noise, and its privacy cost is within three points of the majority’s (Figure 45.4, left).

45.5.6 Check yourself

  • The noise multiplier is doubled. By how much does the noise floor of Equation 45.8 grow, and does it grow more for the small group? (By a factor of 4, since it is proportional to \(\tau^2\), for every group alike. The gap between groups grows by the same factor in absolute terms; the ratio between them, \(s_{\text{major}}/s_{\text{minor}}\), is unchanged.)
  • Why does the curvature \(\lambda\) drop out of the approximate noise floor? (The pull toward the optimum and the cost of a displacement are both proportional to \(\lambda\); the stationary variance falls like \(1/\lambda\) and the loss per unit of variance rises like \(\lambda\).)

45.6 Measuring per-group accuracy

45.6.1 Context

Every number so far was measured on 20,000 test records per group. Real evaluations are not so generous: a test split of 2,000 records with a 5% minority holds 100 minority records. How precisely can a group’s accuracy, and the gap between two groups, be measured, and how much does a single training run say?

45.6.2 Statement

The test-set error of a group’s accuracy is binomial (Fact 4): with \(k\) correct of \(m\) records, report the Clopper–Pearson interval. For the gap between two groups measured on disjoint test records, the two estimates are independent and their variances add, so an approximate 95% interval is \[(\hat p_a - \hat p_b) \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_a(1 - \hat p_a)}{m_a} + \frac{\hat p_b(1 - \hat p_b)}{m_b}}, \tag{45.9}\] with \(\hat p_g\) the measured accuracies and \(m_g\) the numbers of test records. Training randomness is a second, separate source of variation: a group’s accuracy varies from run to run, more for small groups, and is estimated only by training several times.

45.6.3 Derivation

  1. One group. Each test record is classified correctly or not, independently given the model, with probability equal to the group’s true accuracy \(p_g\). The count is binomial, and Clopper–Pearson inverts it exactly (Hypothesis testing).
  2. The gap. The two counts come from disjoint test records, so they are independent given the model. The variance of a difference of independent estimates is the sum of their variances, \(p_a(1 - p_a)/m_a + p_b(1 - p_b)/m_b\). For large counts the difference is approximately normal by the central limit theorem, and 1.96 standard deviations on each side cover 95%.
  3. Two sources. Conditional on the trained model, the spread comes from the test set alone. Over training runs, the model itself varies; by the law of total variance the spread of a single run’s measured accuracy is the test-set variance plus the run-to-run variance of the true accuracy. Only repeated training estimates the second.

45.6.4 Interpretation

Small groups need intervals. The width of the Clopper–Pearson interval falls like \(1/\sqrt m\) (Figure 45.5). A 5-point gap between groups can be seen only with a few hundred minority test records, and a 1-point gap only with thousands (Exercise 4). Report the interval, not only the point estimate, for every group.

Report the spread over runs. In Table 45.2 the run-to-run standard deviation at \(\varepsilon = 4\) is 0.0211 for the minority against 0.0037 for the majority, almost six times as large. A comparison of two training recipes on one run each can easily be reversed for the small group. This is the same lesson as for the overall accuracy of private models (DP-SGD), sharper for small groups.

The evaluation leaks too. Per-group accuracies computed on private test data are statistics of that data. If they are published, they need their own privacy analysis, which Choosing ε and the selection mechanisms of Hyperparameter tuning address.

45.6.5 Example: one model, three test sets

Train the running example at \(\varepsilon = 4\) once. On all 20,000 minority test records its accuracy is 0.8641. Table 45.6 measures it on random subsets.

With 100 minority records, the split a 2,000-record test set with a 5% minority would give, the estimate is 0.880, 1.6 points above the true 0.8641, and the interval is 13.6 points wide. The true accuracy is inside it, as it should be 95% of the time, and the non-private model’s 0.9411 from Table 45.2 lies only just above its upper end, 0.936: on this test set alone the minority’s privacy cost could be anywhere from about half a point to fourteen points. With 500 records the interval is 6.5 points wide and still contains the true value. The majority’s interval, from 1,900 records at a higher accuracy, is 2 points wide. By Equation 45.9 the gap between the groups, measured on the 1,900 and the 100 records, is \(0.954 - 0.880 = 0.074\) with a standard error of \(\sqrt{0.954 \times 0.046/1{,}900 + 0.88 \times 0.12/100} = 0.0329\), so its 95% interval is \(0.074 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0329 = 0.074 \pm 0.064\), from 0.010 to 0.138: the gap is real, but it could be anywhere from one point to fourteen. And this is one training run: by Table 45.2 the minority’s accuracy at \(\varepsilon = 4\) varies by 0.0211 from run to run on top of this.

45.6.6 Check yourself

  • A report gives a private model’s accuracy as 0.84 on a subgroup of 60 test records and 0.85 non-private on the same records. What can you conclude? (Almost nothing about this subgroup. Each accuracy’s 95% interval is about \(2 \times 1.96\sqrt{0.84 \times 0.16/60} = 0.19\) wide, nineteen times the one-point difference. Because both models were tested on the same records, the difference could be measured more precisely from the records on which they disagree, but the report does not say how many there are.)
  • Why doesn’t a larger test set fix the run-to-run variation? (It removes the test-set error for the model at hand, but a different training run produces a different model. Only repeated training estimates how much a group’s accuracy varies with the training noise.)

45.7 Mitigations and what they cost

45.7.1 Context

Both mechanisms come from DP-SGD’s two knobs. Can you choose the knobs, or change the algorithm, so that small groups pay less, without spending more privacy? Each fix in this section moves cost around rather than removing it, and it is worth knowing where to.

45.7.2 Statement

Global scaling is a large clipping norm. Scaling every per-example gradient by \(C/C_{\max}\), where \(C_{\max}\) is a bound that no gradient’s norm exceeds, and adding noise \(\sigma C\), is the same algorithm as DP-SGD with the clipping norm set to \(C_{\max}\), the same noise multiplier and learning rate \(\eta C/C_{\max}\): \[\frac\eta B\Big(\frac C{C_{\max}}\sum_{i\in S_t}g_i + \sigma C\xi\Big) = \frac{\eta C/C_{\max}}B\Big(\sum_{i\in S_t}g_i + \sigma C_{\max}\xi\Big). \tag{45.10}\]

Oversampling a group costs that group’s privacy. If group \(g\)’s records are sampled at rate \(vq\) instead of \(q\), its pull on the update grows by the factor \(v\), and each of its records has the privacy of the subsampled Gaussian at rate \(vq\): \[\varepsilon_g = \varepsilon_{\text{acct}}(vq, \sigma, T, \delta) \;>\; \varepsilon_{\text{acct}}(q, \sigma, T, \delta), \tag{45.11}\] where \(\varepsilon_{\text{acct}}\) is the accountant of DP-SGD. The algorithm’s guarantee is the largest over records, so a fixed target \(\varepsilon\) requires the noise multiplier for rate \(vq\).

45.7.3 Derivation

  1. Global scaling. Factor \(C/C_{\max}\) out of the left-hand side: \(\frac C{C_{\max}}\sum_ig_i + \sigma C\xi = \frac C{C_{\max}}(\sum_ig_i + \sigma C_{\max}\xi)\). With the clipping norm set to \(C_{\max}\) and no gradient longer than \(C_{\max}\), DP-SGD’s clipped sum is \(\sum_ig_i\) and its noise \(\sigma C_{\max}\xi\), which is the right-hand side. Both have sensitivity-to-noise ratio \(1/\sigma\), so the same accountant applies (Exercise 6).
  2. Oversampling: the pull. The expected clipped sum is \(\sum_iq_i\min(1, C/\|g_i\|)g_i\), with \(q_i\) record \(i\)’s sampling rate. With \(q_i = vq\) for group \(g\) and division by the public \(B = qn\), group \(g\)’s term becomes \(vs_gm_g\).
  3. Oversampling: the privacy. The add/remove analysis of record \(x\) in DP-SGD compares the output with and without \(x\); the mixture depends only on \(x\)’s own inclusion probability, because the other records’ terms are the same in both. A record sampled at rate \(vq\) therefore has the privacy of the subsampled Gaussian at rate \(vq\). The sampling rate may depend on the record’s own group, which is part of the record, but the rule must be fixed in advance, not chosen by looking at the data.
  4. The guarantee. \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP must hold for every record, so it is the largest of the per-record values, which is Equation 45.11’s.

45.7.4 Interpretation

Global scaling trades direction for noise. By Equation 45.10, scaling all gradients by the same factor is the large-\(C\) end of the clipping-norm curve of DP-SGD with \(\eta C\) fixed: nothing is clipped, so the update points in the true direction (the blue arrow of Figure 45.2), but the noise \(\sigma C_{\max}\) is as large as possible relative to the gradients. Esipova et al. (2023) propose a version that clips the rare gradients above \(C_{\max}\) instead of discarding them and adapts \(C_{\max}\) during training from a privately estimated count of how many gradients exceed it; they report that it reduces the privacy cost gap and improves accuracy compared with DP-SGD and with earlier mitigations on MNIST, CelebA and two tabular datasets. Because it treats every record alike, it needs no group labels.

Group-aware methods need group labels. Xu, Du & Wu (2021) adjust each group’s contribution according to how much that group is being clipped, giving groups that are clipped more a larger clipping norm, so that the groups’ clipping biases are equalized. Tran, Dinh & Fioretto (2021) add to the training objective penalties on the differences between groups in the quantities their decomposition identifies. Both need each training record’s group, which is itself sensitive and may not be available or lawful to collect, and the per-group statistics they use must be computed privately.

Oversampling buys little at a fixed budget. Fixed at \(\varepsilon = 4\) for every record, sampling the minority four times as often requires \(\sigma = 4.71\) instead of 1.36: the minority’s pull grows by 4 and the noise by \(4.71/1.36 = 3.46\). Its signal-to-noise ratio improves by only \(4/3.46 = 1.16\), while the majority’s falls by 3.46, and the majority’s records are then far more private than the target, \(\varepsilon = 0.86\), which is wasted budget (Exercise 5). Oversampling without changing \(\sigma\) would silently give the minority \(\varepsilon = 20.96\): the group that is already disadvantaged in accuracy would also get the weakest privacy.

Some of the cost is unavoidable. Learning a group’s own rule privately is, in part, a private estimation problem on that group’s \(n_g\) records, and private estimation has a price that no algorithm avoids. Lower bounds proves it for the simplest case: releasing \(d\) attribute means with constant accuracy under \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP needs at least about \(\sqrt d/(e^\varepsilon - 1)\) records, by the fingerprinting argument, whatever the mechanism. Put differently, the error that \(n_g\) records allow shrinks only like \(1/(n_g\varepsilon)\), and the Gaussian mechanism, with error about \(\sqrt{d\ln(1/\delta)}/(n_g\varepsilon)\), is close to the best possible. A smaller group is a smaller dataset, and no clever clipping makes it larger. Feldman (2020) argues the deeper point for any learner: when the data have a long tail of small subpopulations, fitting them well requires memorizing their few examples, which is exactly what DP forbids. What helps is more data from the group, a larger \(\varepsilon\) (with its own costs), or a starting point that already serves the group, such as a public pretrained model (Public data and private fine-tuning).

45.7.5 Example: the clipping norm and oversampling on the running example

Table 45.7 compares three changes at \(\varepsilon = 4\) for every record.

None of the three changes helps the minority. A ten times smaller clipping norm clips the minority more, and its accuracy falls by 1.64 points while the majority’s rises by 0.71: the clipping mechanism of Section 45.4 at work. A ten times larger one clips almost nothing, so the update’s direction is right, but with \(\eta C\) fixed the noise in each step, \(\eta\sigma C/B\), is unchanged while the step along every gradient shorter than 0.5 is ten times smaller: the noise is ten times larger relative to that part of the signal, and the minority falls by 3.44 points: the noise mechanism of Section 45.5 at work. The running example’s \(C = 0.5\) sits between the two failures. These differences are of the size of the run-to-run spread, 0.0157 to 0.0211, so only their direction is clear, not their size.

Oversampling narrows the gap from 9.93 to 3.99 points, but by levelling down: the minority’s accuracy is 2.07 points lower than without it, and the majority’s 8.01 points lower. A smaller gap is not by itself a fairer model. Report both groups’ accuracy and privacy cost, never the gap alone.

45.7.6 Check yourself

  • Why does oversampling a group require more noise, while upweighting its loss does not? (Upweighting leaves every record’s contribution bounded by \(C\) and its sampling rate at \(q\), so the accountant is unchanged, but clipping undoes it. Oversampling raises the group’s sampling rate, which the subsampled Gaussian’s privacy depends on.)
  • A team proposes to fix disparate impact by setting \(C\) to the largest per-example gradient norm observed in the training data. What is wrong? (The clipping norm then depends on the data, and the sensitivity argument of DP-SGD assumes it is fixed in advance. A data-dependent \(C\) must itself be chosen privately, as in Esipova et al.’s adaptive threshold, and the estimate charged to the budget.)

What to take from it. The disparate impact of DP-SGD needs no special data to appear: a 5% group that lives in another region of input space and follows its own rule is enough. Its two causes can be separated by switching the noise off, and each follows the arithmetic of this chapter: clipping lowers the group’s effective share while the majority is learned, and the noise penalizes a small share roughly in proportion to one over the share. Neither knob of DP-SGD removes the effect at a fixed budget; it only moves it between the groups and between the two mechanisms.

45.10 Cheat sheet

Group \(g\) has share \(s_g\), average gradient \(\bar g_g\) and clipping weight \(w_g\), the mean of \(\min(1, C/\|g_i\|)\) over its records; \(\tau = \sigma C/B\). Table 45.9 collects the results.

Rules of thumb

  • Always report accuracy per group, with Clopper–Pearson intervals and the spread over several training runs, next to the non-private model’s.
  • Expect the privacy cost of a group to grow like one over its share of the data, and faster for groups with long gradients: large inputs or a rule the rest of the data does not teach.
  • Clipping alone is disparate. A larger clipping norm (with \(\eta C\) fixed) reduces the clipping part and raises the noise part.
  • Do not upweight a group’s loss to help it: clipping undoes it. Do not oversample a group without accounting at its higher sampling rate.
  • When a group is too small to learn privately, get more data from it or start from a public model that already serves it.
