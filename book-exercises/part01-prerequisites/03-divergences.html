3  Exercises: Divergences

Exercises for Divergences, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part01-prerequisites/solutions_divergences.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part01-prerequisites/solutions_divergences.py.

The solutions use the chapter’s notation and its three acronyms: the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, the total variation (TV) distance and the data processing inequality (DPI). \(r = p/q\) is the likelihood ratio and \(L = \ln r\) the log-likelihood ratio, logarithms are natural, and on the die \(P\) is the loaded die and \(Q\) the fair one (Table 3.1).

3.1 Chain rule for KL

Exercise 1. Chain rule for KL. For joint distributions \(P_{XY}\) and \(Q_{XY}\), show \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{XY}\|Q_{XY}) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_X\|Q_X) + \mathbb E_{x\sim P_X}\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{Y\mid x}\|Q_{Y\mid x})\big]\), and recover additivity as the special case of independent coordinates. Hint: factor \(p(x, y) = p(x)p(y\mid x)\) inside the logarithm. (KL, additivity)

TipSolution
  1. Factor both joints. \(p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y\mid x)\) and \(q(x, y) = q(x)\,q(y\mid x)\), where \(p(x)\), \(q(x)\) are the marginals of \(X\).
  2. The log-likelihood ratio splits into a sum (the logarithm of a product): \[\ln\frac{p(x, y)}{q(x, y)} = \ln\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} + \ln\frac{p(y\mid x)}{q(y\mid x)}. \tag{3.1}\]
  3. Average the first term under \(P_{XY}\). It depends only on \(x\), so its average is \(\sum_xp(x)\ln\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_X\|Q_X)\).
  4. Average the second term in two stages: first over \(y\) given \(x\), then over \(x\). For fixed \(x\), \(\sum_yp(y\mid x)\ln\frac{p(y\mid x)}{q(y\mid x)} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{Y\mid x}\|Q_{Y\mid x})\). Averaging over \(x \sim P_X\) gives the expected conditional KL.
  5. Add the two averages (linearity of expectation). This is the chain rule.

Additivity. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent under both distributions, then \(p(y\mid x) = p_2(y)\) and \(q(y\mid x) = q_2(y)\) for every \(x\). The conditional KL in step 4 is then \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_2\|Q_2)\) for every \(x\), and its average over \(x\) is the same number. The chain rule becomes Equation 3.6.

Concretely, on the die. Let \(Y\) be the roll and \(X\) its parity. Then \((X, Y)\) carries the same information as \(Y\), so the left side is \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) = 0.2939\). The parity part is \(0.0823\) (from Table 3.12). Given “even”, the loaded die gives rolls 2, 4, 6 with probabilities \(0.1/0.7, 0.1/0.7, 0.5/0.7 = \frac17, \frac17, \frac57\), and the fair die gives \(\frac13\) each. Given “odd”, both are uniform on 1, 3, 5.

The expected conditional KL is \(0.7 \times 0.3023 = 0.2116\), and \(0.0823 + 0.2116 = 0.2939\), the KL of the raw roll.

The chain rule says exactly how much the data-processing inequality loses: the KL dropped by keeping only \(X\) is the expected KL of what you threw away.

3.2 Gaussians with different variances

Exercise 2. Gaussians with different variances. Show \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal N(m_0, \sigma_0^2)\|\mathcal N(m_1, \sigma_1^2)) = \ln\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} + \frac{\sigma_0^2 + (m_0 - m_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2} - \frac12\). Check: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal N(0, 1)\|\mathcal N(0, 4)) = \ln2 + \frac18 - \frac12 = 0.3181\). For the Rényi divergence, repeat route 1 of the Gaussian section and find the condition on \(\alpha\) under which the integral is finite. Hint: the coefficient of \(x^2\) must stay negative: \(\alpha/\sigma_0^2 + (1-\alpha)/\sigma_1^2 > 0\). (Completing the square)

TipSolution

Write \(P = \mathcal N(m_0, \sigma_0^2)\) and \(Q = \mathcal N(m_1, \sigma_1^2)\), with means \(m_0, m_1\), and \(\Delta = m_0 - m_1\).

KL.

  1. The log-likelihood ratio. The normal density is \(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x - m)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\) for mean \(m\), so \[L(x) = \ln\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} - \frac{(x - m_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2} + \frac{(x - m_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}. \tag{3.2}\] Unlike the equal-variance case, \(L\) is quadratic in \(x\).
  2. Two second moments under \(P\). \(\mathbb E_P(X - m_0)^2 = \sigma_0^2\). For the other, write \(X - m_1 = (X - m_0) + \Delta\); the cross term averages to 0, so \(\mathbb E_P(X - m_1)^2 = \sigma_0^2 + \Delta^2\).
  3. Average \(L\) under \(P\): \(D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \ln\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} - \frac12 + \frac{\sigma_0^2 + \Delta^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\), the claimed formula.
  4. Check. For \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\|\mathcal N(0, 4)\): \(\sigma_0 = 1\), \(\sigma_1 = 2\), \(\Delta = 0\), so \(D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \ln2 + \frac18 - \frac12 = 0.3181\). A numerical integral of \(p\ln(p/q)\) gives 0.3181 as well. In the other direction, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal N(0, 4)\|\mathcal N(0, 1)) = -\ln2 + 2 - \frac12 = 0.8069\): KL is not symmetric here. With \(\sigma_0 = \sigma_1\) the formula reduces to \(\Delta^2/(2\sigma^2)\), as in Equation 3.15.

Rényi, by route 1.

  1. Constants. \(p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}\) has the constant factor \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_0^{-\alpha}\sigma_1^{-(1-\alpha)}\) times \(\exp\big(-\frac12B(x)\big)\), with \[B(x) = w_0(x - m_0)^2 + w_1(x - m_1)^2, \qquad w_0 = \frac{\alpha}{\sigma_0^2},\quad w_1 = \frac{1 - \alpha}{\sigma_1^2}. \tag{3.3}\]
  2. Finiteness. The coefficient of \(x^2\) in \(-\frac12B\) is \(-\frac12(w_0 + w_1)\). If \(w_0 + w_1 \le 0\), the integrand does not decay in at least one direction (it grows like \(e^{cx^2}\), or, when the coefficient is exactly 0, like \(e^{cx}\) or a constant), and the integral is infinite. So the integral is finite exactly when \[w_0 + w_1 = \frac{\alpha}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{1 - \alpha}{\sigma_1^2} > 0 \iff \sigma_\alpha^2 := \alpha\sigma_1^2 + (1 - \alpha)\sigma_0^2 > 0 \tag{3.4}\] (multiply by \(\sigma_0^2\sigma_1^2 > 0\)). Note \(w_0 + w_1 = \sigma_\alpha^2/(\sigma_0^2\sigma_1^2)\).
  3. Complete the square. For weights with \(w_0 + w_1 > 0\), \(w_0(x - m_0)^2 + w_1(x - m_1)^2 = (w_0 + w_1)(x - \bar m)^2 + \frac{w_0w_1}{w_0 + w_1}\Delta^2\) with \(\bar m = \frac{w_0m_0 + w_1m_1}{w_0 + w_1}\). (Expand both sides: the \(x^2\) and \(x\) terms match by the choice of \(\bar m\), and the constant is what is left.) Here \(\frac{w_0w_1}{w_0 + w_1} = \frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\sigma_0^2\sigma_1^2}\cdot\frac{\sigma_0^2\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_\alpha^2} = \frac{\alpha(1 - \alpha)}{\sigma_\alpha^2}\).
  4. Integrate. \(\int e^{-(w_0 + w_1)(x - \bar m)^2/2}dx = \sqrt{2\pi/(w_0 + w_1)} = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_0\sigma_1/\sigma_\alpha\). So \[\int p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}\,dx = \frac{\sigma_0^{1-\alpha}\sigma_1^{\alpha}}{\sigma_\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)\Delta^2}{2\sigma_\alpha^2}\right). \tag{3.5}\]
  5. Take the logarithm and divide by \(\alpha - 1\). The exponent gives \(\frac{\alpha\Delta^2}{2\sigma_\alpha^2}\), as in the equal-variance case. The prefactor gives \(\frac{(1-\alpha)\ln\sigma_0 + \alpha\ln\sigma_1 - \ln\sigma_\alpha}{\alpha - 1} = \ln\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} + \frac{\ln\sigma_1 - \ln\sigma_\alpha}{\alpha - 1}\). Together, \[D_\alpha(P\|Q) = \ln\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} + \frac{1}{2(\alpha - 1)}\ln\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_\alpha^2} + \frac{\alpha\Delta^2}{2\sigma_\alpha^2} \quad\text{if } \sigma_\alpha^2 > 0, \qquad D_\alpha = \infty \text{ otherwise}. \tag{3.6}\]

When is it finite? For \(0 < \alpha < 1\), \(\sigma_\alpha^2\) is a weighted average of two positive numbers, so always positive. For \(\alpha > 1\), \(\sigma_\alpha^2 = \sigma_0^2 + \alpha(\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_0^2)\). If \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_0\) (the second distribution is at least as wide), it is positive for every \(\alpha\). If \(\sigma_1 < \sigma_0\), it is positive only for \[\alpha < \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_0^2 - \sigma_1^2}. \tag{3.7}\] The reason: \(r^{\alpha-1}\) grows like \(e^{cx^2}\) in the tails when \(Q\) is the narrower one, and for large \(\alpha\) that beats the decay of \(p\).

Checks. As \(\alpha \to 1\), \(\sigma_\alpha^2 \to \sigma_1^2\) and \(\frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\ln\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_\alpha^2} \to -\frac{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}\) (the derivative of \(-\ln\sigma_\alpha^2\) at \(\alpha = 1\)), so \(D_\alpha \to \ln\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} + \frac{\sigma_0^2}{2\sigma_1^2} - \frac12 + \frac{\Delta^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\), the KL above. With \(\sigma_0 = \sigma_1\), \(\sigma_\alpha = \sigma\) and only \(\alpha\Delta^2/(2\sigma^2)\) remains, which is Equation 3.15. Table 3.2 compares the formula with a numerical integral.

For \(\mathcal N(0, 4)\|\mathcal N(0, 1)\) the limit is \(\alpha < \frac{4}{4 - 1} = 1.3333\): every order \(\alpha \ge \frac43\) is infinite, although the KL (order 1) is only 0.8069.

A wider \(P\) against a narrower \(Q\) makes the likelihood ratio grow like \(e^{cx^2}\), and then only low Rényi orders are finite.

3.3 Bhattacharyya and Hellinger

Exercise 3. Bhattacharyya and Hellinger. Show \(D_{1/2}(P\|Q) = -2\ln\big(1 - \frac12H^2\big)\) with \(H^2 = \sum(\sqrt p - \sqrt q)^2\), and check it on the die using Table 3.11 and Table 3.6. For the Gaussian pair, show \(D_{1/2} = \mu^2/4\). (Rényi, f-divergences)

TipSolution
  1. \(D_{1/2}\) in terms of the Bhattacharyya coefficient. Put \(\alpha = \frac12\) in the definition: \(D_{1/2} = \frac{1}{-1/2}\ln\sum_xp^{1/2}q^{1/2} = -2\ln\mathrm{BC}\), with \(\mathrm{BC} = \sum_x\sqrt{p(x)q(x)}\).
  2. Expand the Hellinger sum. \((\sqrt p - \sqrt q)^2 = p - 2\sqrt{pq} + q\). Sum over \(x\): \(p\) and \(q\) each sum to 1, so \(H^2 = 2 - 2\,\mathrm{BC}\).
  3. Solve for \(\mathrm{BC}\): \(\mathrm{BC} = 1 - \frac12H^2\). Substitute into step 1: \(D_{1/2} = -2\ln\big(1 - \frac12H^2\big)\).

On the die. Table 3.11 gives \(H^2 = 0.1317\). Then \(\mathrm{BC} = 1 - 0.06585 = 0.9342\) and \(-2\ln0.9342 = 0.1362\), the \(\alpha = 0.5\) entry of Table 3.6 in both directions.

For the Gaussian pair. \(P = \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\), \(Q = \mathcal N(s, \sigma^2)\), \(\mu = s/\sigma\).

  1. Step 4 of route 1 in the Gaussian section, at \(\alpha = \frac12\), gives \(\mathrm{BC} = \int\sqrt{pq}\,dx = \exp\big(-\frac{\frac12\cdot\frac12 s^2}{2\sigma^2}\big) = e^{-\mu^2/8}\).
  2. So \(D_{1/2} = -2\ln e^{-\mu^2/8} = \mu^2/4\). This is Equation 3.15 at \(\alpha = \frac12\).
  3. As a by-product, \(H^2 = 2\big(1 - e^{-\mu^2/8}\big)\).

At \(\mu = 1/\sqrt2\) (the Gaussian pair with the variance of the Laplace pair at \(t = 1\)) this gives \(D_{1/2} = 0.1250\), the entry in Table 3.8.

Rényi divergence of order \(\frac12\) and squared Hellinger distance carry the same information: each is an increasing function of the other.

3.4 Rejection sampling

Exercise 4. Rejection sampling. In the rejection-sampling example, show in general that \(\mathrm{TV}(P, M) \le E_\gamma(P\|Q)\). Hint: write \(\min(p, \gamma q) = p - e\) with \(e = (p - \gamma q)_+\), so \(m = (p - e)/(1 - E_\gamma)\), and use \(e \le p\). (Hockey-stick)

TipSolution

Write \(E = E_\gamma(P\|Q)\) and assume \(E < 1\), so that some proposals are accepted.

  1. The uncovered part. \(\min(p, \gamma q) = p - (p - \gamma q)_+ = p - e\). (Where \(p \le \gamma q\), \(e = 0\) and the min is \(p\); where \(p > \gamma q\), the min is \(\gamma q = p - e\).)
  2. Normalize. \(\sum_xe(x) = E\) by the definition of \(E_\gamma\), so \(\sum_x\min(p, \gamma q) = 1 - E\) and \(m = (p - e)/(1 - E)\).
  3. The difference \(p - m\). \(p - m = \frac{p(1 - E) - p + e}{1 - E} = \frac{e - pE}{1 - E}\).
  4. Use \(e \le p\). Since \(e \le p\) and \(E \ge 0\), \(e - pE \le e - eE = e(1 - E)\). The right side is non-negative, so \((e - pE)_+ \le e(1 - E)\).
  5. Sum, using the form \(\mathrm{TV} = \sum(p - m)_+\) of Equation 3.20: \[\mathrm{TV}(P, M) = \frac{1}{1 - E}\sum_x(e - pE)_+ \le \frac{1}{1 - E}\sum_xe(1 - E) = E. \tag{3.8}\]

On the die. Table 3.3 runs the envelope \(\gamma\cdot\)fair over the range \(1 \le \gamma \le 3\). The bound holds everywhere, and both sides reach 0 at \(\gamma = 3 = \max r\).

The bound is loose by the factor \(1 - E\) lost in step 4: the deficit \(e\) is not all removed from \(m\), because renormalizing gives part of it back.

A low envelope costs at most \(E_\gamma\) in total variation, so \(E_\gamma\) tells you how low an envelope you can afford.

3.5 DPI for \(E_\gamma\) directly

Exercise 5. DPI for \(E_\gamma\) directly. Without the f-divergence machinery, show \(E_\gamma(PK\|QK) \le E_\gamma(P\|Q)\). Hint: \(PK(B) - \gamma QK(B) = \sum_xK(B\mid x)\big(p(x) - \gamma q(x)\big)\) with \(K(B\mid x) \in [0, 1]\); use the randomized version of Fact 3. (Fact 3, DPI)

TipSolution

This is the data-processing inequality of Section 3.10, for \(E_\gamma\) alone.

  1. Output probabilities of an event. For an output event \(B\), \(PK(B) = \sum_{y\in B}\sum_xp(x)K(y\mid x) = \sum_xp(x)K(B\mid x)\), where \(K(B\mid x) = \sum_{y\in B}K(y\mid x)\) is the probability that input \(x\) lands in \(B\). The same holds for \(Q\). Subtract: \[PK(B) - \gamma QK(B) = \sum_xK(B\mid x)\big(p(x) - \gamma q(x)\big). \tag{3.9}\]
  2. This is a randomized test on the input. \(\phi(x) = K(B\mid x)\) takes values in \([0, 1]\). By the second part of Fact 3, \(\sum_x\phi(x)(p(x) - \gamma q(x)) \le \sum_x(p(x) - \gamma q(x))_+ = E_\gamma(P\|Q)\).
  3. Maximize over \(B\). Step 2 holds for every \(B\). By Fact 3 on the output space, \(E_\gamma(PK\|QK) = \max_B[PK(B) - \gamma QK(B)]\), so it is at most \(E_\gamma(P\|Q)\).

On the die, reporting the parity (\(PK = (0.7, 0.3)\), \(QK = (0.5, 0.5)\) for even, odd):

  • \(\gamma = 1\): \(E_1 = (0.7 - 0.5)_+ = 0.2000\) after processing, against TV \(= 0.3333\) before.
  • \(\gamma = 1.2\): \((0.7 - 0.6)_+ = 0.1000\), against \(0.5 - 1.2/6 = 0.3000\).
  • \(\gamma = 2\): \((0.7 - 1)_+ = 0\), against \(0.1667\). The parity bit has likelihood ratios 1.4 and 0.6, so its \(E_\gamma\) is 0 for every \(\gamma \ge 1.4\).

Any processing only lowers the whole \(E_\gamma\) curve, so every statement made through the \(E_\gamma\) curves survives processing.

3.6 Pinsker’s constant is sharp

Exercise 6. Pinsker’s constant is sharp. For \(P = \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\frac12 + \delta)\) and \(Q = \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\frac12)\), show \(\mathrm{kl}(\frac12 + \delta, \frac12)/(2\delta^2) \to 1\) as \(\delta \to 0\). Hint: expand \(\ln(1 \pm 2\delta)\) to second order. (Pinsker)

TipSolution
  1. Write out kl. \(\mathrm{kl}(\frac12 + \delta, \frac12) = (\frac12 + \delta)\ln\frac{1/2 + \delta}{1/2} + (\frac12 - \delta)\ln\frac{1/2 - \delta}{1/2} = (\frac12 + \delta)\ln(1 + 2\delta) + (\frac12 - \delta)\ln(1 - 2\delta)\).
  2. Expand the logarithms. \(\ln(1 + u) = u - \frac{u^2}2 + O(u^3)\), so \(\ln(1 \pm 2\delta) = \pm2\delta - 2\delta^2 + O(\delta^3)\).
  3. Multiply out, keeping terms up to \(\delta^2\): \[(\tfrac12 + \delta)(2\delta - 2\delta^2) = \delta - \delta^2 + 2\delta^2 + O(\delta^3), \qquad (\tfrac12 - \delta)(-2\delta - 2\delta^2) = -\delta - \delta^2 + 2\delta^2 + O(\delta^3). \tag{3.10}\]
  4. Add. The \(\delta\) terms cancel and the \(\delta^2\) terms give \(2\delta^2\): \(\mathrm{kl}(\frac12 + \delta, \frac12) = 2\delta^2 + O(\delta^3)\). Divide by \(2\delta^2\): the ratio is \(1 + O(\delta) \to 1\).

Why this makes the constant sharp. Here \(\mathrm{TV}(P, Q) = \delta\) (one outcome gains \(\delta\), the other loses it). Pinsker says \(\mathrm{KL} \ge 2\,\mathrm{TV}^2 = 2\delta^2\). If some constant \(c > 2\) worked, \(\mathrm{KL} \ge c\,\mathrm{TV}^2\), the ratio \(\mathrm{kl}/(2\delta^2)\) would stay at least \(c/2 > 1\) for all \(\delta\), which contradicts the limit 1.

How fast. kl is even in \(\delta\) (swapping heads and tails maps \(\delta\) to \(-\delta\)), so the \(O(\delta^3)\) term is in fact zero; carrying the expansion one order further gives the ratio \(1 + \frac23\delta^2 + O(\delta^4)\). Table 3.4 shows it.

Near two fair coins, Pinsker’s inequality is an equality to first order, so its constant cannot be improved.

3.7 Samples needed for two coins

Exercise 7. Samples needed for two coins. You flip a coin that is either fair or has heads probability 0.6. Use additivity and Pinsker to find how many flips \(n\) are necessary before the TV between \(n\) flips of the two coins can reach 0.8. Check: \(\mathrm{kl}(0.6, 0.5) = 0.02014\), so \(n \ge 2\times0.8^2/0.02014\), that is \(n \ge 64\). Is this a lower or an upper bound on the flips you need? (Additivity, Pinsker)

TipSolution
  1. One flip. \(\mathrm{kl}(0.6, 0.5) = 0.6\ln\frac{0.6}{0.5} + 0.4\ln\frac{0.4}{0.5} = 0.6\ln1.2 + 0.4\ln0.8 = 0.02014\).
  2. \(n\) flips, by additivity (Equation 3.6): \(\mathrm{KL}_n = 0.02014\,n\).
  3. Pinsker (Equation 3.32): \(\mathrm{TV}_n \le \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}_n/2} = \sqrt{0.02014\,n/2}\).
  4. Require TV to reach 0.8. That needs \(\sqrt{0.02014\,n/2} \ge 0.8\), that is \(n \ge 2\times0.8^2/0.02014 = 63.6\). Since \(n\) is an integer, \(n \ge 64\). The check value is right.

Lower or upper bound? A lower bound. Pinsker caps the TV, so with fewer than 64 flips no test can reach TV 0.8. It does not say 64 flips are enough. The exact TV (computed on the number of heads, which is a sufficient statistic, so by the equality case of the DPI it has the same TV as the whole sequence) is only 0.5805 at \(n = 64\), and first reaches 0.8 at \(n = 162\). Pinsker is off by a factor of about 2.5 here, because it is tight only near TV \(= 0\). Bretagnolle–Huber (Equation 3.33) gives the weaker requirement \(n \ge \ln\frac{1}{1 - 0.64}/0.02014\), that is \(n \ge 51\): the KL that Pinsker needs for TV \(= 0.8\) is \(2\times0.8^2 = 1.28\), below the crossover 1.59 of Section 3.11, so Pinsker is the stronger of the two here.

Pinsker turns “KL is small” into “no test can succeed yet”, which is always a statement about necessary, not sufficient, sample sizes.

3.8 Monotonicity of TV over samples

Exercise 8. Monotonicity of TV over samples. Show that \(\mathrm{TV}(P^n, Q^n)\) is non-decreasing in \(n\). Hint: dropping the last sample is a channel. (DPI)

TipSolution
  1. The channel. Let \(K\) map \((x_1, \dots, x_{n+1})\) to \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\) with probability 1: \(K(y\mid x) = \mathbf 1[y = (x_1, \dots, x_n)]\). It is a deterministic function, so a channel.
  2. It maps \(P^{n+1}\) to \(P^n\). \(P^{n+1}K(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \sum_{x_{n+1}}p(x_1)\cdots p(x_n)\,p(x_{n+1}) = p(x_1)\cdots p(x_n)\), since \(p\) sums to 1. The same for \(Q\).
  3. Apply the DPI. TV is the f-divergence with \(f(t) = \frac12\lvert t - 1\rvert\) (Table 3.11), so Section 3.10 gives \(\mathrm{TV}(P^n, Q^n) = \mathrm{TV}(P^{n+1}K, Q^{n+1}K) \le \mathrm{TV}(P^{n+1}, Q^{n+1})\).

You can also see it directly from the max form of Equation 3.20: any event about the first \(n\) samples is also an event about the first \(n + 1\) (it ignores the last one), so the maximum over the larger family of events is at least as large.
