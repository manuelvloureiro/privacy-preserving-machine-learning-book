22  Exercises: Selection: exponential mechanism, report-noisy-max, sparse vector

Exercises for Selection: exponential mechanism, report-noisy-max, sparse vector, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part03-dp-foundations/solutions_selection.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part03-dp-foundations/solutions_selection.py.

The solutions use the chapter’s notation. A selection problem has \(|\mathcal R|\) candidates with scores \(u(D, r)\) of sensitivity \(\Delta\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}(D) = \max_r u(D, r)\). \(\mathrm{Lap}(b)\) and \(\mathrm{Gumbel}(\kappa)\) are given by their scales, and logarithms are natural. AboveThreshold is the sparse vector algorithm of Definition 22.2.

22.1 The factor 2 is needed

Exercise 1. The factor 2 is needed. Use weights \(e^{\varepsilon u}\) (no 2) with \(\Delta = 1\) on \(|\mathcal R|\) candidates and the neighbours \(u(D) = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\), \(u(D') = (0, 1, \dots, 1)\). Compute the privacy loss of the output “candidate 1” and show that it tends to \(2\varepsilon\) as \(|\mathcal R| \to \infty\). Check: at \(\varepsilon = 1\) it is 1.3106 for \(|\mathcal R| = 3\) and 1.9977 for \(|\mathcal R| = 1000\). (Exponential mechanism, the two factors)

TipSolution
  1. The pair is allowed. Every score moves by exactly 1, so \(\Delta = 1\). The scores are not monotone: candidate 1 goes down while all others go up.
  2. On \(D\). Candidate 1 has weight \(e^\varepsilon\) and the other \(|\mathcal R| - 1\) have weight 1, so \(\Pr[1 \mid D] = \dfrac{e^\varepsilon}{e^\varepsilon + |\mathcal R| - 1}\).
  3. On \(D'\). Candidate 1 has weight 1 and the others \(e^\varepsilon\), so \(\Pr[1 \mid D'] = \dfrac{1}{1 + (|\mathcal R| - 1)e^\varepsilon}\).
  4. The ratio splits into the two factors of step 1 of the proof of Theorem 22.1: \[\frac{\Pr[1 \mid D]}{\Pr[1 \mid D']} = \underbrace{e^\varepsilon}_{\text{numerator}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1 + (|\mathcal R| - 1)e^\varepsilon}{e^\varepsilon + |\mathcal R| - 1}}_{\text{normalizer}}. \tag{22.1}\]
  5. The values. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) the script gives a privacy loss of 1.0000 for \(|\mathcal R| = 2\) (the normalizer factor is exactly 1), 1.3106 for \(|\mathcal R| = 3\), 1.7761 for \(|\mathcal R| = 10\) and 1.9977 for \(|\mathcal R| = 1000\). The closed form and the mechanism’s probabilities agree to four decimals.
  6. The limit. Divide numerator and denominator of the normalizer factor by \(|\mathcal R| - 1\): it tends to \(e^\varepsilon/1 = e^\varepsilon\). The ratio tends to \(e^{2\varepsilon}\), so the loss tends to \(2\varepsilon\).

With the correct weights \(e^{\varepsilon u/2}\) the same calculation gives at most \(e^{\varepsilon/2}\cdot e^{\varepsilon/2} = e^\varepsilon\).

The two factors of the privacy proof are both reached here: candidate 1’s weight drops by \(e^\varepsilon\), and the normalizer, dominated by the \(|\mathcal R| - 1\) competitors, rises by nearly \(e^\varepsilon\). Monotone scores cannot do both at once, which is why only they may drop the 2.

22.2 A private median

Exercise 2. A private median. Records take values in \(\{0, 1, \dots, 100\}\). Use the exponential mechanism over \(r \in \{0, \dots, 100\}\) with score \(u(D, r) = -\big|\#\{i : x_i < r\} - \#\{i : x_i > r\}\big|\). Show that \(\Delta u = 1\) under add/remove, and use Equation 22.4 to show that with probability at least 0.95 at \(\varepsilon = 1\) the output’s score is within 15.22 of the best score. When some candidate splits the data evenly, this means \(\big|\#\{x_i < r\} - \#\{x_i > r\}\big| \le 15.22\). Hint: \(|\mathcal R| = 101\) and \(t = \ln 20\). (Utility theorem)

TipSolution

Sensitivity.

  1. The argument of step 1 of the proof of Proposition 22.2 does not use that the candidates fill an interval: one record changes the count below \(r\) or the count above \(r\) by 1, or neither, so the imbalance moves by at most 1 and \(\Delta u = 1\) on the grid \(\{0, \dots, 100\}\) too.
  2. The script checks this by brute force on the dataset below: adding a record of every possible value and removing each distinct value, the largest change of any score is 1.

Utility.

  1. There are \(|\mathcal R| = 101\) candidates. With \(t = \ln 20\), \(e^{-t} = 0.05\).
  2. Equation 22.4 gives a gap of at most \(\frac{2\Delta}{\varepsilon}(\ln |\mathcal R| + t) = 2(\ln 101 + \ln 20) = 2\ln 2020 = 15.22\) with probability at least 0.95.
  3. If some candidate \(r^*\) has as many records below it as above it, \(\mathrm{OPT} = 0\), and the gap is the selected candidate’s \(|a - c|\) itself.

A concrete dataset. Take \(n = 1001\) records \(x_i = \lfloor 100\,(i/1000)^2\rfloor\) for \(i = 0, \dots, 1000\), skewed toward 0. The median is 25. Values repeat (ten records equal 25), so no candidate splits the data evenly: at \(r = 25\), 500 records are below and 491 above, so \(\mathrm{OPT} = -9\). Table 22.1 lists the candidates near the top.

Only candidates 24 and 25 are within the bound of 15.22. The exact probability of any other output is \(4.54\times10^{-5}\), far below the guaranteed 0.05. The mechanism outputs 25 with probability 0.7310, and its expected distance from 25 is 0.2690.

The utility theorem bounds the error in rank, not in value: with heavily repeated values, the candidates adjacent to the median can be far off in score, and the mechanism almost never picks them.

22.3 The noisy maximum value

Exercise 3. The noisy maximum value. For independent \(G_r \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(\kappa)\), show that \(\max_r(u_r + G_r)\) is Gumbel with scale \(\kappa\) and location \(\kappa\ln\sum_re^{u_r/\kappa}\), so its mean is \(\kappa\big(\ln\sum_re^{u_r/\kappa} + \gamma\big)\) with \(\gamma = 0.5772\) the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Check it on the five scores of Table 22.3 with \(\kappa = 2\). Hint: \(\Pr[\max \le x] = \prod_rF(x - u_r)\). (Gumbel-max)

TipSolution
  1. The maximum is below \(x\) when every term is. The terms are independent, and \(u_r + G_r \le x\) means \(G_r \le x - u_r\). With the Gumbel cumulative distribution function (CDF) \(F(g) = \exp(-e^{-g/\kappa})\), \[\Pr\Big[\max_r(u_r + G_r) \le x\Big] = \prod_r\exp\big(-e^{-(x - u_r)/\kappa}\big) = \exp\Big(-e^{-x/\kappa}\sum_re^{u_r/\kappa}\Big). \tag{22.2}\]
  2. Recognize a shifted Gumbel. Write \(\sum_re^{u_r/\kappa} = e^{\ell/\kappa}\), that is \(\ell = \kappa\ln\sum_re^{u_r/\kappa}\). The CDF is \(\exp(-e^{-(x - \ell)/\kappa})\): a Gumbel with scale \(\kappa\) and location \(\ell\).
  3. The mean of a Gumbel. For \(G \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(1)\), substitute \(w = e^{-g}\): \(\mathbb E[G] = \int_0^\infty(-\ln w)\,e^{-w}\,\mathrm dw = -\Gamma'(1) = \gamma\). Scaling and shifting give \(\mathbb E[\max_r(u_r + G_r)] = \ell + \kappa\gamma\).
  4. Check. For scores \((4, 3, 3, 1, 0)\) and \(\kappa = 2\): \(\ell = 2\ln(e^2 + 2e^{1.5} + e^{0.5} + 1) = 5.8890\), and the mean is \(5.8890 + 2\times0.5772 = 7.0434\). A million simulated draws give 7.0430.

The location \(\ell\) is the “soft maximum” of the scores: it lies between \(\max_ru_r = 4\) and \(\max_ru_r + \kappa\ln |\mathcal R| = 4 + 2\ln5 = 7.2189\).

The noisy maximum is a smooth function of all the scores, not of the winner’s alone. Releasing it is a separate numeric query, which needs its own budget; report-noisy-max releases only the index.

22.4 Splitting the budget of AboveThreshold

Exercise 4. Splitting the budget of AboveThreshold. With \(\rho \sim \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon_1)\) and \(\nu_i \sim \mathrm{Lap}(2\Delta/\varepsilon_2)\), redo the accuracy derivation to get \(\alpha = \max\big(2\Delta\ln(2/\beta)/\varepsilon_1,\ 4\Delta\ln(2k/\beta)/\varepsilon_2\big)\), and minimize it over \(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 = \varepsilon\). Check: for \(k = 1000\), \(\beta = 0.05\), \(\varepsilon = 1\), \(\Delta = 1\), the equal split gives 84.77 and the best split gives 49.76, with \(\varepsilon_1 = 0.148\). (Laplace tails, union bound)

TipSolution
  1. Threshold noise. \(\Pr[|\rho| \ge \alpha/2] = e^{-(\alpha/2)\varepsilon_1/\Delta}\). This is at most \(\beta/2\) when \(\alpha \ge 2\Delta\ln(2/\beta)/\varepsilon_1\).
  2. Query noise. By the union bound, \(\Pr[\max_i|\nu_i| \ge \alpha/2] \le k\,e^{-(\alpha/2)\varepsilon_2/(2\Delta)}\). This is at most \(\beta/2\) when \(\alpha \ge 4\Delta\ln(2k/\beta)/\varepsilon_2\).
  3. Both. On the event that both noises are below \(\alpha/2\), which has probability at least \(1 - \beta\), the answers are \(\alpha\)-accurate by step 8 of the proof of Theorem 22.6. So \(\alpha\) is the larger of the two requirements.
  4. Minimize. Write \(a = 2\Delta\ln(2/\beta)\) and \(c = 4\Delta\ln(2k/\beta)\). As \(\varepsilon_1\) grows, \(a/\varepsilon_1\) falls and \(c/(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_1)\) rises, so the maximum is smallest where they are equal: \(a/\varepsilon_1 = c/\varepsilon_2 = \alpha^*\). Then \(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 = (a + c)/\alpha^* = \varepsilon\), so \[\alpha^* = \frac{a + c}{\varepsilon} = \frac{2\Delta\ln(2/\beta) + 4\Delta\ln(2k/\beta)}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \varepsilon_1 = \frac{a}{a + c}\,\varepsilon. \tag{22.3}\]
  5. Numbers. With \(k = 1000\), \(\beta = 0.05\): \(a = 2\ln40 = 7.38\) and \(c = 4\ln40000 = 42.39\). The equal split gives \(\max(14.76, 84.77) = 84.77\), which is Dwork & Roth’s \(8(\ln k + \ln(2/\beta))\). The best split gives \(\alpha^* = 49.76\) with \(\varepsilon_1 = 0.148\) and \(\varepsilon_2 = 0.852\).

The threshold noise is drawn once and the query noise \(k\) times, so the union bound makes the query noise the bottleneck. Giving it 85% of the budget improves the accuracy by 41% at the same privacy.

22.5 No query noise

Exercise 5. No query noise. In the two-query counterexample for Algorithm 5 of Table 22.4, with \(\tau = 0\) and \(\rho \sim \mathrm{Lap}(2)\), compute \(\Pr[\bot\top \mid D]\) and \(\Pr[\bot\top \mid D']\). Check: 0.1967 and 0. (Reading a proof for the step that fails)

TipSolution
  1. On \(D\), \(f = (0, 1)\). The first query says \(\bot\) when \(0 < \rho\), and the second says \(\top\) when \(1 \ge \rho\). So \(\Pr[\bot\top \mid D] = \Pr[0 < \rho \le 1]\).
  2. Laplace CDF. \(\Pr[\rho \le 1] = 1 - \frac12e^{-1/2}\) and \(\Pr[\rho \le 0] = \frac12\), so the probability is \(\frac12(1 - e^{-1/2}) = 0.1967\). A million simulated runs give 0.1968.
  3. On \(D'\), \(f = (1, 0)\). Now \(\bot\) needs \(1 < \rho\) and \(\top\) needs \(0 \ge \rho\). No value of \(\rho\) does both, so the probability is 0. A million simulated runs never produced \(\bot\top\).
  4. Which step fails. Step 3 of the proof of Theorem 22.6 shifts the query noise \(\nu_j\) to keep the \(\top\) consistent after \(\rho\) has been shifted for the \(\bot\)s. Without \(\nu_k\) there is nothing to shift, and the two conditions can become incompatible.

A probability that is positive on one dataset and zero on its neighbour has infinite privacy loss: one observation of \(\bot\top\) proves the data are \(D\), not \(D'\).

22.6 Two candidates

Exercise 6. Two candidates. For two candidates whose scores differ by \(d\), compute the probability that the better one is selected by the exponential mechanism with weights \(e^{u/b}\) and by Laplace report-noisy-max with scale \(b\). Hint: the difference of two independent \(\mathrm{Lap}(b)\) variables has \(\Pr[Z_1 - Z_2 > x] = \frac12e^{-x/b}(1 + \frac{x}{2b})\) for \(x \ge 0\). Check: at \(d = b\), 0.7311 against 0.7241. Compare with the 50-model example, where Laplace noise did better. (Gumbel-max, Laplace differences)

TipSolution
  1. Exponential mechanism. \(\Pr[\text{better}] = \dfrac{e^{d/b}}{e^{d/b} + 1} = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-d/b}}\), the logistic function of \(d/b\).
  2. The difference of two Laplace variables. \(Z_2\) is symmetric, so \(Z_1 - Z_2\) has the law of \(Z_1 + Z_2\), whose density is the convolution \(\int\frac{1}{4b^2}e^{-|y|/b - |x - y|/b}\,\mathrm dy\). For \(x \ge 0\), split at \(y = 0\) and \(y = x\): the pieces \(y < 0\) and \(y > x\) each give \(\frac b2e^{-x/b}\), and \(0 \le y \le x\) gives \(xe^{-x/b}\). So the density is \(\frac{1}{4b}\big(1 + \frac{|x|}{b}\big)e^{-|x|/b}\).
  3. Its tail. \(\int_x^\infty\big(1 + \frac yb\big)e^{-y/b}\,\mathrm dy = (2b + x)\,e^{-x/b}\), so \(\Pr[Z_1 - Z_2 > x] = \frac12e^{-x/b}\big(1 + \frac{x}{2b}\big)\).
  4. Laplace report-noisy-max. The better candidate loses when \(Z_2 - Z_1 > d\), so \(\Pr[\text{better}] = 1 - \frac12e^{-d/b}\big(1 + \frac{d}{2b}\big)\).
  5. Numbers. Table 22.2 compares the two.

A million simulated runs at \(d = b\) give 0.7308 for the Gumbel noisy max and 0.7239 for the Laplace one.

  1. Against the 50 models. With two candidates the exponential mechanism is better at every gap in the table. On the 50 models of the chapter, with the same scale \(b = 2\) at \(\varepsilon = 1\), Laplace noise picks the best model with probability 0.4241 against 0.3935 and has the smaller expected gap, 1.40 against 1.54.

Neither private argmax dominates the other: which one selects better depends on how many competitors are close to the top. Both have the same privacy guarantee, so the choice is about accuracy on the score profiles you expect.

22.7 The Laplace mechanism as an exponential mechanism

Exercise 7. The Laplace mechanism as an exponential mechanism. Take a query \(f\) with sensitivity \(\Delta = 1\), candidates on the whole real line, and the score \(u(D, h) = -|f(D) - h|\) (Proposition 22.1). Show that the exponential mechanism with the generic weights \(e^{\varepsilon u/2}\) adds \(\mathrm{Lap}(2/\varepsilon)\) noise, that its normalizer does not depend on \(f(D)\), and that its privacy loss is therefore only \(\varepsilon/2\). Then drop the 2 and check that the result is the Laplace mechanism at \(\varepsilon\). Check: at \(\varepsilon = 1\), with \(f(D) = 0\) and \(f(D') = 1\), both normalizers are 4.0000 with the 2 and 2.0000 without; the largest log ratio of the densities is 0.5000 and 1.0000; the root-mean-square errors are 2.8284 and 1.4142. (Exponential mechanism, the normalizer factor)

TipSolution
  1. Without the 2. Proposition 22.1 covers this case: the normalizer is \(2/\varepsilon\) whatever \(f(D)\) is, and the mechanism is the Laplace mechanism at \(\varepsilon\). At \(\varepsilon = 1\) the script’s numerical integrals give 2.0000 for both datasets and a largest log ratio of 1.0000.
  2. With the 2. The weights \(e^{\varepsilon u/2}\) are the weights of Proposition 22.1 at budget \(\varepsilon/2\), so the density is the \(\mathrm{Lap}(2/\varepsilon)\) density centred at \(f(D)\), and step 3 of that proof with \(\varepsilon/2\) in place of \(\varepsilon\) gives the normalizer \(4/\varepsilon\), again independent of the data. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) the script finds 4.0000 for both datasets.
  3. The privacy loss with the 2. The normalizer factor is exactly 1, so only the candidate’s own factor is left, \(e^{\varepsilon(u(D, h) - u(D', h))/2} \le e^{\varepsilon/2}\). The largest log ratio of the two densities, over all \(h\), is 0.5000: the generic recipe spends only half its budget.
  4. What the 2 wastes. Both mechanisms meet the budget \(\varepsilon\), but the generic one has privacy loss at most \(\varepsilon/2\), so it leaves half the budget unused.
  5. The cost of the 2. A \(\mathrm{Lap}(b)\) variable has root-mean-square size \(\sqrt2\,b\): 2.8284 with the 2 and 1.4142 without.

The factor 2 pays for a normalizer that can move against the candidate. When the scores are a shifted copy of one another, as here, the normalizer cannot move, and the generic recipe adds twice the noise it needs.

22.8 Records for a private learner

Exercise 8. Records for a private learner. For the private learner of Equation 22.7, find the number of records each term needs for an excess error of \(\alpha = 0.05\) at \(\beta = 0.05\), for the class of 1,001 thresholds and for a class of \(10^6\). Then find the \(\varepsilon\) below which the privacy term needs more records than the sampling term. Check: 33,913 and 793 records at \(\varepsilon = 1\) for 1,001 thresholds; 56,015 and 1,345 for \(10^6\); privacy dominates below \(\varepsilon = 0.0234\). (Utility of the exponential mechanism, uniform convergence)

TipSolution
  1. The two requirements. Ask each excess term of Equation 22.7 to be at most \(\alpha/2\). The sampling term needs \(2\sqrt{\ln(2|\mathcal H|/\beta)/(2n)} \le \alpha/2\); squaring gives \(\ln(2|\mathcal H|/\beta)/(2n) \le \alpha^2/16\), that is \(n \ge 8\ln(2|\mathcal H|/\beta)/\alpha^2\). The privacy term needs \(2\ln(|\mathcal H|/\beta)/(\varepsilon n) \le \alpha/2\), that is \(n \ge 4\ln(|\mathcal H|/\beta)/(\alpha\varepsilon)\).
  2. 1,001 thresholds. \(\ln(2 \times 1001/0.05) = \ln 40{,}040 = 10.598\), so the sampling term asks for \(8 \times 10.598/0.0025 = 33{,}912.4\), that is 33,913 records. \(\ln(1001/0.05) = 9.905\), so the privacy term asks for \(4 \times 9.905/0.05 = 792.4\), that is 793 records at \(\varepsilon = 1\).
  3. \(10^6\) classifiers. The logarithms become 17.50 and 16.81, and the requirements 56,015 and 1,345. A thousandfold larger class costs 1.65 times more records for sampling and 1.70 times more for privacy.
  4. When privacy dominates. The privacy requirement exceeds the sampling one when \(4\ln(|\mathcal H|/\beta)/(\alpha\varepsilon) > 8\ln(2|\mathcal H|/\beta)/\alpha^2\), that is when \(\varepsilon < \alpha\ln(|\mathcal H|/\beta)/(2\ln(2|\mathcal H|/\beta))\). For 1,001 thresholds this is \(0.05 \times 9.905/(2 \times 10.598) = 0.0234\). At \(\varepsilon = 0.01\) the privacy term needs 79,236 records, more than the 33,913 for sampling.

Since the two logarithms are close, privacy dominates roughly when \(\varepsilon < \alpha/2\). At the accuracies machine learning cares about, a few percent, that means strong privacy, \(\varepsilon\) around 0.01; at \(\varepsilon = 1\) privacy is a small surcharge on the records you need anyway.

22.9 Sparse with basic and advanced composition

Exercise 9. Sparse with basic and advanced composition. For \(k = 1000\) queries, \(\beta = 0.05\), \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\delta = 10^{-6}\), compare the margin of Sparse with basic composition (Equation 22.17) and with advanced composition, as the number \(c\) of answers above grows. Find the smallest \(c\) at which advanced composition gives the smaller margin, and explain where it comes from. Check: advanced composition wins from \(c = 29\) (margins 3,240 against 3,218), and at \(c = 1000\) the margins are 140,035 and 23,691. (Composition of AboveThreshold runs)

TipSolution
  1. Both margins. Each run gets failure probability \(\beta/c\), so both margins are \(8(\ln k + \ln(2c/\beta))/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{run}}\). They differ only in the budget of a run: \(\varepsilon/c\) with basic composition, and the largest \(\varepsilon_0\) whose \(c\)-fold advanced composition is at most \(\varepsilon\) with advanced composition.
  2. The crossover. The script compares the two for \(c = 1, 2, \dots\) and finds that advanced composition first wins at \(c = 29\): the margins are 3,240 (basic) and 3,218 (advanced). At \(c = 28\) they are 3,120 and 3,155.
  3. Why there. For small \(\varepsilon_0\), advanced composition spends about \(\varepsilon_0\sqrt{2c\ln(1/\delta)}\), so \(\varepsilon_0 \approx \varepsilon/\sqrt{2c\ln(1/\delta)}\). That beats \(\varepsilon/c\) once \(c > 2\ln(1/\delta) = 27.6\), and the drift term \(c\varepsilon_0\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2)\) pushes the crossover to 29.
  4. Large \(c\). At \(c = 1000\) the margins are 140,035 and 23,691: a factor of 5.9, close to \(\sqrt{c/(2\ln(1/\delta))} = \sqrt{1000/27.6} = 6.0\).

This is the same crossover as for advanced composition in Advanced and optimal composition: composing \(c\) runs pays \(\sqrt c\) instead of \(c\), but only once \(c\) is large compared with \(2\ln(1/\delta)\).

22.10 Many more candidates

Exercise 10. Many more candidates. In the 50-model example at \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\Delta = 1\), the tail bound of Theorem 22.2 with \(t = 3\) says the gap exceeds 13.82 with probability at most \(e^{-3}\). By how much does that gap grow if there are \(10^6\) candidates instead of 50, and what does that say about the cost of candidates? Check: 33.63. (Utility of the exponential mechanism)

TipSolution
  1. The gap at 50 candidates. Equation 22.4 with \(\Delta = 1\), \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(t = 3\) gives \(2(\ln 50 + 3) = 2(3.912 + 3) = 13.82\).
  2. The gap at \(10^6\) candidates. Only \(\ln|\mathcal R|\) changes: \(\ln 10^6 = 13.816\), so the gap is \(2(13.816 + 3) = 33.63\), which is what the chapter’s script prints.
  3. The comparison. The number of candidates grows by a factor of \(10^6/50 = 20{,}000\), and the gap by a factor of \(33.63/13.82 = 2.4\), because it depends on the number of candidates only through its logarithm.
  4. Why the bound does not need few bad candidates. Step 3 of the proof bounds each bad candidate’s probability by \(e^{-\varepsilon a/(2\Delta)}\) and adds at most \(|\mathcal R|\) of them, which is where \(\ln|\mathcal R|\) comes from.

A factor of 20,000 in candidates costs a factor of 2.4 in the gap: selection pays for its candidates in accuracy, logarithmically, and never in privacy.
