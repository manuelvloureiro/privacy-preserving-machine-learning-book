21  Exercises: Advanced and optimal composition

Exercises for Advanced and optimal composition, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part03-dp-foundations/solutions_composition.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part03-dp-foundations/solutions_composition.py.

The solutions use the chapter’s notation and its abbreviation for differential privacy (DP). \(P = M(D)\) and \(Q = M(D')\), \(L = \ln(p/q)\) is the privacy loss with \(Y \sim P\), \(\delta(\varepsilon) = \mathbb E[(1 - e^{\varepsilon - L})_+]\), and randomized response at level \(\varepsilon\) is truthful with probability \(t = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\).

21.1 The sharp mean loss

Exercise 1. The sharp mean loss. Show that \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2) \le \varepsilon^2/2\) and \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2) \le \varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)\) for every \(\varepsilon \ge 0\), and that the ratio of the two mean bounds tends to \(\frac12\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). Check: at \(\varepsilon = 1\) the three values are 0.4621, 0.5 and 1.7183. (Lemma 21.3, Theorem 21.3)

TipSolution
  1. \(\tanh u \le u\) for \(u \ge 0\). Both sides are 0 at \(u = 0\), and the derivative of \(\tanh\) is \(1 - \tanh^2 u \le 1\), the derivative of \(u\). So \(\tanh u\) never catches up. With \(u = \varepsilon/2\): \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2) \le \varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon^2/2\).
  2. Against \(\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)\). Step 3 of the proof of Proposition 21.1 shows that \(\tanh(\varepsilon/2) = \frac{e^\varepsilon - 1}{e^\varepsilon + 1}\). The denominator \(e^\varepsilon + 1\) is at least 2, so \(\tanh(\varepsilon/2) \le \frac12(e^\varepsilon - 1) \le e^\varepsilon - 1\).
  3. The ratio. By step 2, \(\frac{\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)}{\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1)} = \frac{1}{e^\varepsilon + 1}\), which tends to \(\frac12\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) and decreases as \(\varepsilon\) grows.
  4. Check. Table 21.1 lists the three values.

For small \(\varepsilon\) the sharp bound and \(\varepsilon^2/2\) agree, and the Dwork–Rothblum–Vadhan bound is twice as large. For large \(\varepsilon\) the gap explodes, which is why the \(\tanh\) form of advanced composition matters most when \(k\varepsilon^2\) is large.

21.2 When does advanced composition pay?

Exercise 2. When does advanced composition pay? Show that Equation 21.9 is below \(k\varepsilon\) exactly when \(k > 2\ln(1/\delta')/(2 - e^\varepsilon)^2\), for \(\varepsilon < \ln 2\). Check: for \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and \(\delta' = 10^{-5}\) the threshold is 28.8, so advanced composition wins from \(k = 29\). (Theorem 21.1)

TipSolution
  1. Write the inequality. \(\varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1) < k\varepsilon\).
  2. Divide by \(\varepsilon > 0\) and collect the \(k\) terms: \(\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} < k(2 - e^\varepsilon)\).
  3. Sign. For \(\varepsilon < \ln 2\), \(2 - e^\varepsilon > 0\), so both sides are positive and squaring keeps the inequality: \(2k\ln(1/\delta') < k^2(2 - e^\varepsilon)^2\).
  4. Divide by \(k(2 - e^\varepsilon)^2 > 0\): \(k > 2\ln(1/\delta')/(2 - e^\varepsilon)^2\). (For \(\varepsilon \ge \ln 2\) the right side of step 2 is not positive, and advanced composition never wins.)
  5. Check. \(2\ln 10^5 = 23.03\) and \((2 - e^{0.1})^2 = 0.8948^2 = 0.8007\), so the threshold is 28.8. The script confirms: advanced composition gives 2.8336 at \(k = 28\) (above \(2.8\)) and 2.8891 at \(k = 29\) (below \(2.9\)).

With the \(\tanh\) mean term, the same steps give \(k > 2\ln(1/\delta')/(1 - \tanh(\varepsilon/2))^2\), which is 25.5 at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), so that form wins from \(k = 26\). At \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) the original form needs \(k > 186.6\).

Advanced composition is a large-\(k\) tool: below about \(2\ln(1/\delta')\) steps, the \(\delta'\) it spends buys nothing.

21.3 Two rounds by hand

Exercise 3. Two rounds by hand. From Table 21.1, compute \(\delta(\varepsilon')\) for two rounds of randomized response at \(\varepsilon = \ln 3\), for all \(\varepsilon' \in [0, 2\ln 3]\). Check: \(\delta(0) = \frac12\), \(\delta(\ln 3) = \frac38\), \(\delta(2\ln 3) = 0\). Compare with what basic composition says. (Lemma 21.1, Theorem 21.4)

TipSolution
  1. Which outputs count. By Lemma 21.1, \(\delta(\varepsilon') = \sum_y p(y)(1 - e^{\varepsilon' - L(y)})_+\), and only outputs with \(L(y) > \varepsilon'\) contribute. For \(\varepsilon' \ge 0\), the outputs with \(L = 0\) and \(L = -2\ln 3\) never count. Only \((0, 0)\), with \(p = \frac9{16}\) and \(L = 2\ln 3\), counts, and only while \(\varepsilon' < 2\ln 3\).
  2. The curve. \(\delta(\varepsilon') = \frac9{16}\big(1 - e^{\varepsilon'}/9\big) = \frac{9 - e^{\varepsilon'}}{16}\) for \(0 \le \varepsilon' \le 2\ln 3\), and 0 beyond.
  3. Check. \(\varepsilon' = 0\): \(\frac{8}{16} = \frac12\). \(\varepsilon' = \ln 3\): \(\frac{6}{16} = \frac38\). \(\varepsilon' = 2\ln 3\): \(0\). Enumeration of the four outputs and Equation 21.15 give the same three values.
  4. Compared with basic composition. Basic composition says \((2\ln 3, 0)\), the end point of this curve, and is exact there. For two rounds there is nothing to gain at \(\delta = 0\). What the curve adds is every other point: for example, the pair is \((\ln 3, \frac38)\)-DP. A \(\delta\) of \(\frac38\) is useless for privacy, which is the point: with \(k = 2\) the loss has not had the chance to concentrate.

Composition gains come from many steps. With two steps the worst output has probability \(\frac9{16}\), and you cannot trade it for a small \(\delta\).

21.4 The per-step δ

Exercise 4. The per-step \(\delta\). Prove the identity \(1 - (1 - \delta)^k \le k\delta\) used for advanced composition with \(\delta > 0\), and show that Equation 21.15 is non-trivial only for a target \(\delta_{\mathrm{total}} > 1 - (1 - \delta)^k\). Check: with \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), \(\delta = 5\times10^{-8}\), \(k = 100\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{total}} = 10^{-5}\), the optimal \(\varepsilon'\) is 4.4469 and advanced composition gives 5.9926. (Theorem 21.4, Corollary 21.1)

TipSolution
  1. The inequality is a union bound. \(1 - (1 - \delta)^k\) is the probability that at least one of \(k\) independent events of probability \(\delta\) happens: some step shows its flag. The union bound gives at most \(k\delta\). (Or by induction: \((1 - \delta)^{k} \ge (1 - \delta)^{k-1} - \delta \ge 1 - (k - 1)\delta - \delta\).)
  2. The floor of Equation 21.15. \(\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon') \ge 0\), so the right side of Equation 21.15 is at least \(1 - (1 - \delta)^k\) for every \(\varepsilon'\). This floor is attained by the composed four-point pair: the flags have infinite loss. So no finite \(\varepsilon'\) achieves a smaller total \(\delta\), and the script returns \(\varepsilon' = \infty\) for a target of \(4\times10^{-6}\).
  3. Above the floor. For a target \(\delta_{\mathrm{total}} > 1 - (1 - \delta)^k\), solve \(\delta_{\mathrm{RR}}(\varepsilon') = \frac{\delta_{\mathrm{total}} - (1 - (1 - \delta)^k)}{(1 - \delta)^k}\), a pure randomized-response problem.
  4. Check. \(1 - (1 - 5\times10^{-8})^{100} = 4.999988\times10^{-6}\), just below \(k\delta = 5\times10^{-6}\). That leaves about \(5\times10^{-6}\) for the randomized-response part, and the optimal \(\varepsilon'\) is 4.4469. Advanced composition with \(\delta' = 10^{-5} - 100 \times 5\times10^{-8} = 5\times10^{-6}\) gives 5.9926, and its \(\tanh\) form 5.4404.

The per-step \(\delta\) is spent outright, step by step; only what is left can be traded against \(\varepsilon'\).

21.5 Which pairs factor through randomized response?

Exercise 5. Which pairs factor through randomized response? For \(P = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)\) and \(Q = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)\), find the smallest \(\varepsilon\) for which Equation 21.12 is a valid channel, and write the channel. Hint: a row of \(K\) goes negative exactly where the pair violates \(\varepsilon\)-DP. Check: \(\varepsilon = \ln 2.5 = 0.9163\); the channel rows are then \((0.7, 0.3, 0)\) and \((0, 0.3, 0.7)\). (Theorem 21.2)

TipSolution
  1. Positivity. \(K(y \mid 0) \ge 0\) iff \(e^\varepsilon p(y) \ge q(y)\), and \(K(y \mid 1) \ge 0\) iff \(e^\varepsilon q(y) \ge p(y)\). Together: \(e^\varepsilon \ge \max_y\max(p/q, q/p)\), that is \(\varepsilon \ge \max_y|L(y)|\).
  2. The ratios. \(p/q = 2.5, 1, 0.4\) on the three outputs. The largest \(|\ln(p/q)|\) is \(\ln 2.5 = 0.9163\). So the smallest valid \(\varepsilon\) is \(\ln 2.5\), the pair’s pure-DP level.
  3. The channel. With \(e^\varepsilon = 2.5\), \(K(\cdot \mid 0) = (2.5P - Q)/1.5 = (1.05, 0.45, 0)/1.5 = (0.7, 0.3, 0)\) and \(K(\cdot \mid 1) = (2.5Q - P)/1.5 = (0, 0.3, 0.7)\).
  4. Check. \(t = 2.5/3.5 = 0.7143\), and \(t(0.7, 0.3, 0) + (1 - t)(0, 0.3, 0.7) = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) = P\). Below \(\ln 2.5\) the construction fails: at \(\varepsilon = 0.8\), \(K(\cdot \mid 0) = (0.7448, 0.3, -0.0448)\) has a negative entry, at the third output, where \(q/p = 2.5 > e^{0.8}\).

At the tight level each row of the channel puts zero mass on one output: randomized response’s “report 0” can never produce the output that most favours \(D'\).

21.6 Composing Gaussians exactly

Exercise 6. Composing Gaussians exactly. Show that \(k\) Gaussian mechanisms with noise \(\sigma\) on sensitivity-1 queries, composed non-adaptively, have the same \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) as one Gaussian pair with shift \(\sqrt k/\sigma\). Hint: the privacy loss of each step is normal; use Part 1’s Gaussian pair. Then explain the gap in Figure 21.4 by comparing the mean loss \(k/(2\sigma^2)\) with \(k\varepsilon_0\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2)\) for the per-step \(\varepsilon_0\) at \(\delta_0 = 5\times10^{-9}\). Check: for \(\sigma = 20\) and \(k = 1{,}000\), the exact \(\varepsilon\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) is 7.511, the true mean loss is 1.25, and \(k\varepsilon_0\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2) = 29.93\). (Theorem 21.4, Gaussian pair)

TipSolution
  1. One step. The worst pair of a sensitivity-1 Gaussian mechanism is \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) against \(\mathcal N(1, \sigma^2)\). With \(\mu = 1/\sigma\), Part 1 (Divergences, the Gaussian pair) shows that its privacy loss under \(P\) is normal with mean \(\mu^2/2\) and variance \(\mu^2\).
  2. \(k\) steps. By Lemma 21.2, the total loss is the sum of \(k\) independent such losses: normal with mean \(k\mu^2/2\) and variance \(k\mu^2\).
  3. One pair with shift \(\mu_k = \sqrt k\mu\) has loss with mean \(\mu_k^2/2 = k\mu^2/2\) and variance \(\mu_k^2 = k\mu^2\): the same distribution.
  4. Same loss, same curve. By Lemma 21.1, \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) depends on the pair only through the distribution of \(L\) under \(P\). So the two curves agree. (The same holds in the other direction by symmetry.)
  5. Check. \(\mu_k = \sqrt{1000}/20 = 1.5811\), and solving the Gaussian \(\delta(\varepsilon) = 10^{-5}\) by bisection gives \(\varepsilon = 7.511\).
  6. The gap. The true mean loss of the run is \(k\mu^2/2 = 1000/800 = 1.25\). The per-step point at \(\delta_0 = 5\times10^{-9}\) is \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.2453\), and optimal composition must assume the worst mechanism with that \(\varepsilon_0\), randomized response, whose mean loss per step is \(\varepsilon_0\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2)\). Over \(k\) steps that is \(29.93\), 24 times the truth. The fluctuation term \(\varepsilon_0\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\) with the remaining \(\delta' = 5\times10^{-6}\) is 38.32 on top.
  7. Why. A Gaussian step’s loss is rarely as large as \(\varepsilon_0\): \(\varepsilon_0\) was chosen so that it is exceeded only with probability about \(\delta_0\). Randomized response with the same \(\varepsilon_0\) has loss \(\pm\varepsilon_0\) on every output. Replacing each Gaussian step by its worst-case \((\varepsilon_0, \delta_0)\) stand-in inflates both the mean and the spread of the total.

The \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) summary of one step is a worst case over a whole class, and composing worst cases compounds the loss. Composing the actual loss distributions avoids it.

21.7 Two different steps

Exercise 7. Two different steps. Compose randomized response at \(\varepsilon_1 = \ln 3\) with randomized response at \(\varepsilon_2 = \ln 2\). List the four outputs with their probabilities and losses, and compute \(\delta(\varepsilon')\) at \(\varepsilon' = 0\), \(\ln\frac32\) and \(\ln 2\). Check: \(\frac12\), \(\frac38\) and \(\frac13\). (Lemma 21.1, Lemma 21.2)

TipSolution
  1. The two steps. The first is truthful with probability \(\frac34\), the second with \(\frac23\).
  2. The four outputs (Table 21.2). On \(D\) the probabilities multiply. On \(D'\) “truthful” and “flipped” swap roles. By Lemma 21.2 the loss adds: \(\pm\ln 3 \pm \ln 2\).
  1. \(\delta(0)\). The outputs with \(e^L > 1\) count: \(\frac12(1 - \frac16) + \frac14(1 - \frac23) = \frac5{12} + \frac1{12} = \frac12\).
  2. \(\delta(\ln\frac32)\). Only \(e^L = 6\) counts: \(\frac12(1 - \frac{3/2}{6}) = \frac12\cdot\frac34 = \frac38\).
  3. \(\delta(\ln 2)\). Again only \(e^L = 6\): \(\frac12(1 - \frac26) = \frac13\). And \(\delta(\ln 6) = 0\), the basic composition point \((\ln 3 + \ln 2, 0)\).

With different \(\varepsilon_i\) the loss takes \(2^k\) distinct values in general, not \(k + 1\). For two steps you can list them; for many, the exact computation is #P-complete, as Murtagh & Vadhan showed.

21.8 Laplace noise in many dimensions

Exercise 8. Laplace noise in many dimensions. One record can move each of \(d\) counts by up to 1. Release each count with its own Laplace noise of scale \(1/\varepsilon_0\) and compose the \(d\) steps with the \(\tanh\) form of advanced composition (Equation 21.17) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). Show that \(\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon/(2\sqrt{2d\ln(1/\delta)})\) keeps the total at most \(\varepsilon\) when \(\varepsilon \le 8\ln(1/\delta)\), so the error per count grows like \(\sqrt d\). At \(\varepsilon = 1\), compare this closed form with the largest \(\varepsilon_0\) the formula allows. Check: at \(d = 1000\) the closed form gives \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.00330\), which spends 0.5054 of the budget, and a root-mean-square error of 429.2; the largest \(\varepsilon_0\) is 0.00645, with error 219.2. (Corollary 21.1, budgeting)

TipSolution
  1. The first term. With \(\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon/(2\sqrt{2d\ln(1/\delta)})\), the term \(\varepsilon_0\sqrt{2d\ln(1/\delta)}\) is exactly \(\varepsilon/2\).
  2. The drift term. Since \(\tanh y \le y\), the term \(d\varepsilon_0\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2)\) is at most \(d\varepsilon_0^2/2 = d\varepsilon^2/(16d\ln(1/\delta)) = \varepsilon^2/(16\ln(1/\delta))\). This is at most \(\varepsilon/2\) when \(\varepsilon \le 8\ln(1/\delta)\), which at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) allows any \(\varepsilon\) up to 92.
  3. The error. A \(\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon_0)\) count has root-mean-square error \(\sqrt2/\varepsilon_0 = 2\sqrt2\sqrt{2d\ln(1/\delta)}/\varepsilon\), which grows like \(\sqrt d\).
  4. Numbers. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(d = 1000\), \(\varepsilon_0 = 1/(2\sqrt{2000\ln 10^5}) = 0.00330\), and advanced composition charges \(0.5054\) for the \(d\) steps: the closed form wastes about half the budget, because its drift term is tiny. Its error is 429.2. The largest \(\varepsilon_0\) that keeps the total at 1, found by bisection, is 0.00645, with error 219.2, the value of Table 21.2. At \(d = 100\) and \(10{,}000\) the closed form gives 135.7 and 1,357.2, against 69.3 and 693.0.

A closed-form budget is easy to state and safe, but it can leave half the budget unused. When the per-step mechanism is fixed, solve for the largest step budget, or better, compute the exact composition.
