30  Exercises: f-DP and Gaussian differential privacy

Exercises for the chapter on differential privacy (DP) as a trade-off curve, f-DP and Gaussian differential privacy, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part04-privacy-accounting/solutions_gaussian_dp.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part04-privacy-accounting/solutions_gaussian_dp.py.

The solutions use the chapter’s notation. \(\mu\)-Gaussian differential privacy (GDP) means the trade-off curve \(G_\mu(a) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(1 - a) - \mu)\). A test has type I error \(a\) and type II error \(b\), and \(\alpha\) is reserved for Rényi orders. \(P = M(D)\) and \(Q = M(D')\), the privacy loss is \(L = \ln(p/q)\), and Dong, Roth & Su (2022) is cited as Dong et al. Three more acronyms appear: the total variation (TV) distance, the central limit theorem (CLT) for composition, and differentially private stochastic gradient descent (SGD), written DP-SGD.

30.1 The Laplace mechanism is GDP

Exercise 1. The Laplace mechanism is GDP. Find the smallest \(\mu\) such that the Laplace mechanism with \(\varepsilon = 1\) is \(\mu\)-GDP, that is \(G_\mu \le T(\mathrm{Lap}(0, 1), \mathrm{Lap}(1, 1))\) everywhere. Check numerically that the binding point is on the diagonal, derive the closed form this gives, and prove that it suffices. What \(\varepsilon\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) does this GDP summary report, and what is the truth? Check: \(\mu = 1.0301\) (the same as Part 1’s Gaussian with equal TV); the GDP summary gives \(\varepsilon = 4.5301\), while the mechanism is \((1, 0)\)-DP. (Ordering trade-off curves, symmetry)

TipSolution

Write \(T_\varepsilon = T(\mathrm{Lap}(0, 1/\varepsilon), \mathrm{Lap}(1, 1/\varepsilon))\), Part 1’s Laplace curve with \(\kappa = \varepsilon\).

  1. Both curves are symmetric. \(G_\mu\) is symmetric (Part 1, Exercise 1), and \(T_\varepsilon\) is too: its first and last pieces, \(1 - e^\varepsilon a\) and \(e^{-\varepsilon}(1 - a)\), are reflections of each other, and the middle piece \(b = e^{-\varepsilon}/(4a)\) is symmetric in \(a\) and \(b\).
  2. A necessary condition on the diagonal. On the diagonal \(a = b\), \(G_\mu(a) = a\) means \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - a) - \mu = \Phi^{-1}(a) = -\Phi^{-1}(1 - a)\), so \(a = \Phi(-\mu/2)\). The Laplace curve meets the diagonal on its middle piece: \(a^2 = e^{-\varepsilon}/4\), so \(a = \frac12e^{-\varepsilon/2}\). For \(G_\mu \le T_\varepsilon\) the Gaussian must cross the diagonal no further out, \(\Phi(-\mu/2) \le \frac12 e^{-\varepsilon/2}\), that is \[\mu \ge \mu^* = 2\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac12e^{-\varepsilon/2}\big). \tag{30.1}\]
  3. It is also sufficient. Both curves are symmetric, so by the duality of the chapter (step 3 there) each is the upper envelope of its lines \(f_{\varepsilon',\delta(\varepsilon')}\), \(\varepsilon' \ge 0\). Since \(f_{\varepsilon',\delta}\) falls as \(\delta\) grows, \(G_\mu \le T_\varepsilon\) holds as soon as \(\delta_G(\varepsilon') \ge \delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}(\varepsilon')\) for every \(\varepsilon' \ge 0\), where \(\delta_G\) is Equation 30.10 at \(\mu = \mu^*\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}(\varepsilon') = 1 - e^{(\varepsilon' - \varepsilon)/2}\) for \(\varepsilon' \le \varepsilon\) and 0 beyond (the Laplace profile of Part 3, The Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms, Exercise 2). Let \(D(\varepsilon') = \delta_G(\varepsilon') - \delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}(\varepsilon')\).
    • At \(\varepsilon' = 0\): \(\delta_G(0) = 2\Phi(\mu^*/2) - 1 = 1 - 2\Phi(-\mu^*/2) = 1 - e^{-\varepsilon/2} = \delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}(0)\) by the choice of \(\mu^*\), so \(D(0) = 0\).
    • The slope. Part 3 (Exercise 3 of The Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms) showed \(\delta_G'(\varepsilon') = -e^{\varepsilon'}\Phi(v)\) with \(v = -\varepsilon'/\mu^* - \mu^*/2\), and \(\delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}'(\varepsilon') = -\frac12e^{(\varepsilon' - \varepsilon)/2}\) on \([0, \varepsilon]\). So \(D' \ge 0\) exactly when \(h(\varepsilon') = e^{\varepsilon'/2}\Phi(v) \le \frac12e^{-\varepsilon/2} = \Phi(-\mu^*/2) = h(0)\).
    • \(h\) is non-increasing. \(h'(\varepsilon') = e^{\varepsilon'/2}\big[\frac12\Phi(v) - \varphi(v)/\mu^*\big]\). Since \(v \le -\mu^*/2 < 0\), the Mills-ratio bound \(\Phi(v) < \varphi(v)/|v|\) (Concentration inequalities) gives \(\Phi(v)/\varphi(v) < 1/|v| \le 2/\mu^*\), so \(h' < 0\).
    • Conclusion. \(h(\varepsilon') \le h(0)\), so \(D' \ge 0\) and \(D \ge 0\) on \([0, \varepsilon]\). Beyond \(\varepsilon\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{Lap}} = 0 \le \delta_G\). Hence \(G_{\mu^*} \le T_\varepsilon\), and with step 2, \(\mu^*\) is the smallest \(\mu\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\).
    The script confirms it numerically. It finds the smallest \(\mu\) with \(G_\mu \le T_\varepsilon\) on a grid of 1,999 values of \(a\) by bisection. For \(\varepsilon = 1\) it gives \(\mu = 1.0301\), equal to the closed form 1.0301, and the two curves come closest (they touch) at \(a = 0.3035\), the grid point next to the diagonal point \(\frac12e^{-1/2} = 0.3033\). For \(\varepsilon = 0.5\): \(\mu = 0.5618\) from both, touching at \(a = 0.3895\), next to \(0.3894\).
  4. The same as matching TV. The diagonal point of a symmetric curve is where the best advantage \(1 - a - b = 1 - 2a\) is attained (Part 1). So \(\mu^*\) is the Gaussian shift with the same total variation as the Laplace pair, \(2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1 = 1 - e^{-\varepsilon/2}\), which Part 1 found to be 1.0301 at \(\kappa = 1\).
  5. What the summary reports. By Equation 30.10, 1.0301-GDP gives \(\varepsilon = 4.5301\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) (and 0.5618-GDP gives 2.2706). The Laplace mechanism is \((1, 0)\)-DP.

The GDP summary of a Laplace mechanism is tight at one point, the diagonal, and very loose in the tails: the Laplace privacy loss is bounded by \(\varepsilon\), while every \(G_\mu\) has an unbounded one.

30.2 Group privacy

Exercise 2. Group privacy. Let \(h(a) = 1 - f(a)\) be the largest power at type I error \(a\) that a curve \(f\) allows. Show that if \(T(P, Q) \ge f\) and \(T(Q, R) \ge f\), then \(T(P, R) \ge 1 - h(h(a))\), so an \(f\)-DP mechanism is \(\big(1 - h^{\circ k}\big)\)-DP for groups of \(k\) records (Dong et al., Theorem 2.14). For \(f = G_\mu\), show that \(1 - h^{\circ k} = G_{k\mu}\). What \(\varepsilon\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) does a 0.5-GDP mechanism give a group of 2 and a group of 4, and what does Part 3’s group property (Equation 19.6) give when applied to the single point \((1.9931, 10^{-5})\) instead? Check: groups of 2 and 4 are 1-GDP and 2-GDP, so \(\varepsilon = 4.3772\) and \(9.9973\). From the point, a group of 4 gets \((7.9724, 4.57\times10^{-3})\), while 2-GDP has \(\delta(7.9724) = 5.20\times10^{-4}\). (Trade-off curves as power bounds, GDP)

TipSolution
  1. A curve bounds power. \(T(P, Q) \ge f\) says that every test \(\phi\) with \(\mathbb E_P\phi \le a\) has type II error at least \(f(a)\), that is, power \(\mathbb E_Q\phi \le 1 - f(a) = h(a)\). Since \(f\) is non-increasing, \(h\) is non-decreasing.
  2. Chain the bounds. Take a test \(\phi\) of \(P\) against \(R\) with \(\mathbb E_P\phi \le a\). Read as a test of \(P\) against \(Q\), it has power \(\mathbb E_Q\phi \le h(a)\). Read as a test of \(Q\) against \(R\), its type I error is \(\mathbb E_Q\phi \le h(a)\), so its power is \(\mathbb E_R\phi \le h(\mathbb E_Q\phi) \le h(h(a))\), using that \(h\) is non-decreasing. So its type II error against \(R\) is at least \(1 - h(h(a))\), and \(T(P, R) \ge 1 - h\circ h\).
  3. Groups. Datasets that differ in \(k\) records are joined by a path \(D = D_0 \simeq D_1 \simeq \dots \simeq D_k\). Applying step 2 along the path \(k - 1\) times gives \(T(M(D_0), M(D_k)) \ge 1 - h^{\circ k}\).
  4. The Gaussian case. For \(f = G_\mu\), \(h(a) = 1 - \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(1 - a) - \mu) = \Phi(\mu - \Phi^{-1}(1 - a)) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(a) + \mu)\), using \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - a) = -\Phi^{-1}(a)\). Each application of \(h\) adds \(\mu\) inside \(\Phi\), so \(h^{\circ k}(a) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(a) + k\mu)\) and \(1 - h^{\circ k}(a) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(1 - a) - k\mu) = G_{k\mu}(a)\). The script checks \(1 - h(h(a)) = G_{2\mu}(a)\) at \(\mu = 0.5\) on a grid of 1,999 values of \(a\): the largest difference is \(2.2\times10^{-16}\). For the Gaussian mechanism the bound is tight, because a group of \(k\) shifts the mean by up to \(k\) sensitivities.
  5. The numbers. A 0.5-GDP mechanism is 1-GDP for groups of 2 and 2-GDP for groups of 4, so by Equation 30.10 it gives \(\varepsilon = 4.3772\) and \(9.9973\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\).
  6. Going through one point instead. 0.5-GDP gives \(\varepsilon = 1.9931\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). Part 3’s group property turns \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) into \(\big(k\varepsilon, \delta\frac{e^{k\varepsilon} - 1}{e^\varepsilon - 1}\big)\):

The point route pays twice (Table 30.1): \(\delta_k\) is 1.7 times the curve’s \(\delta\) at the same \(\varepsilon\) for a pair, and 8.8 times for a group of 4.

Group privacy is one more place where keeping the curve beats keeping a point: for GDP the curve of a group is again Gaussian, with \(\mu\) growing linearly in the group size.

30.3 A tensor product by hand

Exercise 3. A tensor product by hand. Show that \(f_{\varepsilon,0}\otimes f_{\varepsilon,0} = \max\big(f_{2\varepsilon,0}, f_{0,\tanh(\varepsilon/2)}\big)\), and read off its privacy profile \(\delta(\varepsilon')\) for \(0 \le \varepsilon' \le 2\varepsilon\) from the corners. Check: at \(\varepsilon = 1\), \(\delta(0) = 0.4621\), \(\delta(1) = 0.3378\), \(\delta(2) = 0\). (Tensor product, duality)

TipSolution

Let \(\pi = e^\varepsilon/(1 + e^\varepsilon)\), so randomized response reports the true bit with probability \(\pi\), and note \(2\pi - 1 = \tanh(\varepsilon/2)\) and \(\pi/(1 - \pi) = e^\varepsilon\).

  1. The product pair. As in Table 30.5, the output pairs 11, 01 or 10, and 00 have likelihood ratios \(q/p = e^{2\varepsilon}\), \(1\) and \(e^{-2\varepsilon}\), and \(P\)-probabilities \((1 - \pi)^2\), \(2\pi(1 - \pi)\) and \(\pi^2\).
  2. The corners. Adding outcomes in decreasing order of \(q/p\) gives the corners \((0, 1)\), \(\big((1 - \pi)^2, 1 - \pi^2\big)\), \(\big(1 - \pi^2, (1 - \pi)^2\big)\) and \((1, 0)\). At \(\varepsilon = 1\) these are \((0.0723, 0.4656)\) and \((0.4656, 0.0723)\).
  3. First segment. It has slope \(-e^{2\varepsilon}\) and starts at \((0, 1)\): \(b = 1 - e^{2\varepsilon}a\). It ends at the second corner because \(e^{2\varepsilon}(1 - \pi)^2 = \pi^2\).
  4. Middle segment. Slope \(-1\) through \(\big((1 - \pi)^2, 1 - \pi^2\big)\): \(b = 1 - \pi^2 + (1 - \pi)^2 - a = 1 - (2\pi - 1) - a = 1 - \tanh(\varepsilon/2) - a\).
  5. Last segment. Slope \(-e^{-2\varepsilon}\) through \((1, 0)\): \(b = e^{-2\varepsilon}(1 - a)\).
  6. A convex polygon is the maximum of its lines. So the curve is \(\max\big(0, 1 - e^{2\varepsilon}a, e^{-2\varepsilon}(1 - a), 1 - \tanh(\varepsilon/2) - a\big)\). The first three terms make up \(f_{2\varepsilon,0}\) and the last (with 0) is \(f_{0,\tanh(\varepsilon/2)}\), as claimed. On a grid of 1,999 values of \(a\), the two sides differ by at most \(2.2\times10^{-16}\) at \(\varepsilon = 1\), where \(\tanh(1/2) = 0.4621\).

The privacy profile. By the duality, \(\delta(\varepsilon') = \max_a(1 - f(a) - e^{\varepsilon'}a)\), and for a polygon the maximum is at a corner. The two interior corners, and the midpoint \((1 - \pi, 1 - \pi)\) of the middle segment, give \(1 - b - e^{\varepsilon'}a\) equal to \(\pi^2 - (1 - \pi)^2e^{\varepsilon'}\), \(\big(1 - (1 - \pi)^2\big) - (1 - \pi^2)e^{\varepsilon'}\) and \(\pi - (1 - \pi)e^{\varepsilon'}\). For \(\varepsilon' \ge 0\) the first is the largest: it beats the second by \((e^{\varepsilon'} - 1)(1 - \pi^2 - (1 - \pi)^2) = (e^{\varepsilon'} - 1)\,2\pi(1 - \pi) \ge 0\), and the midpoint value is the average of the two. So \[\delta(\varepsilon') = \pi^2 - (1 - \pi)^2e^{\varepsilon'}, \qquad 0 \le \varepsilon' \le 2\varepsilon, \tag{30.2}\] which reaches 0 at \(\varepsilon' = 2\varepsilon\). At \(\varepsilon = 1\) (\(\pi^2 = 0.5344\), \((1 - \pi)^2 = 0.0723\)):

At \(\varepsilon' = 0\) all three points give \(2\pi - 1 = \tanh(1/2)\), the total variation of the product pair. Table 30.2 agrees with the hockey-stick formula applied to the exact loss.

Two composed \((\varepsilon, 0)\) mechanisms are \((2\varepsilon, 0)\)-DP and \((0, \tanh(\varepsilon/2))\)-DP at once, and only the pair of statements, not either one, is the whole guarantee.

30.4 Inverting the duality

Exercise 4. Inverting the duality. Find the largest \(\mu\) such that \(\mu\)-GDP implies \((1, 10^{-5})\)-DP. How many full-batch Gaussian steps with \(\sigma = 20\) fit in that budget? How many DP-SGD steps with \(q = 0.01\), \(\sigma = 1\) fit in budgets \(\varepsilon = 1, 2, 4\) according to the CLT? Check: \(\mu^* = 0.2681\); 28 full-batch steps; 418, 1463 and 4978 DP-SGD steps. (GDP duality, composition)

TipSolution
  1. \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) grows with \(\mu\). A larger \(\mu\) gives a lower curve, so every supporting line moves down and \(\delta(\varepsilon) = 1 + G_\mu^*(-e^\varepsilon)\) goes up. So the set of \(\mu\) that satisfy \(\delta_\mu(1) \le 10^{-5}\) is an interval \([0, \mu^*]\), found by bisection on Equation 30.10: \(\mu^* = 0.2681\), where \(\delta_{\mu^*}(1) = 1.00\times10^{-5}\).
  2. Full batch. \(k\) Gaussian steps with \(\sigma = 20\) compose to \(\sqrt k/20\)-GDP (Equation 30.12). The budget allows \(\sqrt k/20 \le 0.2681\), so \(k \le (20 \times 0.2681)^2\), and the largest integer is \(k = 28\).
  3. DP-SGD by the CLT. With \(\mu = q\sqrt{k(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1)}\) the budget allows \(k \le (\mu^*/q)^2/(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1)\). With \(q = 0.01\) and \(\sigma = 1\):

\(\mu^*\) grows more slowly than \(\varepsilon\), and \(k\) grows like \(\mu^{*2}\), so doubling the budget from 2 to 4 multiplies the number of steps by 3.40 (Table 30.3). Subsampling is what makes these numbers large: full batch with \(\sigma = 1\) would allow only \(k \le \mu^{*2}\), less than one step for \(\varepsilon = 1\).

Remember that these step counts inherit the CLT’s optimism: the chapter showed that for this example the CLT under-reports \(\varepsilon\), so a certified accountant would allow fewer steps.

30.5 Berry–Esseen for randomized response

Exercise 5. Berry–Esseen for randomized response. Compute \(\mathrm{kl}\), \(\kappa_2\) and \(\bar\kappa_3\) for \(f_{\varepsilon_0,0}\), and show that Equation 30.14 gives \(\mu = 2\sqrt k\sinh(\varepsilon_0/2)\) for \(k\) copies. Check: \(\mathrm{kl} = \varepsilon_0\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2) = 0.004996\) at \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.1\); at \(k = 100\), \(\mu = 1.0004\) and \(\gamma = 0.0562\). (CLT functionals)

TipSolution

Let \(t = \tanh(\varepsilon_0/2) = 2\pi - 1\) with \(\pi = e^{\varepsilon_0}/(1 + e^{\varepsilon_0})\).

  1. The privacy loss under \(P\). Randomized response outputs the likely value with probability \(\pi\), where \(L = \ln(\pi/(1 - \pi)) = \varepsilon_0\), and the other value with probability \(1 - \pi\), where \(L = -\varepsilon_0\).
  2. The functionals are moments of \(L\) (the chapter’s derivation, step 1):
    • \(\mathrm{kl} = \mathbb E_P L = \varepsilon_0\pi - \varepsilon_0(1 - \pi) = \varepsilon_0 t\);
    • \(\kappa_2 = \mathbb E_P L^2 = \varepsilon_0^2\);
    • \(\bar\kappa_3 = \mathbb E_P|L - \varepsilon_0t|^3 = \pi\,\varepsilon_0^3(1 - t)^3 + (1 - \pi)\,\varepsilon_0^3(1 + t)^3\). With \(\pi = (1 + t)/2\) this is \(\frac{\varepsilon_0^3(1 - t^2)}{2}\big((1 - t)^2 + (1 + t)^2\big) = \varepsilon_0^3(1 - t^2)(1 + t^2)\).
  3. The variance. \(\kappa_2 - \mathrm{kl}^2 = \varepsilon_0^2(1 - t^2) = \varepsilon_0^2/\cosh^2(\varepsilon_0/2)\), using \(1 - \tanh^2 = 1/\cosh^2\).
  4. \(\mu\) for \(k\) copies. Equation 30.14 gives \[\mu = \frac{2k\varepsilon_0t}{\sqrt{k\,\varepsilon_0^2/\cosh^2(\varepsilon_0/2)}} = 2\sqrt k\,\tanh(\varepsilon_0/2)\cosh(\varepsilon_0/2) = 2\sqrt k\,\sinh(\varepsilon_0/2). \tag{30.3}\]
  5. \(\gamma\) for \(k\) copies. \(\gamma = \frac{0.56\,k\varepsilon_0^3(1 - t^2)(1 + t^2)}{\big(k\varepsilon_0^2(1 - t^2)\big)^{3/2}} = \frac{0.56\,(1 + t^2)\cosh(\varepsilon_0/2)}{\sqrt k}\).
  6. Check. At \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.1\): \(\mathrm{kl} = 0.1\tanh(0.05) = 0.004996\). At \(k = 100\): \(\mu = 20\sinh(0.05) = 1.0004\) and \(\gamma = 0.0562\), from the closed form and from the general formula alike.

Since \(\sinh x \ge x\), \(\mu \ge \sqrt k\,\varepsilon_0\): at finite \(k\) the CLT centre sits slightly above the limit \(\sqrt{\sum\varepsilon_i^2}\). The band \(\gamma\) shrinks only like \(0.56/\sqrt k\) (Table 30.4): quadrupling \(k\) from 100 to 400 halves it.

The Berry–Esseen band is honest but wide: at \(k = 100\) it allows the true curve anywhere within 0.0562 of \(G_\mu\), while the actual error in \(\delta(1)\) was 0.0014 (0.1271 from the CLT against the exact 0.1257).

30.6 A sanity limit for the subsampled CLT

Exercise 6. A sanity limit for the subsampled CLT. Put \(q = 1\) in Equation 30.17 and compare with the exact \(\sqrt k/\sigma\). Which way is it off, and why does the derivation not apply? Check: the ratio, the same for every \(k\), is 1.3108 for \(\sigma = 1\), 1.0659 for \(\sigma = 2\) and 1.0101 for \(\sigma = 5\). (Subsampled CLT, expansions)

TipSolution
  1. The formula at \(q = 1\). \(\mu_{\mathrm{CLT}} = \sqrt{k(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1)}\), while full-batch training is exactly \(\sqrt k/\sigma\)-GDP. Their ratio is \(\sigma\sqrt{e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1}\), independent of \(k\).
  2. Which way. \(e^x - 1 \ge x\) for all \(x\), so \(\sigma^2(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1) \ge 1\): the formula over-states \(\mu\) at \(q = 1\). As \(\sigma\) grows, \(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1 \approx 1/\sigma^2\) and the ratio tends to 1.
  1. Why the derivation fails. Step 3 of the derivation expanded \(\ln(1 + qW)\) to second order, which needs \(qW\) small. At \(q = 1\), \(\ln(1 + W) = (y - \frac12)/\sigma^2\) exactly: the loss is Gaussian with variance \(1/\sigma^2\), not \(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1\). The expansion replaced the variance of \(\ln(1 + W)\) by the variance of \(W\), which is larger, and the gap grows as \(\sigma\) shrinks (Table 30.5).
  2. The regime. The CLT formula is meant for \(q \to 0\) with \(q\sqrt k\) fixed. There \(qW\) is small with high probability and the expansion is accurate, although for small \(\sigma\) the heavy right tail of \(W\) is exactly where it is least accurate.

Formulas derived for a limit regime should be checked at the edges of that regime; here the edge \(q = 1\) is known exactly and shows the formula’s bias.
