31  Exercises: Concentrated differential privacy

Exercises for Concentrated differential privacy, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part04-privacy-accounting/solutions_concentrated_dp.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part04-privacy-accounting/solutions_concentrated_dp.py.

The solutions use the chapter’s notation. A mechanism is \(\rho\)-zero-concentrated differentially private (\(\rho\)-zCDP) when its Rényi differential privacy (RDP) curve satisfies \(\varepsilon(\alpha) \le \rho\alpha\) for all \(\alpha > 1\); \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-differential privacy (DP) is the guarantee you report; \(\ell = \ln(1/\delta)\), and \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) throughout, so \(\ell = 11.5129\). Divergences are Kullback–Leibler (KL) or Rényi.

31.1 Laplace in a zCDP budget

Exercise 1. Laplace in a zCDP budget. The Laplace mechanism with scale \(b\) on a sensitivity-1 query is \(\varepsilon\)-DP with \(\varepsilon = 1/b\). Using its exact RDP curve (Equation 3.25 with \(t = \varepsilon\)), find numerically the smallest \(\rho\) for \(\varepsilon = 1\), and compare it with \(\varepsilon^2/2\) and with randomized response at the same \(\varepsilon\). Check: the smallest \(\rho\) is the KL divergence \(t + e^{-t} - 1 = 0.3679\), reached as \(\alpha \to 1\). (Pure DP to zCDP, tightness)

TipSolution
  1. The curve. The outputs on neighbours are \(\mathrm{Lap}(0, b)\) and \(\mathrm{Lap}(1, b)\), a shift of \(t = 1/b = \varepsilon\) scale units. By Equation 3.25, \[\varepsilon(\alpha) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha - 1}e^{(\alpha - 1)t} + \frac{\alpha - 1}{2\alpha - 1}e^{-\alpha t}\right). \tag{31.1}\] The script evaluates it in log space, so large orders do not overflow.
  2. The smallest \(\rho\) is the largest ratio. \(\varepsilon(\alpha) \le \rho\alpha\) for all \(\alpha\) exactly when \(\rho \ge \sup_{\alpha > 1}\varepsilon(\alpha)/\alpha\). Table 31.1 evaluates the ratio at \(t = 1\).
  1. The ratio is largest as \(\alpha \to 1\). There \(\varepsilon(\alpha) \to D_{\mathrm{KL}} = t + e^{-t} - 1\) (the KL divergence of the Laplace pair, from Divergences), and \(\alpha \to 1\). On the fine grid of orders the maximum of \(\varepsilon(\alpha)/\alpha\) is \(0.3679\), equal to the KL divergence \(0.3679\).
  2. Compare. The general bound is \(\varepsilon^2/2 = 0.5000\); randomized response at \(\varepsilon = 1\) needs \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2) = 0.4621\); the Laplace mechanism only \(0.3679\).

The general \(\varepsilon^2/2\) of Theorem 31.2 is a convenient upper bound, not the cost of any mechanism at fixed \(\varepsilon\). Every \(\varepsilon\)-DP mechanism is a post-processing of randomized response (Theorem 21.2), so none needs more than randomized response’s \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2) = 0.4621\) at \(\varepsilon = 1\) (Theorem 31.3). The Laplace mechanism, whose privacy loss reaches \(\pm\varepsilon\) only in its tails, needs \(0.3679\). Charging it \(\varepsilon^2/2\) is safe but overcharges by a factor \(0.5000/0.3679 = 1.36\); the sharp \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\) still overcharges by \(0.4621/0.3679 = 1.26\).

31.2 Calibrating σ for many steps

Exercise 2. Calibrating \(\sigma\) for many steps. Derive Equation 31.18. Then find the noise multiplier that makes \(k = 100\) Gaussian steps \((1, 10^{-5})\)-DP by the simple conversion, and the \(\varepsilon\) that the improved conversion and the exact Gaussian profile give for that \(\sigma\). Check: \(\sigma = 49.01\). (Optimizing over \(\alpha\), inverting a conversion)

TipSolution
  1. Inversion. The simple conversion Equation 31.15 is \(\varepsilon = \rho + 2\sqrt{\rho\ell}\), increasing in \(\rho\). Put \(x = \sqrt\rho \ge 0\): \(x^2 + 2\sqrt\ell\,x = \varepsilon\). Add \(\ell\) to both sides: \((x + \sqrt\ell)^2 = \varepsilon + \ell\). So \(x = \sqrt{\varepsilon + \ell} - \sqrt\ell\) (the other root is negative), and every \(\rho \le x^2\) meets the target. That is Equation 31.18.
  2. The total budget. With \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(\ell = 11.5129\), \(\rho = 0.02082\).
  3. Split it over the steps. Composition adds \(\rho\), so each of the 100 steps may spend \(\rho/100 = 2.0820 \times 10^{-4}\). A Gaussian step with noise multiplier \(\sigma\) spends \(1/(2\sigma^2)\), so \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{2\rho/100} = \sqrt{100}/\sqrt{2\rho} = 49.01\).
  4. Check the other conversions. 100 steps at \(\sigma = 49.01\) give back \(\rho = 0.02082\) and \(\varepsilon = 1.0000\) by the simple formula. The improved conversion gives \(0.8118\). The composition of 100 Gaussian steps is itself a Gaussian pair with \(\mu = \sqrt{2\rho}\), whose exact \(\varepsilon\) is \(0.7416\).

Calibrating with the simple formula buys a guarantee that is really about \(\varepsilon = 0.74\). Calibrating with the exact Gaussian profile instead would let you use less noise for the same reported \(\varepsilon\).

31.3 Hölder for two steps

Exercise 3. Hölder for two steps. Write out Equation 31.19 for \(m = 2\) and \(\alpha = 2\), and check it on \(P = \mathrm{Bern}(0.2)\), \(Q = \mathrm{Bern}(0.8)\) with \(R = \mathrm{Bern}(0.4)\) and with \(R = \mathrm{Bern}(0.5)\). When does it hold with equality? Check: the left side is \(1.1787\); the right side is \(1.1872\) for \(R = \mathrm{Bern}(0.4)\) and \(1.1787\) for \(R = \mathrm{Bern}(0.5)\). (Hölder’s inequality, group privacy)

TipSolution
  1. The orders. With group size \(m = 2\) and \(\alpha = 2\): the first order is \(\frac{m\alpha - 1}{m - 1} = 3\), the coefficient \(\frac{m\alpha}{m\alpha - 1} = \frac43\), and the second order \(m\alpha = 4\): \[D_2(P\|Q) \le \tfrac43D_3(P\|R) + D_4(R\|Q). \tag{31.2}\] The Hölder exponents are \(u = \frac{m\alpha - 1}{\alpha(m - 1)} = \frac32\) and \(v = \frac{m\alpha - 1}{\alpha - 1} = 3\), and indeed \(\frac23 + \frac13 = 1\).
  2. Evaluate. Each divergence is a two-term sum, \(D_\alpha(P\|Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\sum_y P(y)^\alpha Q(y)^{1-\alpha}\). Table 31.2 lists the values.
  1. When equality holds. Hölder’s inequality \(\mathbb E[XY] \le (\mathbb E X^u)^{1/u}(\mathbb E Y^v)^{1/v}\) is an equality when \(X^u\) and \(Y^v\) are proportional. Here \(X = (P/R)^\alpha\) and \(Y = (R/Q)^{\alpha - 1}\), ratios of the densities at an output drawn from \(R\), so \(X^u = (P/R)^3\) and \(Y^v = (R/Q)^3\): equality needs \(\ln(P/R) - \ln(R/Q)\) to be the same for every outcome. For Bernoulli distributions that means the log-odds of \(R\) sit exactly halfway between those of \(P\) and \(Q\). The log-odds of \(0.2\), \(0.4\), \(0.5\), \(0.8\) are \(-1.3863\), \(-0.4055\), \(0.0000\), \(1.3863\), so \(\mathrm{Bern}(0.5)\) is the midpoint and \(\mathrm{Bern}(0.4)\) is not.
  2. The link to the Gaussian. A chain of Gaussians \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\), \(\mathcal N(1, \sigma^2)\), \(\mathcal N(2, \sigma^2)\) has the same structure: both log-ratios are linear in \(y\) with the same slope, so they differ by a constant. That is why group privacy \(m^2\rho\) is exact for the Gaussian mechanism.

The inequality loses nothing when the middle dataset is “halfway” in the sense of the privacy loss. For the Gaussian mechanism every chain of neighbours is like that.

31.4 A mixed pipeline

Exercise 4. A mixed pipeline. A pipeline runs 20 propose-test-release steps, each \((0.25, 10^{-8})\)-DP, and 50 Gaussian steps with \(\sigma = 5\). Find its \(\rho\), its \(\delta_a\), and \(\varepsilon\) at total \(\delta = 10^{-5}\) with the improved conversion. Check: \(\rho = 1.625\). (Approximate zCDP)

TipSolution
  1. Each stability step. An \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP step is \(\delta\)-approximately \((\varepsilon^2/2)\)-zCDP (Proposition 31.3, part 1), so each costs \(\rho = 0.25^2/2 = 0.03125\) and fails with probability \(10^{-8}\).
  2. Each Gaussian step costs \(1/(2 \cdot 25) = 0.020\) and never fails.
  3. Compose. \(\rho = 20 \times 0.03125 + 50 \times 0.020 = 1.6250\) and \(\delta_a = 1 - (1 - 10^{-8})^{20} = 2.0000 \times 10^{-7}\).
  4. Convert. Spend the rest of the \(\delta\) on the conversion: \(\delta' = 10^{-5} - \delta_a\). The improved conversion at \(\rho = 1.625\) and this \(\delta'\) gives \(\varepsilon = 9.463\), at total \(\delta = \delta_a + \delta' = 10^{-5}\) (Proposition 31.3, part 3, which allows \(\delta_a + (1 - \delta_a)\delta' \le 10^{-5}\)).
  5. Compare. Treating the two kinds of steps separately, the Gaussians alone (\(\rho = 1\)) convert to \(\varepsilon = 7.084\) at \(\delta = 10^{-5} - 2 \times 10^{-7} = 9.8 \times 10^{-6}\), and basic composition of the stability steps adds \(20 \times 0.25 = 5\), for \(12.084\).

Twenty steps of \(\varepsilon = 0.25\) cost 5 under basic composition but only \(\rho = 0.625\) in the zCDP ledger, because they compose like Gaussian steps. That is the point of approximate zCDP.

31.5 The subsampled Gaussian at order 2

Exercise 5. The subsampled Gaussian at order 2. From Equation 31.27, show \(\varepsilon(2) = \ln\big(1 + q^2(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1)\big)\), and evaluate it for \(q = 0.01\), \(\sigma = 1\). Then show that the lower bound Equation 31.28 is positive exactly when \(\alpha > 1 + 2\sigma^2\ln(1/q)\). Check: \(\varepsilon(2) = 1.7181 \times 10^{-4}\). (Binomial expansion)

TipSolution
  1. The three terms of \(A_2\). In Equation 31.27 with \(\alpha = 2\), \(j = 0\) gives \((1 - q)^2\), \(j = 1\) gives \(2q(1 - q)e^0\), and \(j = 2\) gives \(q^2e^{(4 - 2)/(2\sigma^2)} = q^2e^{1/\sigma^2}\).
  2. Add. \((1 - q)^2 + 2q(1 - q) = (1 - q)(1 + q) = 1 - q^2\), so \(A_2 = 1 - q^2 + q^2e^{1/\sigma^2} = 1 + q^2(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1)\).
  3. Divide \(\ln A_2\) by \(\alpha - 1 = 1\). For \(q = 0.01\), \(\sigma = 1\): the closed form and the binomial sum both give \(1.7181 \times 10^{-4}\). Without subsampling, \(\varepsilon(2) = 1\), so subsampling at 1% buys a factor of about 5,800 at this order: \(1/q^2 = 10^4\), divided by \(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1 = e - 1 = 1.72\).
  4. Where the lower bound turns positive. \(\rho_0\alpha + \frac{\alpha\ln q}{\alpha - 1} > 0\) with \(\rho_0 = 1/(2\sigma^2)\) and \(\alpha > 1\) means \(\frac{\alpha - 1}{2\sigma^2} > \ln(1/q)\), that is \(\alpha > 1 + 2\sigma^2\ln(1/q)\).
  5. Check. For \(q = 0.01\), \(\sigma = 1\), the threshold is \(10.21\). The bound is \(-0.1169\) at \(\alpha = 10\) and \(0.4343\) at \(\alpha = 11\).

The threshold \(1 + 2\sigma^2\ln(1/q)\) is where the curve bends in Figure 31.4: below it the rare event “the record is in every sample” is too rare to matter, above it that event dominates.

31.6 The tCDP conversion

Exercise 6. The tCDP conversion. Apply the conversion Equation 31.31 of truncated concentrated DP (tCDP) to \(10{,}000\) steps of the running example with \(\omega = 4\) and \(\omega = 8\), saying which branch applies and where the unconstrained best order lies. Check: \(7.469\) and \(8.289\). (Constrained optimization over \(\alpha\))

TipSolution
  1. The rule. By Proposition 31.5, the first branch of Equation 31.31 applies when the unconstrained best order \(\alpha^* = 1 + \sqrt{\ell/\rho}\) is at most \(\omega\), that is when \(\ell \le (\omega - 1)^2\rho\). Otherwise the best admissible order is \(\omega\) itself.
  2. \(\omega = 4\). Per step \(\rho = 9.0789 \times 10^{-5}\) (chapter, Table 31.6), so \(10{,}000\) steps give \(\rho = 0.9079\). Then \((\omega - 1)^2\rho = 8.171 < \ell = 11.5129\): second branch, \(\varepsilon = 7.469\). The unconstrained minimizer \(\alpha^* = 4.561\) lies beyond \(\omega\).
  3. \(\omega = 8\). \(\rho = 1.1171\) and \((\omega - 1)^2\rho = 54.736 \ge \ell\): first branch, with \(\alpha^* = 4.210 \le 8\), and \(\varepsilon = 8.289\).

A larger \(\omega\) makes more orders available but forces a larger slope, because the line has to clear the curve on more orders. Here the slope wins, and the smaller truncation point gives the better \(\varepsilon\).

31.7 A line for a 5-DP mechanism

Exercise 7. A line for a 5-DP mechanism. A mechanism is \(5\)-DP. Find the \(\rho\) that Theorem 31.2 and Theorem 31.3 charge for it, find the order at which each line \(\rho\alpha\) reaches the pure-DP cap \(\varepsilon = 5\) of Equation 31.7, and say how an accountant should describe the mechanism. Check: \(\rho = 12.5\) and \(4.9331\); the sharp line reaches the cap at \(\alpha = 1.0136\). (Pure DP to zCDP, the cap on the RDP curve)

TipSolution
  1. The general constant. Theorem 31.2 charges \(\varepsilon^2/2 = 25/2 = 12.5\). The line \(12.5\alpha\) is above 5 at every order \(\alpha > 1\), because \(12.5 > 5\) already at \(\alpha = 1\). So it is worse than the cap everywhere.
  2. The sharp constant. Theorem 31.3 charges \(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon/2) = 5\tanh(2.5) = 4.9331\). This line starts just below the cap and reaches it where \(4.9331\alpha = 5\), at \(\alpha = 5/4.9331 = 1.0136\).
  3. What the accountant should keep. Above \(\alpha = 1.0136\) the cap \(\varepsilon = 5\) is below both lines, and the exact curve of randomized response, which bounds every \(5\)-DP mechanism (Theorem 31.3), is lower still. Converting either line back gives a worse guarantee than the one you started with. The simple conversion Equation 31.15 returns \(\rho\) plus a positive term, and \(\rho = 4.9331\) is already within \(0.07\) of 5, so the result exceeds 5 for every \(\delta < 1\).

For a large \(\varepsilon\), as in local DP, describe the mechanism by its RDP curve or by \(\varepsilon\) itself, never by a single \(\rho\). A line through the origin cannot follow a curve that flattens almost at once.
