10  Exercises: Membership inference

Exercises for Membership inference, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part02-attacks/solutions_membership_inference.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part02-attacks/solutions_membership_inference.py.

The solutions use the chapter’s notation. \(\phi = y\langle \theta, x\rangle\) is the logit of the model’s confidence in the true label, IN and OUT are the distributions of a record’s \(\phi\) over models trained with and without it, and an attack’s true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) are measured over (target model, pool point) pairs of the running example. The likelihood ratio attack (LiRA) is the per-record test of the chapter, and attacks are compared by their receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Exercises 1 and 7 reuse the chapter’s experiment with seed 0, so their numbers match the chapter’s tables; Exercise 6 draws a fresh pool.

10.1 Accuracy with an unbalanced prior

Exercise 1. Accuracy with an unbalanced prior. Suppose a fraction \(\pi_1\) of the queried records are members. Show that “member if correctly classified” has accuracy \(\pi_1(1 - e_{\mathrm{train}}) + (1 - \pi_1)e_{\mathrm{test}}\). With the running example’s errors, compare it with the trivial attack “always non-member” at \(\pi_1 = 0.5\) and \(\pi_1 = 0.1\). Check: 0.5584 against 0.5, and 0.3247 against 0.9. What does this say about reporting accuracy? (The win rate of a game, Proposition 10.1)

TipSolution
  1. Condition on membership, as in Proposition 9.1 of Threat models and privacy games: accuracy \(= \pi_1\,\mathrm{TPR} + (1 - \pi_1)(1 - \mathrm{FPR})\).
  2. The rates of this attack. By Proposition 10.1 with the 0-1 loss, \(\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - e_{\mathrm{train}}\) and \(\mathrm{FPR} = 1 - e_{\mathrm{test}}\), so \(1 - \mathrm{FPR} = e_{\mathrm{test}}\).
  3. Substitute: accuracy \(= \pi_1(1 - e_{\mathrm{train}}) + (1 - \pi_1)e_{\mathrm{test}}\).
  4. The running example has \(e_{\mathrm{train}} = 0.1495\) and \(e_{\mathrm{test}} = 0.2663\).
    • At \(\pi_1 = 0.5\): \(0.5 \times 0.8505 + 0.5 \times 0.2663 = 0.5584\), against 0.5 for “always non-member”. This is \(\frac12 + \frac12 \times 0.1168\), the advantage of the chapter.
    • At \(\pi_1 = 0.1\): \(0.1 \times 0.8505 + 0.9 \times 0.2663 = 0.3247\), against 0.9 for “always non-member”.

The attack has the same TPR and FPR in both cases; only the prior changed. At \(\pi_1 = 0.1\) it says “member” to 73% of non-members, and most queries are non-members, so its accuracy collapses below the trivial rule.

Accuracy mixes the attack with the prior. TPR and FPR do not depend on the prior, which is one more reason to report them, and to report them at a low FPR, where a “member” verdict stays reliable even when members are rare.

10.2 The Gaussian likelihood ratio

Exercise 2. The Gaussian likelihood ratio. For \(H_0: \mathcal N(0, 1)\) against \(H_1: \mathcal N(s, 1)\), show \(\ln\Lambda(t) = st - s^2/2\). Use it to find, for the two kinds of record of Table 10.1, the z-score cut that the threshold \(\ln\Lambda \ge 1.497\) puts on each kind, and check that the pooled FPR is \(10^{-3}\). Check: cuts 3.244 (typical) and 2.374 (atypical). (Neyman–Pearson, per-example calibration)

TipSolution
  1. Write the log-densities. \(\ln\mathcal N(t; s, 1) = -\frac12(t - s)^2 - \frac12\ln(2\pi)\) and \(\ln\mathcal N(t; 0, 1) = -\frac12t^2 - \frac12\ln(2\pi)\).
  2. Subtract. The constants cancel: \(\ln\Lambda(t) = -\frac12(t^2 - 2st + s^2) + \frac12t^2 = st - \frac{s^2}2\).
  3. Spot checks (from the script, computing both forms): at \(t = 3.0902\), \(s = 0.5\) gives \(1.4201\) and \(s = 4\) gives \(4.3608\); at \(t = 1\), \(s = 4\) gives \(-4.0000\). The same z-score is strong evidence for a record with a large separation and weak evidence for one with a small separation.
  4. Invert. For \(s > 0\), \(\ln\Lambda \ge \tau\) is the same as \(t \ge (\tau + s^2/2)/s\). With \(\tau = 1.497\):
    • typical records, \(s = 0.5\): \(t \ge (1.497 + 0.125)/0.5 = 3.244\);
    • atypical records, \(s = 4\): \(t \ge (1.497 + 8)/4 = 2.374\).
  5. FPR of each kind. Under OUT, \(t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\), so the FPRs are \(1 - \Phi(3.244) = 5.90\times10^{-4}\) and \(1 - \Phi(2.374) = 0.0088\).
  6. Pool. \(0.95 \times 5.90\times10^{-4} + 0.05 \times 0.0088 = 0.0010\). The TPR is \(0.95\,(1 - \Phi(3.244 - 0.5)) + 0.05\,(1 - \Phi(2.374 - 4)) = 0.0503\), as in the chapter.

A single threshold on the likelihood ratio becomes a different z-score cut for each kind of record: stricter where the separation is small, looser where it is large. That is how the Neyman–Pearson test spends its FPR budget where it buys the most TPR.

10.3 Same AUC, different exposure

Exercise 3. Same AUC, different exposure. Repeat the comparison of attacks A and B of Proposition 10.3 at \(r = 0.01\). Check: AUC 0.5050, \(\mu = 0.0177\), TPR at \(10^{-3}\) 0.01099 for A and 0.00106 for B. (AUC as a ranking probability, Gaussian trade-off)

TipSolution
  1. The formulas. Proposition 10.3 gives A the AUC \(\frac12 + \frac r2\), best advantage \(r\) and \(\mathrm{TPR} = r + (1 - r)\alpha\), and B the AUC \(\Phi(\mu/\sqrt2)\), best advantage \(2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1\) and the TPR of Equation 10.10 with \(s = \mu\).
  2. Match the AUCs. Setting \(\Phi(\mu/\sqrt2) = \frac12 + \frac r2\) and inverting \(\Phi\) gives \(\mu = \sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(\frac12 + \frac r2)\).
  3. Numbers at \(\alpha = 10^{-3}\) (Table 10.1). At \(r = 0.01\) the AUC is \(0.5050\), so \(\mu = \sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(0.5050) = 0.0177\). A’s TPR is \(0.01 + 0.99 \times 10^{-3} = 0.01099\), and B’s is \(1 - \Phi(3.0902 - 0.0177) = 0.00106\).

At \(r = 0.01\), the AUC is 0.505, which most papers would call “no better than chance”. Yet A identifies one member in a hundred with certainty, ten times the TPR of B at FPR \(10^{-3}\).

An AUC near one half says that the attack is weak on average, not that it is weak for everyone. A small exposed group barely moves the AUC and dominates the low-FPR end.

10.4 How many non-members?

Exercise 4. How many non-members? You want to claim, at 95% confidence, that an attack’s FPR is below \(\alpha\), and you see no false positives at all. How many non-members do you need for \(\alpha = 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-5}\)? Check: 2,995, 29,956 and 299,572. Hint: \((1 - \alpha)^n \le 0.05\). (Estimating rates from trials)

TipSolution
  1. The event to rule out. If the true FPR were \(\alpha\) or more, then seeing zero false positives among \(n\) independent non-members would have probability at most \((1 - \alpha)^n\).
  2. Make it unlikely. The one-sided 95% upper bound from zero counts (Hypothesis testing) is below \(\alpha\) exactly when \((1 - \alpha)^n \le 0.05\).
  3. Solve. Take logarithms (both sides negative): \(n \ge \ln 0.05/\ln(1 - \alpha) = \ln 20/(-\ln(1 - \alpha))\).
  4. Plug in. \(\alpha = 10^{-3}\): \(n \ge 2{,}994.2\), so 2,995. \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\): \(n \ge 29{,}955.8\), so 29,956. \(\alpha = 10^{-5}\): \(n \ge 299{,}571.7\), so 299,572. Each is about \(3/\alpha\), the rule of three.

The running example has 64,000 non-member pairs, so a threshold at FPR \(10^{-3}\) leaves about 64 false positives, enough for an interval of about \(\pm 25\%\) on the FPR (the chapter’s \([0.00077, 0.00128]\)). At FPR \(10^{-5}\) the same data could not even certify a bound. The pairs are also not fully independent: the same 1,000 points recur across the 128 target models, which is one more reason the interval is too narrow.

Every factor of ten lower in FPR costs a factor of ten more non-members. Reporting a TPR at FPR \(10^{-5}\) needs hundreds of thousands of non-member evaluations.

10.5 Membership in a Gaussian mean

Exercise 5. Membership in a Gaussian mean. A “model” is the mean \(\hat\theta\) of \(n\) points drawn from \(\mathcal N(0, I_d)\). The attacker sees \(\hat\theta\) and a target \(z\), and uses \(T = \langle z, \hat\theta\rangle/(\|z\|/\sqrt n)\). Show that \(T \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\) when \(z\) is not in the sample, and approximately \(\mathcal N(\sqrt{d/n}, 1)\) when it is. Deduce the TPR at FPR \(10^{-3}\) for \(d = 1{,}000\) and \(n = 100\), and check it by simulation. Check: separation 3.1623, TPR 0.5287. Hint: given \(z\), \(\langle z, \sum_{j \ne i}x_j\rangle \sim \mathcal N(0, (n - 1)\|z\|^2)\), and \(\|z\|^2 \approx d\). (Per-record separation, Proposition 10.4)

TipSolution
  1. OUT. If \(z\) is not in the sample, \(\hat\theta = \frac1n\sum_jx_j\) is independent of \(z\) and \(\hat\theta \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d/n)\). Given \(z\), \(\langle z, \hat\theta\rangle\) is a linear combination of independent Gaussians, so it is \(\mathcal N(0, \|z\|^2/n)\). Dividing by \(\|z\|/\sqrt n\) gives \(T \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\), exactly, whatever \(z\) is.
  2. IN. If \(z = x_i\), then \(\hat\theta = \frac1n\big(z + \sum_{j \ne i}x_j\big)\) and \[\langle z, \hat\theta\rangle = \frac{\|z\|^2}n + \frac1n\Big\langle z, \sum_{j \ne i}x_j\Big\rangle. \tag{10.1}\] Given \(z\), the second term is \(\mathcal N\big(0, (n - 1)\|z\|^2/n^2\big)\) by the hint.
  3. Standardize. Divide by \(\|z\|/\sqrt n\): \(T = \|z\|/\sqrt n + \mathcal N\big(0, (n - 1)/n\big)\).
  4. Approximate. \(\|z\|^2\) is a sum of \(d\) squared standard normals, with mean \(d\) and relative spread \(\sqrt{2/d}\), so \(\|z\|/\sqrt n \approx \sqrt{d/n}\). And \((n - 1)/n \approx 1\). So \(T \approx \mathcal N(\sqrt{d/n}, 1)\).
  5. TPR. By Equation 10.10 with separation \(s = \sqrt{d/n}\), \(\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - \Phi(3.0902 - s)\).

The simulation (Table 10.2) matches the formula within its sampling error at \(n = 100\). At \(n = 25\) the approximation in step 4 is cruder: the IN variance is \(24/25\), not 1, and \(\|z\|/\sqrt n\) varies from target to target. The simulated FPR stays at about \(10^{-3}\) because step 1 is exact.

Exposure depends on the ratio of parameters to records, \(d/n\). With as many dimensions as records, a member is flagged 1.8% of the time at FPR \(10^{-3}\); with ten times more dimensions, more than half the time. The tracing attacks of Lower bounds build on exactly this statistic.

10.6 The onion effect

Exercise 6. The onion effect. Remove the 50 outliers from the pool, retrain all target and shadow models, and rerun online LiRA. Before running, predict whether the TPR at FPR \(10^{-3}\) on typical points goes up or down. Then run it (the solutions script does) and explain the result with self-influence. (Self-influence, outliers)

TipSolution
  1. Setup. Draw a new pool as in the chapter (seed 0 stream, after the earlier exercises), train 128 target and 64 shadow models on it, and compute online LiRA with a variance per record. Restrict the evaluation to typical points: build the ROC curve from the (target model, typical point) pairs only and read off the TPR at FPR \(10^{-3}\). Then delete the 50 outliers, leaving a pool of 950, retrain all 192 models on random halves of it, and repeat.
  2. Result. On typical points the TPR at FPR \(10^{-3}\) goes from 0.0027 with the outliers to 0.0037 without them, with 60,800 typical member pairs behind each number. (The first number differs from the 0.0020 in Table 10.4 because there the threshold was set on all points, outliers included, and the pool is a fresh draw.)
  3. Why it goes up. Each model now trains on 475 points instead of 500, all typical, and the outliers no longer take up the model’s capacity. The typical points that are hardest to predict, those whose labels go against the trend, become the most atypical points in the data. Their self-influence rises, and with it their separation and their exposure. The mean separation of typical points, estimated from the shadow models, goes from 0.502 to 0.621.

Privacy risk is relative to the rest of the dataset. Removing the most exposed records does not make the others safe; it promotes the next layer. Carlini et al. call this the privacy onion effect. A defence has to bound the exposure of every record, not remove the current worst ones.

10.7 The shadow attack model is a threshold

Exercise 7. The shadow attack model is a threshold. Show that if the attack model for class \(c\) is \(\sigma(\kappa_c + \lambda_c\phi)\) with \(\lambda_c > 0\), then within class \(c\) it ranks pairs exactly as the loss does, and it says “member” with probability above \(\frac12\) when \(\phi > -\kappa_c/\lambda_c\). Compute these cut points for the running example. Check: 0.751 for \(y = -1\) and 0.813 for \(y = +1\). (Shadow models, Lemma 10.1)

TipSolution
  1. Monotone in \(\phi\). \(\sigma\) is increasing and \(\lambda_c > 0\), so \(\phi \mapsto \sigma(\kappa_c + \lambda_c\phi)\) is increasing. Within class \(c\), a larger \(\phi\) always gives a larger attack score.
  2. Monotone in the loss. By Lemma 10.1, \(\ell = \ln(1 + e^{-\phi})\) is decreasing in \(\phi\). So within the class, the attack score is a decreasing function of the loss, and it orders pairs exactly as “lower loss means member” does. Any threshold on the score is a threshold on the loss, and the two attacks have the same ROC curve on the pairs of that class.
  3. The cut point. \(\sigma(u) > \frac12\) exactly when \(u > 0\), so the attack model favours “member” when \(\kappa_c + \lambda_c\phi > 0\), that is, \(\phi > -\kappa_c/\lambda_c\).
  4. The running example. The fitted models are \(\kappa_{-1} = -0.179\), \(\lambda_{-1} = 0.238\) and \(\kappa_{+1} = -0.128\), \(\lambda_{+1} = 0.158\). The cut points are \(0.179/0.238 = 0.751\) and \(0.128/0.158 = 0.813\) (computed from the unrounded coefficients).
  5. Across classes. Pooling the two classes with one threshold on the attack score puts slightly different thresholds on \(\phi\) in each class, so the pooled ROC differs from the loss attack’s only through that offset. The chapter’s Table 10.3 shows how little that buys: AUC 0.577 for both, and TPR at FPR 1% of 0.0104 against 0.0103 for the loss attack.

An attack model that sees only the record-blind output \((y, \phi)\) can do no more than choose a loss threshold per class. Its value is calibration, not power.
