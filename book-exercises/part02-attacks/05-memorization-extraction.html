13  Exercises: Memorization and training-data extraction

Exercises for Memorization and training-data extraction, each with a worked solution. Try each exercise before opening its solution. The script code/part02-attacks/solutions_memorization.py computes every number quoted here; run it with uv run python code/part02-attacks/solutions_memorization.py. With the default seed it rebuilds the chapter’s contact list and its canary “my pin is 58745”.

The notation is the chapter’s: \(\mathcal R\) is the canary’s randomness space, the rank of a candidate counts the candidates at least as likely (itself included), and exposure is \(\log_2|\mathcal R| - \log_2\operatorname{rank}\). \(N(c, w)\) are the counts of an \(n\)-gram model, \(\alpha = 0.1\) its smoothing constant and \(V = 39\) its alphabet size. In a written-out context, _ stands for a space.

13.1 Expected exposure of a small space

Exercise 1. Expected exposure of a small space. For \(|\mathcal R| = 10\) and a canary that was not inserted (no ties), compute \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{exposure}]\) from Equation 13.11 and \(\Pr[\mathrm{exposure} \ge 2]\) exactly. Check: 1.1428 bits and 0.2. (Null distribution of exposure)

TipSolution
  1. The rank is uniform. The canary was not inserted, so the model does not depend on it, and with no ties its rank is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, 10\}\) (step 2 of the null-distribution derivation).
  2. The mean. \(\mathbb E[\log_2\operatorname{rank}] = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}\log_2i = \frac{\log_2(10!)}{10} = \frac{\log_2 3{,}628{,}800}{10} = \frac{21.791}{10} = 2.1791\).
  3. Subtract from \(\log_2 10 = 3.3219\): \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{exposure}] = 3.3219 - 2.1791 = 1.1428\) bits. It is below the limit \(\log_2e = 1.4427\): the \(O(\ln N/N)\) correction in Stirling’s formula is still large at \(N = 10\).
  4. The tail. Exposure \(\ge 2\) means \(\log_2\operatorname{rank} \le \log_210 - 2\), that is \(\operatorname{rank} \le 10/4 = 2.5\). Ranks 1 and 2 qualify, so the probability is \(2/10 = 0.2\), below the bound \(2^{-2} = 0.25\). The bound is loose here because \(10 \cdot 2^{-2}\) is not an integer.

Exposure has the same law for every model when the canary is absent and there are no ties: it depends only on \(|\mathcal R|\).

13.2 Samples for extraction

Exercise 2. Samples for extraction. A secret has probability \(p = 0.0442\) after its prefix. How many samples does the bound Equation 13.6 ask for to extract it with probability 99%, and how many does the exact condition \((1 - p)^m \le 0.01\) ask for? Check: 105 and 102. (Samples needed)

TipSolution
  1. The bound. \(m \ge \ln(1/0.01)/p = 4.6052/0.0442 = 104.19\), so \(m = 105\).
  2. The exact condition. \((1 - p)^m \le 0.01\) is \(m\ln(1 - p) \le \ln 0.01\). Dividing by the negative \(\ln(1 - p) = -0.04521\) flips the inequality: \(m \ge 4.6052/0.04521 = 101.9\), so \(m = 102\).
  3. Check. With 102 samples the success probability is \(1 - 0.9558^{102} = 0.9901\); with 101 it is 0.9896, just short of 99%.
  4. Why the bound is close. \(1 - p \le e^{-p}\) loses only a factor \(e^{-p^2/2}\) per sample for small \(p\) (\(\ln(1 - p) = -p - p^2/2 - \dots\)). The relative error of the bound is about \(p/2 = 2\%\), which is the gap between 104.2 and 101.9.

For rare strings the bound and the exact count agree; the useful rule is “about \(4.6/p\) samples for 99%”.

13.3 A canary in unique contexts has maximal exposure

Exercise 3. A canary in unique contexts has maximal exposure. Suppose the order \(n\) is large enough that every context met while scoring a pin after “my pin is” contains at least the prefix’s last two characters, the “s” and the space after it (for five digits and the end-of-line symbol, \(n \ge 8\)), and that these contexts, each a stretch of the prefix ending in “s” followed by the digits scored so far, occur in the training data only in the \(k \ge 1\) copies of the canary. (A context that held only the space would not do: a space followed by digits can also occur in the background text.) Show that the canary is the unique most likely pin, so its exposure is \(\log_2|\mathcal R|\). Hint: compare the canary with a pin that first differs at position \(j\); after position \(j\) that pin’s contexts were never seen, so each of its later symbols has probability \(1/V\), and use Equation 13.3. Check: the order-8 model of the script gives the canary of Table 13.5 exposure 16.61 at \(k = 1\). (Fact 2, rank)

TipSolution

Write the canary as \(\hat r = \hat r_1\dots\hat r_5\) and let \(\hat r_6\) be the end-of-line symbol; a candidate \(r\) is scored on the same six steps (Equation 13.1).

  1. Which contexts have counts. Every context met while scoring contains the “s” that ends the prefix, so by assumption it can only have been seen in the canary’s copies. The canary’s own contexts were seen exactly \(k\) times each, always followed by the canary’s next symbol. A context that is not on the canary’s path was never seen.
  2. Take a candidate \(r \ne \hat r\) and let \(j\) be the first position where \(r_j \ne \hat r_j\). Positions before \(j\) contribute the same factors to both scores, so only positions \(j, \dots, 6\) matter.
  3. Position \(j\). Both are scored in the canary’s context \(c_j\). By Equation 13.3 the canary gets \(\frac{k + \alpha}{k + \alpha V}\) and \(r\) gets \(\frac{\alpha}{k + \alpha V}\), strictly less.
  4. Positions after \(j\). The candidate’s context now contains \(r_j \ne \hat r_j\), so it is not on the canary’s path and was never seen: \(N(c) = 0\) and Equation 13.2 gives \(\alpha/(\alpha V) = 1/V\) for every symbol. The canary keeps getting \(\frac{k + \alpha}{k + \alpha V}\).
  5. Compare the factors. \(\frac{k + \alpha}{k + \alpha V} \ge \frac1V\) is equivalent to \(V(k + \alpha) \ge k + \alpha V\), that is \(Vk \ge k\), which holds. At \(k = 1\): \(0.2245 \ge 1/39 = 0.0256\).
  6. Conclude. Every factor of the canary is at least the candidate’s, and the one at position \(j\) is strictly larger, so \(p(\hat r) > p(r)\). This holds for every \(r \ne \hat r\), so the canary has rank 1 and exposure \(\log_2|\mathcal R| = \log_2 10^5 = 16.61\).

On the chapter’s corpus. No background line contains s_, because names end in a vowel, so the contexts of the order-8 model after “my pin is” all contain s_ and the assumptions hold. The script gives exposure 16.61 at \(k = 1\) for order 8, and also for order 6, where the assumption fails at the last steps but the canary still wins.

A model with enough context to give a secret its own path memorizes it completely from one copy. Exposure saturates, and the only remaining question is whether an attacker can find the prefix.

13.4 Copies needed by hand

Exercise 4. Copies needed by hand. A secret’s five steps have counts (true digit, strongest rival) without the secret of \((1, 2), (0, 2), (0, 5), (0, 1), (0, 1)\), and every rival has a lower code than the true digit. What is \(k^*\)? Which step decides it? If a larger model split that context so that the rival count at the third step fell from 5 to 2, what would \(k^*\) become? Check: 6, then 3. (Equation 13.7)

TipSolution
  1. Ties go to the rival. Greedy decoding breaks ties toward the lowest code, and every rival has a lower code, so the true digit must win strictly at every step: \(k \ge \text{rival} - \text{true} + 1\).
  2. Per step. \(2 - 1 + 1 = 2\), \(2 - 0 + 1 = 3\), \(5 - 0 + 1 = 6\), \(1 - 0 + 1 = 2\), \(1 - 0 + 1 = 2\).
  3. The maximum is \(k^* = 6\), set by the third step. With 5 copies the first two digits come out right, the third goes to the rival, and decoding leaves the secret’s path.
  4. After the split the third step needs \(2 - 0 + 1 = 3\), so the steps need \(2, 3, 3, 2, 2\) and \(k^* = 3\). The bottleneck is now shared by the second and third steps.
  5. If ties went the other way (rivals with higher codes), every requirement would drop by one and \(k^*\) would be 5 before the split.

A larger model helps the secret only where it splits the bottleneck context. Splitting an easy context changes nothing.

13.5 Exposure as a membership test over many canaries

Exercise 5. Exposure as a membership test over many canaries. Plant 20 canaries “my pin is \(r_i\)”, one copy each, in the training set of the order-3 model of the chapter, and draw 20 more pins that you do not plant. Declare “planted” when the exposure is at least 3 bits. Use Equation 13.10 to bound the false positive rate, then measure both rates. Why is the detection so much weaker than for the single canary of Table 13.5, which reached 4.05 bits with one copy? Check: the bound is \(2^{-3} = 0.125\); the script measures a true positive rate of 0.25 and a false positive rate of 0.15 on the 20 unplanted pins, and 0.1250 over all 99,980 unplanted pins. (Exposure, hypothesis testing)

TipSolution
  1. The bound. An unplanted pin is independent of the model, so by Equation 13.10 its exposure is at least 3 with probability at most \(2^{-3} = 0.125\). That is the false positive rate of the test “exposure \(\ge 3\)”.
  2. Measured. All 40 exposures come from one model trained with the 20 planted canaries. Planted pins: 5 of 20 reach 3 bits, a true positive rate of 0.25, and their mean exposure is 1.82 bits. Unplanted pins: 3 of 20 reach it, 0.15, with mean exposure 1.64. Twenty pins is a small sample: over all 99,980 unplanted pins the fraction is exactly 0.1250, on the bound, because the scores have no ties and the rank of a random pin is uniform.
  3. Why the test is weak. At order 3 only the first digit of a canary has a context that the background never uses, s_. With one canary, that context sends its whole count to the canary’s first digit, worth 3.46 bits (Table 13.2). With 20 canaries, the context s_ is followed 20 times by 20 first digits spread over 10 values. Each canary’s first digit gets about 2 of the 20 counts, and it competes with the first digits of the other 19 canaries instead of with nothing. Its advantage over a random first digit shrinks from a factor of 11 to about \((2 + \alpha)/(\text{average count} + \alpha) \approx 1\). The remaining digits share their contexts with 5,000 background pins, where one copy is a negligible vote.
  4. Reading it as a test. At false positive rate 0.125 the test finds a quarter of the planted canaries: barely better than chance. Auditing with many canaries needs either canaries that do not share contexts (different prefixes) or more copies.

Canaries planted with the same prefix compete for the same contexts. Their exposures measure memorization of the set, not of each one.

13.6 Which secrets an unprompted sampler finds

Exercise 6. Which secrets an unprompted sampler finds. By the chain rule, one sample from the start of a line equals a given training line with probability \(p(\text{line})\). Predict the fraction of the secrets of each duplication group that appear at least once among 20,000 samples from the order-6 model (trained without the public lines), and compare with a simulation. Check: for the secrets with 16 copies the mean \(p(\text{line})\) is \(1.34 \times 10^{-4}\), the predicted fraction 0.91 and the simulated one 0.96; for 4 copies, 0.01 and 0.02. (Chain rule, samples needed)

TipSolution
  1. One sample. A temperature-1 sample draws each symbol from \(p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\) and stops at the end-of-line symbol, so it equals a given line, end of line included, with probability \(p(\text{line})\) (Equation 13.1).
  2. Many samples. The samples are independent, so a line with probability \(p\) appears at least once among \(m\) of them with probability \(1 - (1 - p)^m\), as in the derivation of Equation 13.6.
  3. Average over each group. For each secret compute \(p\) under the model and average \(1 - (1 - p)^{20{,}000}\) over the 100 secrets of the group. Averaging the probabilities, not plugging in the mean \(p\), matters: the per-secret probabilities differ by orders of magnitude.
  4. Compare (Table 13.1).
  1. Why the probability climbs so fast with \(k\). The line’s probability is a product over its 13 symbols. The name’s first symbols are drawn in contexts shared with every line (the start of a line, then one or two letters). Each copy is one vote in each of those contexts, so each factor grows with \(k\), and the product of several growing factors grows faster than \(k\): from 4 to 16 copies the mean probability rises by a factor of 280. Once the name reaches contexts of its own, the remaining factors approach 1 (Table 13.2).

The predictions agree with the simulation within the sampling error of 100 secrets per group. They also explain the chapter’s attack: the 4-copy secrets have a 1 in 100 chance each, so almost none appear, although 76% of them are discoverably memorized at order 6.

An unprompted attacker meets a secret at the rate \(p(\text{line})\), which is the product of every vote along its path, prefix included. Holding the prefix removes most of that product.
